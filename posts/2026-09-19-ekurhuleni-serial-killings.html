Fear grips Ekurhuleni as ninth body found in serial killings probe

The Southern Wire Desk·Johannesburg·Saturday, 19 September 2026

Nine women. Two and a half months. One sprawling, unforgiving stretch of the East Rand. By the time investigators pulled the ninth body from a quiet corner of Dawn Park this week, the numbers had stopped reading like a crime docket and started reading like a siege. Ekurhuleni — a metro of more than three million people, of factory floors and taxi ranks and suburban cul-de-sacs — is in the grip of something it has not faced in a generation, and everyone from cabinet ministers to corner-shop owners is now saying the same thing out loud: this has to stop.

The timeline itself is chilling in its patience. The first body surfaced in Kempton Park in mid-July, not far from the flight path of one of the busiest airports on the continent, life going on overhead as if nothing below had changed. Then, silence — the kind of silence that lets a city convince itself an isolated tragedy was just that. It wasn't. Body two came weeks later. Then three, four, five, each one chipping away at the comfortable theory that this was a one-off. By the time the count reached nine, comfortable theories were the first casualty.

Police have now formally named four of the dead: Dineo Motapane, 38; Gracious Nkomo, 28; Elizabeth Moselakgomo, 38; and Itumeleng Kekana, 32. Behind each name is a household that has gone quiet, a chair at a table that won't be filled, a phone that rings out. The remaining five victims are still being identified, a process slowed by the condition in which some of the bodies were recovered and by the grim logistics of tracing families who, in some cases, have no idea their daughter, sister or mother is even among the dead. Forensic teams are working against decomposition, against scattered case files, against a killer — or killers — who appear to have chosen victims with no obvious connection to one another beyond geography and gender.

That question — one perpetrator or several — is the one keeping senior detectives up at night. The bodies span multiple suburbs stitched loosely together by major roads, not the tight cluster you would expect from a single offender working a comfort zone. Some investigators see the fingerprints of one meticulous individual, someone who has learned, refined, and grown bolder with each disappearance. Others aren't so sure, and privately admit the province may be dealing with a copycat pattern, or worse, two threats running in parallel and being read as one. Either way, the case file has swollen into one of the most complex serial investigations South African policing has confronted in years — the kind that gets its own dedicated incident room, its own hotline, its own task force pulled off other dockets entirely.

The political response has matched the scale of public alarm. President Cyril Ramaphosa has personally ordered police leadership to leave "no stone unturned," language that within the corridors of the Union Buildings translates into real resource shifts: additional forensic capacity, extra detectives seconded from neighbouring provinces, and a reward now standing north of R400,000 for information that leads to an arrest. That figure alone tells its own story. Rewards of that size aren't offered for routine cases — they're offered when a government wants the public watching every alley, every unfamiliar car, every neighbour who's started acting strangely.

And the public is watching. In the suburbs closest to where bodies have been found, the change is visible after dark: women walking in twos and threes who used to walk alone, community WhatsApp groups lighting up at the first sign of anything out of place, informal patrol groups forming on street corners that a year ago would have been empty by 8pm. Local safety forums describe a fear that has settled over daily routines — school pickups timed differently, gym sessions moved to daylight hours, ride-share trips logged and shared with a friend as standard practice now rather than an afterthought. It is the sort of quiet, cumulative behavioural shift that doesn't show up in crime statistics but reshapes a community all the same.

At least one suspect has already appeared in court, but detectives have been unusually candid about the limits of what that arrest actually resolves. Nobody investigating this case is prepared to say the danger has passed. If anything, the tone from those closest to the investigation has hardened rather than relaxed: one officer working the operation described it, without much room for softening, as a war — not a docket to be closed, but a hunt to be won. That framing has stuck, both inside the investigation and in how residents now talk about their own neighbourhoods.

What happens next will likely determine how this moment is remembered — as the case that finally forced a reckoning with violence against women in South Africa, or as one more headline that faded once the news cycle moved on. For now, forensic teams keep working, families keep waiting for confirmation they don't want to receive, and an entire metro keeps glancing over its shoulder.
