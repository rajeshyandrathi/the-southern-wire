Drought, drones and displacement: Darfur's war enters a crueller phase

Politics Desk

Sudan's civil war has produced so many grim superlatives over the past two and a half years that a new one barely registers outside the region, but the latest accounting from the United Nations deserves attention. Briefing on the situation in late October, a UN spokesperson described civilians across the country as caught between drone warfare, state repression and severe deprivation, with both the Sudanese army and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces accused of abuses against the people trapped between them. It is a description that captures a war which has long since stopped being a contest between two armies and become a slow strangulation of the population caught in the middle.

Darfur remains the epicentre of the suffering. The International Organization for Migration estimates that the region is now home to roughly 65 percent of Sudan's 8.6 million internally displaced people, a concentration of human dislocation that strains the imagination even by the standards of a conflict that has already produced the world's largest displacement crisis. Camps and informal settlements across North and West Darfur that were already overcrowded from earlier waves of fighting are absorbing yet more arrivals, with aid agencies warning that the capacity to register, feed and shelter the newly displaced has been exhausted in several districts.

Hunger is compounding the violence. Scarce rainfall through the 2026 growing season has severely damaged crops across North and West Darfur in particular, undercutting what little subsistence farming had survived the war and pushing families who had been getting by on their own harvests back into dependence on aid deliveries that themselves are routinely blocked, delayed or looted by armed groups along the way. The combination of drought and conflict has created the kind of layered crisis that humanitarian officials describe as the hardest to break: each shock makes the next one more lethal.

Diplomatic efforts to end the fighting have produced little beyond further proof of how entrenched the war has become. Repeated mediation attempts by regional and international bodies have failed to translate into a durable ceasefire, let alone a political settlement, and officials involved in the process now speak candidly about the limits of what outside pressure can achieve against two sides that each believe they can still win outright. The Quintet mechanism's own consultations have concluded that ending the war will require far more than a ceasefire agreement, a sobering admission from a process that was itself meant to be the breakthrough.

For a continent whose leaders have spent much of the past year preoccupied with contested elections and coup anxieties elsewhere, Sudan's war has settled into the uncomfortable category of a crisis so large and so prolonged that it risks being treated as a permanent feature of the regional landscape rather than an emergency demanding urgent intervention. The civilians still trapped in Darfur's camps have no such luxury of distance.
