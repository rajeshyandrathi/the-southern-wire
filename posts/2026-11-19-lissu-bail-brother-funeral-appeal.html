Tundu Lissu asked for bail to bury his brother. Tanzania’s courts said no.

Politics Desk

There is a particular cruelty in the timing of Tundu Lissu's legal defeat, and it has little to do with the courtroom theatrics that dominated his two-year treason saga. Earlier this month, Tanzania's High Court acquitted the Chadema opposition leader of treason over his calls to disrupt last year's general election, only to convict him instead on the lesser charge of incitement and sentence him to four years in prison. It was presented by the state as a moderation. For Lissu, it was still a prison sentence, delivered the same week he needed to bury his own brother.

His legal team's response has been to fight on two fronts at once. A notice of intention to appeal has been filed against both the incitement conviction itself and the four-year sentence attached to it, taking aim at the High Court's decision to swap in a lesser charge rather than acquit him outright. The appeal will eventually land before the Court of Appeal, Tanzania's highest judicial body — the same tier of the system that, back in July, had already thrown out the state's own attempt to revise the case against him, a ruling his supporters took as an early sign the treason charge itself was built on thin ground.

The bail request is the part that cuts closest. Lissu asked to be released pending his appeal specifically so he could attend his elder brother's funeral, a request that speaks to the ordinary, unpolitical cost a years-long treason prosecution extracts from a person's life outside the courtroom. The proceedings closed without any announced grant of bail, leaving him to face that loss from custody while his lawyers argue procedure in a separate filing.

Rights groups have not been quiet about what they think the conviction represents. Amnesty International has called the incitement verdict and sentence a travesty and demanded Lissu's immediate release, framing the substitution of charges as less a legal correction than a face-saving retreat by a state that could not make treason stick but was unwilling to let him walk away with nothing. Chadema has treated the acquittal on the original charge as a vindication regardless, and the opposition's reading of the last two years — a treason case assembled, tested in court, and ultimately unable to survive scrutiny — has only hardened.

What happens next runs on two separate clocks. The appeal against the incitement conviction will take its own time working through Tanzania's highest court, with no guarantee of a swift hearing. The more immediate question is whether a renewed bail application, now that the funeral has already been missed, gets any more sympathetic hearing than the first one did. For a man who has spent much of the past two years moving between courtrooms, hospital beds and exile, the distinction between winning the legal argument and actually walking free has rarely mattered more than it does right now.
