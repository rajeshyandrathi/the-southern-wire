Business & Economy

Sibanye's South African mines are having a strong year. Its American ones just had an expensive strike.

Business Desk

Sibanye-Stillwater's third-quarter numbers tell two completely different stories depending on which hemisphere you're reading them from. Group adjusted EBITDA rose 9% year on year to R10.8 billion, a result the company credits to firmer commodity prices and steady operations at home, and the South African platinum group metals business did the heavy lifting: its adjusted EBITDA climbed 16% to R5.8 billion, helped by a 4E basket price that rose 15% to R37,200 an ounce. For a company whose South African operations were written off as structurally challenged only a few years ago, that is as close to a clean win as mining results get.

The gold side of the South African business is more mixed. Output fell 12% to 155,449 ounces as the country's older, deeper mines continue their long decline in tonnes mined, even as a 35% higher average gold price kept the segment's earnings strong in rand terms. It is the same tension every deep-level South African gold producer has been managing for years: shrinking volumes offset, for now, by a price environment generous enough to paper over the decline. Sibanye's answer to that long-term problem is the Burnstone project, still being advanced toward a steady-state output of around 130,000 ounces a year, a bet that new, shallower ground can eventually replace what the old shafts can no longer deliver.

Then there is the United States, where the picture is considerably less comfortable. A five-week strike that began on 3 September at Sibanye's American PGM operations forced the company to cut its 2026 production forecast for that business to between 245,000 and 255,000 ounces, down sharply from the 280,000 to 300,000 ounces originally guided. Costs in that segment jumped 39% in the third quarter to $1,617 an ounce, and the US PGM unit is now the only part of Sibanye's portfolio expected to miss its 2026 operating targets outright, a stark contrast to a South African business that the company says remains firmly on track.

That contrast matters beyond this single earnings cycle. Sibanye built its American PGM exposure, acquired through the Stillwater deal years ago, as a geographic hedge against exactly the kind of operational risk that has traditionally been associated with South African mining rather than Montana's. A five-week strike and a blown production forecast on the American side, delivered in the same quarter that South African operations posted double-digit earnings growth, inverts the hedge the deal was supposed to provide, at least for one reporting period.

None of this changes Sibanye's underlying message to shareholders: diversification across gold, South African PGMs and American PGMs is still the strategy, and one weak quarter in one geography is the kind of volatility the structure is designed to absorb. But a quarter where South Africa outperforms and the United States falls short of its own guidance is a reminder that diversification only smooths results when the segments actually take turns being the problem, rather than handing the company a surprise in the operation it once treated as the stable one.
