Schools are raising fees by up to 10% next year, and parents say they're already battling

Business Desk

Most South African schools will raise their fees by between 5% and 10% for the 2027 academic year, according to figures now circulating among parent bodies and school governance structures as budget season gets underway. The increases sit comfortably above the current inflation rate, and they land at a moment when households are already absorbing a string of smaller cost pressures that individually look manageable but collectively have left many families, in the words of one parent quoted in recent coverage, simply "going to battle" to keep up.

The arithmetic behind the increases is not mysterious. Schools, like every other institution dependent on municipal services and imported materials, have spent the past several years managing electricity tariff increases, insurance costs, and staff salary pressures that outstrip headline inflation even in a year when load shedding itself has largely receded. Independent schools, which set fees with the most discretion, have generally pushed toward the higher end of the range, while public schools' governing bodies, working within tighter regulatory constraints on fee-setting, have mostly clustered closer to the lower end — though even a 5% increase, compounded over several consecutive years of similar rises, adds up to a meaningfully larger annual outlay than most household budgets have grown to match.

For families already stretched, the timing compounds an uncomfortable year. Petrol prices barely moved in November despite a headline fuel-levy cut, after a separate and less-publicised tax component absorbed almost all of the advertised relief, and a weakening rand threatens to push up the cost of anything imported, from textbooks to school uniforms manufactured using foreign inputs. None of these pressures is dramatic in isolation. Together, they describe a cost-of-living environment in which discretionary spending keeps shrinking even as headline inflation numbers stay within the Reserve Bank's target band.

School governing bodies, for their part, argue they have limited room to manoeuvre. Teacher salaries, which make up the largest single line item in most school budgets, are negotiated well in advance and rarely move in step with what any individual school can afford in a given year, and maintenance backlogs deferred during leaner years eventually have to be addressed regardless of the broader economic climate. The result is a structural tension that shows no sign of easing: schools facing their own genuine cost pressures, and parents facing a genuine squeeze on take-home pay, with neither side able to simply absorb the other's problem.

What happens next will likely play out school by school, as governing bodies finalise their 2027 fee structures over the coming months and parent bodies weigh in through the consultation processes most schools are legally required to run. For many families, the practical question is less about the percentage increase itself than about which other household expense gets cut to make room for it.
