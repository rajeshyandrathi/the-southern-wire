Washington books Abu Dhabi for new Ukraine-Russia talks as both sides count October's toll

World Desk·Tuesday, 3 November 2026

The United States will host a new round of trilateral talks between Ukrainian and Russian delegations in Abu Dhabi, with discussions expected to cover post-war security guarantees, de-escalation measures and humanitarian issues, according to American officials. The announcement arrives against a backdrop of battlefield assessments showing Russia suffered its heaviest monthly losses of the entire war in October, even as its forces continued grinding, incremental advances in the east.

Analysts at the Institute for the Study of War assess that Russian troops lost ground in October for the second consecutive month, a reversal of the slow territorial gains that characterised much of the preceding year, while Ukraine says it recovered more ground than Russia managed to capture over the same period. Kostyantynivka, a town Russian forces have been attempting to fully seize for more than a year, remains only partially under their control despite sustained pressure.

None of that battlefield pressure has translated into any let-up in the war's toll on Ukrainian civilian infrastructure. Russia launched hundreds of drones in a single overnight barrage at the start of November, part of a sustained campaign against the power grid that has left hundreds of thousands of households across multiple regions without electricity as winter approaches. Ukraine's own long-range strike campaign against Russian oil infrastructure has, by some open-source estimates, disabled roughly a tenth of Russia's refining capacity within a matter of days — a mutual escalation of infrastructure targeting that shows no sign of abating ahead of the talks.

The diplomatic track carries its own complications. Likely Russian drones violated Moldovan and Romanian airspace in recent days, a pattern of spillover that has unsettled NATO's eastern flank regardless of what progress, if any, emerges from Abu Dhabi. Ukraine's air force also disclosed that its air raid alert app was compromised, briefly displaying a false nuclear alert that officials moved quickly to clarify was not a genuine threat — an incident that underscored how fragile public information infrastructure remains even as formal negotiations proceed.

With both sides still far apart on the fundamental terms of any settlement, the Abu Dhabi meeting looks more likely to manage the margins of the conflict — prisoner exchanges, humanitarian corridors, perhaps narrow de-escalation steps — than to produce the kind of breakthrough that would actually end the fighting.
