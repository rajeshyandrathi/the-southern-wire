With Sunday's declaration looming, South Africa's hung metros turn from counting votes to counting friends

The Southern Wire Desk·Johannesburg

By Saturday, the arithmetic of South Africa's municipal election had mostly stopped moving, but the politics had barely begun. With the Independent Electoral Commission expected to make its formal declaration on Sunday, attention in Johannesburg, Tshwane and a string of smaller hung councils has shifted from results slips to the far messier business of finding enough votes on a council floor to install a mayor. Once the declaration lands, the Municipal Structures Act gives councils a strict window to hold their first sitting and elect a speaker, a mayor and a council whip, and in a year as fragmented as this one, that window is where the real contest now sits.

Johannesburg is the clearest test case. No party is anywhere near a majority in the city, and the arithmetic that mattered on election night now matters even more at the negotiating table: whether ActionSA's Herman Mashaba is prepared to sit in a coalition alongside the DA's Helen Zille, a relationship so openly hostile in the recent past that one party's refusal to work with the other could hand the ANC, hobbled nationally but still numerically necessary, a decisive swing vote in choosing who becomes mayor. Multiple accounts of the pre-declaration jockeying describe a city where the ANC is simultaneously the party bleeding the most support and the party every serious coalition now has to court.

Tshwane presents an even tighter puzzle. With ActionSA and the ANC separated by a fraction of a percentage point in the capital and the DA sitting third with real leverage, any of several two- or three-party combinations could plausibly produce a governing majority, which is exactly the kind of result that tends to produce long, bitter horse-trading rather than a quick resolution. Smaller parties that would have been afterthoughts in a two-horse race, among them the Freedom Front Plus and the Patriotic Alliance, are already positioning themselves as kingmakers in councils where a handful of seats can decide who gets the chains of office.

The pressure is not only political but procedural. Parties that fail to agree on a coalition within the prescribed period risk a council sitting that simply cannot produce a majority vote, forcing repeat sittings, provincial intervention or, in the most severe cases, administrators being sent in to keep basic services running while politicians keep talking. Several provincial governments have already signalled they are watching the bigger metros closely, mindful of how damaging a prolonged governance vacuum would be in cities still recovering from years of coalition instability, service-delivery backlogs and, in Johannesburg's case, a rotating cast of mayors that became a national punchline during the last term.

What happens over the next fortnight will say more about the practical health of South Africa's coalition era than the election itself did. Voters delivered a fractured verdict almost everywhere that matters; it now falls to politicians who spent the campaign attacking each other to decide, in private rooms over the next two weeks, who they can tolerate governing alongside. For a country that has grown used to disappointment in exactly this kind of negotiation, the next fortnight will be watched as closely as polling day was.
