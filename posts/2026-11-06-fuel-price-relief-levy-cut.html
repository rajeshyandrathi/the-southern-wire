A temporary tax cut, not falling oil prices, is what's keeping petrol flat this month

Business Desk

South African motorists woke up this week to petrol prices that had barely moved, with both 93 and 95 octane grades adjusted down by around a cent a litre when the Department of Mineral and Petroleum Resources' monthly change took effect on 4 November. Diesel users fared considerably better, with prices falling by roughly R1.20 a litre. On the surface, it reads as good news delivered at exactly the moment South Africans needed it, arriving in the same week as a hard-fought municipal election in which cost-of-living pressure was a constant theme on the campaign trail.

The real story sits one layer beneath the headline number. International product prices actually rose during the review period that determines each month's adjustment, and the Central Energy Fund's own projections ahead of the announcement had warned of increases of as much as R1.59 a litre for petrol, a figure that would have pushed inland 95 well past R32 a litre and made for one of the sharpest single-month jumps of the year. What spared motorists that outcome was not the market, but the Treasury: Finance Minister Enoch Godongwana's temporary cut to the general fuel levy, worth R1.50 a litre on petrol and a far larger R3.00 a litre on diesel, running from 4 November through to 1 December.

That the levy cut was timed to run through this specific window, rather than being a permanent adjustment to the tax, matters for how much relief motorists should actually expect to bank. A Slate Levy of just under 136 cents a litre also still applies to both fuels this month, a mechanism designed to recover previously under-recovered costs from the regulated price-setting system, which further complicates any simple comparison between what drivers are paying now and what they might pay once the temporary levy relief lapses at the start of December.

Set against the year as a whole, the scale of the swings becomes clearer still. Petrol was trading as low as R20.10 a litre inland in February, before a combination of rand weakness at various points in the year and shifting international product prices pushed October's inland price for 95 up to R30.25 and 93 to R29.88. A roughly fifty percent increase over nine months is the kind of move that shows up directly in household budgets, transport costs and the retail prices of anything that has to be trucked to market, which in South Africa is most things.

For now, the levy cut has bought the Treasury and motorists a month of calm at the pumps precisely when political attention was focused elsewhere. What happens when that relief expires at the start of December, against a backdrop of international prices that are still pointing upward rather than down, is the question economists and transport operators alike are already asking, with little reassurance so far from government on whether the cut will be extended or simply allowed to lapse.
