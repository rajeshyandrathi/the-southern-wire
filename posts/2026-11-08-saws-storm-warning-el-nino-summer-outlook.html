A level 4 storm warning across four provinces is just the preview: SAWS says this entire summer will run hot and dry

Weather Desk

The South African Weather Service issued a Yellow Level 4 warning for severe thunderstorms across Gauteng, Limpopo, Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal on 4 and 5 November, bringing damaging winds, hail and localised flooding to a stretch of the country that has already lived through a volatile few weeks of heat warnings, fire-danger alerts and now storm activity in quick succession. The warning forced disruptions in several areas just as the country was still absorbing the aftermath of last week's municipal elections, a reminder of how little patience this transitional season has shown for anyone's calendar.

More consequential than any single storm warning, though, is the seasonal outlook sitting behind it. SAWS's forecast for the period running from this November through January points to below-normal rainfall as the most likely outcome across most of the country, paired with above-normal temperatures, a combination the service attributes directly to the El Niño pattern expected to shape the 2026/27 summer. That outlook was first flagged back in October, and the past fortnight's swing between extreme heat and severe storms is consistent with the kind of volatile, front-loaded weather El Niño summers in South Africa have tended to produce.

The practical implications stretch well beyond comfort. Drier, hotter conditions elevate wildfire risk across the grassland and fynbos regions that have already seen extreme fire-danger warnings this season, strain agricultural planning in areas dependent on predictable early-summer rainfall, and complicate water-resource management in municipalities still recovering from previous drought cycles. A below-normal rainfall season layered on top of infrastructure that has not always coped well with either extreme heat or sudden storm surges leaves several provinces more exposed than the headline seasonal outlook alone might suggest.

For a public more accustomed to tracking individual storm or heatwave warnings day to day, the harder story to absorb is the cumulative one: this is not a single hot weekend or one bad storm system, but the opening weeks of a summer SAWS has been warning for months will run consistently against the historical average. Farmers, municipal planners and disaster management officials have had that warning since October, and the volatility of the past fortnight is arguably the outlook arriving on schedule rather than any kind of surprise.

What happens over the rest of November will be the clearest early test of how that seasonal pattern plays out in practice. If the current swing between storm warnings and heat alerts continues, provinces already managing fire risk and water constraints will need to treat this as the season's baseline rather than an aberration, with the harder months of December and January still to come.
