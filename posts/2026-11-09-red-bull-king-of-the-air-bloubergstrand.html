The world's best kiteboarders are already circling Bloubergstrand for Red Bull's King of the Air

Lifestyle Desk

Red Bull King of the Air returns to Bloubergstrand for its waiting period from 21 November through 6 December, the long window during which organisers watch the famously reliable Cape Town wind and pick the single day strong enough to run the competition at full intensity. It is a format that only really makes sense in a handful of places on earth, and Table Bay, with its unfiltered view across the water to Table Mountain and the kind of consistent southeasterly "Cape Doctor" wind that has made the Cape coastline a magnet for big-air kiteboarding for well over a decade, remains the sport's most storied stage.

The competition's premise is almost absurdly simple to describe and considerably harder to watch without flinching: riders launch themselves as high and as technically as the wind allows, with judges scoring height, rotation and landing across a knockout bracket that eliminates until a single champion remains. On the right day, with the wind running at full strength, competitors have been recorded reaching heights that would be unthinkable in almost any other board sport, launching thirty metres or more above the water in pursuit of the kind of score that separates a good run from a legendary one.

For Bloubergstrand and the broader Table View area, King of the Air functions as one of the more reliable tourism and economic boosts on the city's calendar, drawing international kiteboarders, their support teams and a substantial crowd of spectators who gather along the beach for whichever day the organisers eventually call. Local accommodation, restaurants and the kite schools that have proliferated along this stretch of coast all plan their November and early December around the event's waiting period, even with the exact competition day impossible to pin down more than a few days in advance.

That uncertainty is itself part of the event's identity rather than a logistical flaw to be apologised for. Unlike almost any other major action-sports competition, King of the Air refuses to commit to a fixed date, insisting instead on waiting for conditions worthy of the athletes attempting them — a stance that has, over multiple editions, produced some of the most spectacular footage in kiteboarding's short competitive history precisely because organisers were willing to wait rather than run a mediocre contest on a predetermined schedule.

With the waiting period now open, Cape Town's kiteboarding community, hospitality operators and casual beachgoers alike settle into the same annual ritual: watching the wind forecasts as closely as the athletes themselves, waiting for the call that turns an ordinary stretch of Bloubergstrand into one of the sport's most important stages for a single, unrepeatable afternoon.
