Spain just had its first general strike in 14 years. Its prime minister called an election instead of an answer.

World Desk

Spain shut down for a day over a crisis that has nothing to do with the usual triggers for national strikes — not pensions, not labour law, not public-sector pay. On 11 November, the UGT and CCOO unions joined forces with tenants' associations for the country's first general strike in 14 years, and its first joint walkout with housing groups since 2012, over one grievance: rent that has become genuinely unaffordable for an entire generation of Spanish workers.

The scale of disruption matched the rarity of the occasion. Ports and carmakers reported turnout above 90% among striking workers, factories across the country stood idle, and Spain's Renfe rail network absorbed a full-day stoppage by drivers the day before the main strike, followed by partial stoppages that bled disruption into the days after. Police clashed with picketers at Mercamadrid, the capital's main wholesale produce market, using pepper spray against a crowd that organisers insist was protesting a cost-of-living problem rather than mounting an industrial dispute in the traditional sense.

The number driving all of it is almost absurd on its face: one widely cited estimate puts average rent at nearly 99% of a young worker's monthly salary in parts of Spain, a ratio that leaves essentially nothing for anything else a wage is supposed to cover. That is not a market adjusting to high demand. It is a housing market that has structurally priced an entire cohort of working adults out of independent living, and the strike's organisers built their entire campaign around making that single, visceral number impossible for the government to keep deflecting.

Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez's response has reframed what looked like a labour dispute into something closer to an existential political threat. After his minority government failed to secure parliamentary approval for a package of housing measures meant to address exactly this pressure, Sánchez called a snap election for 29 November — barely two and a half weeks after the strike itself. That is not the move of a government confident it has the votes to pass housing reform through ordinary legislative channels; it is the move of a government betting that an election fought explicitly on affordability might produce a parliament more willing to act than the one currently blocking him.

Analysts tracking the fallout expect the strike to keep the housing crisis at the centre of Spanish political debate through the election itself, which is precisely what Sánchez's opponents and his own coalition partners will be fighting to control the narrative around. A government that just watched its own workers shut down the country's ports and factories over rent has roughly two and a half weeks to convince voters it understands the problem better than the unions who organised against it — a tight window, for a crisis that took years to build.
