Netflix breaks its own rule for Greta Gerwig's Narnia, giving it weeks in IMAX before anyone can stream it

Culture Desk

Netflix has committed to a two-week exclusive global IMAX engagement for Greta Gerwig's adaptation of The Chronicles of Narnia, opening the film in theatres on 25 November, more than four weeks before it becomes available to stream on 23 December. For a company whose entire theatrical strategy has for years been built around minimising or skipping cinema windows altogether, a gap this long, more than double the roughly 45-day PVOD window that has become standard across the industry, amounts to a genuine strategic departure, even if only for this one title.

Company insiders have been quick to push back on any reading of the Narnia release as a broader pivot toward traditional theatrical distribution, telling trade press that each film's release pattern is being decided individually rather than according to a new company-wide policy. That framing matters commercially: Netflix's stock and subscriber narrative has long rested on the idea that its content drives the platform directly, and a sustained shift toward theatrical-first releases would complicate that story in ways the company is clearly keen to avoid signalling prematurely.

Still, the scale of the Narnia bet is hard to wave away as a one-off curiosity. A leaked internal report points to Netflix targeting around 30 theatrical releases a year under film chair Dan Lin, organised around specific genre priorities rather than opportunistic one-offs, suggesting the company sees selective theatrical exposure as a tool for prestige titles and franchise-building rather than a wholesale change in how most of its content reaches audiences. Gerwig's track record with large-scale, culturally dominant adaptations makes Narnia a logical test case for exactly that kind of calculated exception.

The rest of Netflix's November slate underlines how unusual the Narnia treatment really is. The month's schedule leans heavily on nearly thirty Christmas-themed titles headed straight to streaming, alongside Guillermo del Toro's Frankenstein, which arrived on the platform on 7 November with no theatrical run at all. Against that backdrop, a multi-week IMAX exclusive for a single franchise title reads less as a change in direction and more as Netflix calibrating its theatrical ambitions to the handful of releases it judges capable of justifying the investment.

Separately, reports that Netflix has used generative AI workflows across roughly 300 titles released in 2026, primarily in post-production, point to a studio investing in efficiency gains behind the scenes even as it spends selectively, and visibly, on theatrical prestige in front of audiences. Both strategies, cost discipline in production and calculated exceptions in distribution, speak to a company trying to have its scale and its credibility at the same time.
