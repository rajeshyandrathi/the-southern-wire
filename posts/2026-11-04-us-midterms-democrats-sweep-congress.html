Democrats seize both chambers of Congress in a midterm result Trump insists isn't a rebuke

World Desk·Wednesday, 4 November 2026

The uncertainty that hung over Tuesday's vote lifted overnight. Major US networks have now called both the House and the Senate for the Democratic Party, handing the party full control of Congress for the first time in the current administration and ending two years in which the White House faced little legislative resistance.

The Senate flip was the bigger surprise of the night. Democrats picked up seats in Ohio, Iowa, South Carolina, Maine, North Carolina, Kansas and Texas, a sweep that ran well beyond the handful of competitive races analysts had flagged before polls opened. In Maine, Republican incumbent Susan Collins conceded her race, one of the clearest single signals that the night had broken decisively against her party. Networks project Democrats will hold at least the mid-fifties in seats once the final two unresolved races are settled.

The House result, confirmed after the Senate call, extends the same pattern. Projections put the Democratic caucus comfortably past the 218 seats needed for a majority, restoring the party's ability to set the chamber's agenda, chair its committees and issue subpoenas — tools it has lacked for the past two years.

Reaction from the White House was characteristically defiant. The president congratulated Democrats on the win but rejected the idea that the result amounted to a verdict on his presidency, pointing instead to turnout patterns and local factors in the states that flipped. Democratic leaders read it differently, framing the sweep as a mandate to use their new majorities as a check on an administration that has leaned heavily on executive action over the past two years.

Beyond Capitol Hill, Tuesday's ballot also reshaped a handful of governors' mansions, with races in Kansas and Nebraska among those that moved toward the Democrats. For a White House that has treated trade and foreign policy — including the recent move to strip South Africa of its AGOA benefits — as largely executive terrain, a hostile Congress will not undo decisions already taken. But it does hand Democrats fresh leverage over funding, oversight hearings and the political capital the administration will need to sustain an assertive agenda through its final two years.
