Cleared of treason, Tundu Lissu fights a four-year sentence instead

Politics Desk

Tundu Lissu walked into a Tanzanian courtroom earlier this month facing a charge that could have ended in his execution. He walked out acquitted of treason but convicted of a lesser offence, incitement, tied to remarks he made about disrupting the country's 2025 general election. The sentence attached to that lesser charge, four years in prison, is now the fight. His party, Chadema, has confirmed it will take the case to the Court of Appeal, Tanzania's highest judicial body, while simultaneously pursuing a bail application to get its leader out of custody while that appeal proceeds.

The gap between what Lissu was charged with and what he was convicted of is not a technicality. Treason in Tanzania carries the death penalty, and Lissu spent nineteen months in pretrial detention under that threat before the High Court finally delivered its verdict. That the court found no basis for the treason charge but still found grounds to jail him for incitement has been read by his supporters not as a vindication but as a face-saving compromise: serious enough to keep the country's most prominent opposition figure off the streets, light enough to avoid the international backlash a death-penalty case would have invited.

International rights organisations have not read it as a compromise worth accepting. Amnesty International has called the conviction and sentence a travesty of justice and demanded Lissu's immediate and unconditional release, a position echoed by other human rights monitors who have tracked the case since his arrest. Their argument rests less on the specific legal findings than on the broader pattern: an opposition leader detained for a year and a half, tried on a capital charge, and ultimately convicted on a charge that still removes him from public life at precisely the moment his party needs him most.

That timing is the part Chadema cannot ignore. Tanzania's political calendar does not pause for appeals court schedules, and a four-year sentence, even one under appeal, functions in practice as a disqualification from the kind of frontline campaigning Lissu has built his career on. His legal team's parallel push for bail is as much a political necessity as a legal one: every month he remains in custody while his appeal grinds through the system is a month his party operates without its most recognisable voice.

The case sits inside a wider regional pattern that rights groups have grown increasingly vocal about: the use of serious, sometimes capital, charges against opposition figures across East Africa, charges that are then quietly downgraded once international attention peaks, leaving a conviction standing even as the original allegation collapses. Whether Tanzania's Court of Appeal ultimately overturns the incitement conviction or lets it stand, the nineteen months Lissu already spent in detention facing a charge the High Court itself rejected will remain the part of this story no appeal can undo.
