A year after the truce was announced, Israeli strikes on Gaza have never actually stopped

World Desk

The word "ceasefire" has come to mean something looser and more contested in Gaza than it does almost anywhere else the term gets used. In the days since the truce's first anniversary passed, strikes have continued to kill Palestinians across the territory in a steady, grinding rhythm that bears little resemblance to what the word is supposed to describe. Israeli strikes targeting a vehicle in Gaza City's Tal al-Hawa neighbourhood killed a man named Hani Hamed Kahil and wounded five others, according to local reporting, one of several similarly scaled incidents recorded in recent days.

A separate set of strikes killed five people across the Gaza Strip, with the territory's civil defence agency providing the casualty figures and the Israeli military saying only that it had targeted "several terrorists," a formulation that has become almost boilerplate in official statements regardless of the circumstances of any given strike and that rarely satisfies independent efforts to verify who exactly was killed and why. Earlier strikes in the same period hit the Nuseirat refugee camp, part of what humanitarian monitors describe as a continuing pattern of shelling and demolition in areas that were supposed to have seen fighting cease.

The cumulative toll from the broader war, now well past its two-year mark since the October 2023 Hamas attack that triggered it, has climbed past 74,500 according to figures from Gaza's health ministry, numbers the United Nations has stated it considers reliable despite the near-impossible conditions under which they are compiled. That figure alone places this conflict among the deadliest of the current century for the territory it has consumed, and it continues to grow even in a period officially designated as a ceasefire.

Reconstruction, the promise that was supposed to follow any durable truce, remains almost entirely theoretical. Much of Gaza remains in ruins, with a US-appointed oversight board still negotiating for permission to bring in tents and building materials ahead of a winter that aid agencies warn will be lethal for a population already living in collapsed infrastructure without reliable electricity or clean water. Each month that passes without meaningful reconstruction access deepens a humanitarian crisis that the ceasefire was meant to begin resolving rather than simply pause.

For Palestinians in Gaza, the distinction between a declared ceasefire and a war that has merely changed its tempo has become close to meaningless in daily life, where death from an airstrike remains a live possibility regardless of what diplomatic language describes the current state of the conflict. For the mediators who negotiated the truce a year ago, the gap between what was announced and what has actually happened on the ground remains the single most damaging fact working against the agreement's credibility.
