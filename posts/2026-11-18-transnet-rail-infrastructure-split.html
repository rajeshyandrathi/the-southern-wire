Transnet's freight railway is finally getting split in two

Business Desk

A technical task team is being assembled before the end of the year to finalise something South African logistics planners have been promising since 2023: the formal corporatisation of the Transnet Rail Infrastructure Manager, splitting the entity that owns and maintains the country's freight rail tracks away from the rest of the Transnet group. The team, confirmed in the latest Operation Vulindlela quarterly report, draws officials from the Presidency, National Treasury and the Department of Transport, a line-up that signals the project has moved from technical planning into direct political oversight.

The infrastructure manager, known by the acronym TRIM, has existed in interim form since 2023 and 2024, created specifically to let private rail operators access Transnet's network without having to negotiate directly with the state freight company that also competes with them for cargo. Making TRIM a genuinely separate, permanent entity has been the harder, slower half of that reform: an interim body is easy to stand up, but corporatising it properly, with its own governance, balance sheet and accountability structure independent of Transnet's broader group, has repeatedly slipped past earlier target dates.

A parallel transaction is moving alongside it. Government is also working to unbundle the Transnet National Ports Authority from the group, mirroring the logic applied to rail: separate the infrastructure owner from the operator competing to use that infrastructure, in theory creating a more level playing field for private players. Grindrod's recent test train on the North-East Corridor, run as the first private train operating company to do so, is the clearest physical evidence yet that this reform agenda is producing results rather than remaining a policy document.

The stakes for South African exporters are not abstract. Years of underinvestment in rail maintenance pushed freight volumes onto the country's roads, adding cost and wear to a highway network never built to carry that load, while mining and agricultural exporters lost reliability they could not easily replace. Opening the rail network to private operators, with a genuinely independent infrastructure manager setting access terms, is the mechanism government has settled on to reverse that shift, and the task team's job is to make sure TRIM's separation is real enough to deliver it.

None of that guarantees the December target holds. Transnet's restructuring has missed enough earlier deadlines that scepticism is the rational starting position, and a task team mandate is, by itself, a plan to make a plan rather than a completed separation. What makes this round different is the seniority of the officials now directly accountable for the timeline, and the fact that a private operator has already run a train on the network the reform is meant to open up.
