Gaza's ceasefire has an international force on paper. In practice, it still doesn't have a base.

World Desk

More than five weeks into the truce that halted the war in Gaza, the mechanism meant to hold it together is still mostly theoretical. The International Stabilisation Force endorsed by the UN Security Council last November was conceived as a roughly 10,000-strong deployment, a number Washington has since quietly walked back; officials now concede privately that the US is unlikely to recruit much beyond 8,000 personnel, and the force currently on the ground is smaller still — a rotating contingent of around 500 troops, drawn mainly from Morocco and Kosovo, a fraction of what the ceasefire's own architecture assumed would be in place by now.

The force's planned base has become its own flashpoint. Reporting from mid-October indicated Israel has blocked construction of the ISF's intended facility at the so-called Board of Peace site inside Gaza, a holdup that, if accurate, means the stabilisation force central to the ceasefire's long-term enforcement has nowhere to actually operate from. A security mechanism without a functioning headquarters is a security mechanism that exists mainly in UN resolution text, not on the ground where the truce it is meant to protect is actually being tested daily.

Those daily tests have continued at a pace that makes a mockery of the word "ceasefire" as commonly understood. Gaza's government media office counted 5,989 violations of the agreement since it took hold, including 753 in October alone — figures that come from Gaza's own authorities and should be read with that sourcing in mind. The Israeli military, for its part, has attributed 31 violations to Hamas and other Palestinian armed groups in just the first half of October. Both counts cannot be fully reconciled, and neither side has produced an independently verified tally, but the sheer scale of allegation on both sides tells its own story: whatever "ceasefire" means in Gaza right now, it does not mean the absence of violence.

The diplomatic architecture around all of this remains, on paper, more ambitious than anything attempted in previous rounds of this conflict — a UN-endorsed roadmap, a named stabilisation force, a phased plan toward a more durable peace. The gap between that architecture and its implementation is precisely where the current stall lives. Recruitment shortfalls, a blocked base and dueling violation counts are not signs of a peace process collapsing outright; they are signs of a peace process that was always going to be harder to execute than it was to announce, now encountering exactly the friction that was predictable from the day the ceasefire was signed.

For the people actually living inside Gaza, the distinction between a ceasefire that is formally in effect and one that is functionally enforced matters far more than which government's violation count is more accurate. Until the stabilisation force has both the troop numbers it was promised and an actual base to deploy from, the truce will keep operating closer to a fragile pause than the durable peace its architects described when they signed it into existence.
