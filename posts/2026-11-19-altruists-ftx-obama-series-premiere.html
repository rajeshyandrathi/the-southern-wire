Netflix just turned the biggest fraud in effective altruism into eight hours of television

Culture Desk

Every great scam has a pitch that sounds reasonable until the money disappears, and FTX's pitch sounded more reasonable than most: a crypto exchange run by a rumpled wunderkind who claimed he wanted to get stupendously rich only so he could give almost all of it away. The Altruists, premiering globally on Netflix today in a full eight-episode drop, is the first major dramatization brave enough to take that pitch seriously instead of just mocking it.

Anthony Boyle plays Sam Bankman-Fried with the specific, uncomfortable commitment the role demands: not a cartoon villain, but a true believer whose certainty about his own righteousness never wavers, even as the numbers underneath him do. Julia Garner, as Caroline Ellison, carries the series' other half — the lieutenant who did the arithmetic nobody else wanted to do and kept showing up anyway.

The involvement of Barack and Michelle Obama's Higher Ground Productions gives the series a credibility the subject matter doesn't obviously deserve, and that tension is clearly intentional. Effective altruism, the philosophy FTX wrapped itself in, was never really about crypto — it was about a generation of extremely online, extremely confident young people convincing each other that enough spreadsheet logic could justify almost any shortcut.

What The Altruists risks, and mostly avoids, is turning Bankman-Fried into either a monster or a martyr. Instead the show sits in the much less comfortable space between them: a man who told himself a story about doing good so convincingly that he believed it right up until the exchange he ran evaporated billions of dollars of customer money that was never supposed to be his to spend.

For South African audiences who watched the FTX collapse mostly as an abstraction — a crypto headline, a number with too many zeroes — the series offers something more useful than outrage: a close look at how plausible the con sounded from inside the room. That's the genuinely unsettling part. Not that Bankman-Fried lied, but that for a very long time, to a very large number of smart people, he didn't sound like he was lying at all.
