South Africa’s central bank decides today whether 7.25% was ever meant to last

Business Desk

There's a particular kind of tension that builds around a Reserve Bank decision everyone expects to go a certain way, and today is one of those days. The Monetary Policy Committee reconvenes with the repo rate sitting at 7.25%, and the overwhelming market read is that it won't stay there past this afternoon. A quarter-point cut, back to 7%, is what traders, bank economists and the futures curve have all converged on — which makes today less a guessing game than a test of whether the consensus actually holds once the Governor starts reading the statement.

To understand why a cut is even plausible, it helps to remember how South Africa got to 7.25% in the first place. This was not supposed to be a hiking year. The committee spent most of the prior cycle cutting, easing policy for the better part of a year as inflation cooperated and growth needed the help. Then the US-Iran war broke out in February, oil markets lurched, and imported inflation did what imported inflation always does to a current-account-sensitive economy like this one — it forced the Bank's hand. By September, with price pressures running uncomfortably hot, the committee reversed course entirely and hiked, unanimously, back up to 7.25%.

Nine months on, the inflationary shock that justified that hike looks like it has largely worked its way through the system, even if it hasn't fully reversed. Headline inflation is running at 4.4%, above the Bank's 3% target but well shy of the levels that triggered the September move, and the external pressure from the Middle East has eased as the immediate war risk premium has unwound. That is precisely the kind of backdrop that gives a central bank room to declare a hike "job done" and start handing relief back, especially with December fuel prices also pointing lower — a rare moment where two separate cost pressures ease in the same month.

The stakes of getting this right cut in both directions. Cut too early and the Bank risks looking exactly like it did in the lead-up to September: caught flat-footed by a world that doesn't stay calm for long, with Middle East tensions, global shipping costs and a still-fragile rand all capable of reigniting the same imported-inflation problem that forced the last reversal. Hold steady, and it disappoints a market and a heavily indebted consumer base that have spent weeks pricing in exactly the opposite outcome, with mortgage holders and small businesses that geared up for relief left carrying the higher repayment load for another six weeks until the next meeting.

What makes this decision different from the Bank's usual incremental calls is the whiplash quality of the year behind it: cut, cut, cut, then a sudden unanimous hike, and now a vote on whether to undo that hike almost as quickly as it arrived. Few MPC cycles compress that much reversal into twelve months, and it leaves the committee's credibility, as much as the rate itself, on the table this afternoon.

However the vote lands, the practical effect reaches further than bond desks and bank treasuries. A cut flows into every variable-rate mortgage, vehicle repayment and credit card balance in the country within days, at precisely the moment households are also absorbing December's fuel and travel costs. A hold leaves that relief exactly where it's been sitting since September — priced in by the market, but not yet real in anyone's bank account.
