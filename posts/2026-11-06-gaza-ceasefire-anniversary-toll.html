A year into Gaza's ceasefire, Israeli strikes have killed more than 1,600 Palestinians and reconstruction still has not begun

World Desk

A year after a ceasefire was announced in Gaza, Palestinian health officials say Israeli strikes carried out since that announcement have killed more than 1,600 people, a figure that sits uneasily alongside the word "ceasefire" itself and underscores how incomplete the truce has been in practice even as it has held in the broadest sense, preventing a return to full-scale war. This week alone, strikes near Khan Younis killed five Palestinians, including a 12-year-old boy, according to Gaza's civil defence agency, while separate Israeli operations killed several alleged militants, among them a figure the military said had held an Israeli hostage during the war's earlier phase.

Gaza's government-run authorities report that police officers specifically have not been spared either, putting the police death toll since the ceasefire at 75, with five additional officers reportedly killed in a drone strike in the Al-Mawasi area in the days before this report. Figures from Gaza's health and civil-defence bodies cannot be independently verified and differ in detail from the Israeli military's own public accounting of its operations, but the broad pattern they describe, of strikes continuing on a near-daily basis despite the formal end of major hostilities, is not seriously disputed by either side.

On the diplomatic and reconstruction track, movement has been slow enough that frustration is becoming harder for international officials to conceal. Nickolay Mladenov, the high representative of the US-established Board of Peace tasked with overseeing Gaza's reconstruction, arrived in Israel this week specifically to seek permission to bring in tents and building materials, a sign that even the most basic humanitarian logistics, let alone large-scale rebuilding, remain subject to case-by-case negotiation rather than an agreed, functioning process a full year on.

The human cost of that delay is visible in the most immediate, physical terms as winter approaches. Humanitarian organisations report large numbers of displaced families still living in tents along Gaza's beaches, with no durable housing options available and aid groups warning that cold-weather conditions will make an already dire humanitarian situation considerably worse. Separately, an advocacy organisation has documented more than 1,100 children in Gaza who have undergone limb amputations as a result of Israeli strikes during the war, and says Israeli restrictions are limiting many of those children's ability to access specialist treatment abroad.

Inside Israel, domestic politics have added another layer of uncertainty to the reconstruction stalemate. The country's president has been consulting party leaders on the formation of a new government, with Gaza policy featuring prominently in those discussions, while the issue also surfaced repeatedly in the recent American midterm campaign, a reminder that decisions shaping Gaza's immediate future are being negotiated as much in Jerusalem and Washington's domestic political arenas as in any direct talks between the parties to the conflict itself.
