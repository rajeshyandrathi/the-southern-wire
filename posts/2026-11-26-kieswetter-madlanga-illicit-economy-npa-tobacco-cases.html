Kieswetter tells Madlanga commission that 80 tobacco cases vanished into a ‘black hole’ at the NPA

Former SARS commissioner Edward Kieswetter put South Africa’s illicit economy at R1.2 trillion and said the state’s fight against organised crime is too fragmented to work. His testimony continues today.

Crime Desk·Thursday, 26 November 2026·4 min read

Key points

  • Former SARS commissioner Edward Kieswetter testified at the Madlanga commission on Wednesday, 25 November
  • He estimated South Africa’s illicit economy at R1.2 trillion and said crime has outpaced the state’s response since 2021
  • SARS referred 80 illicit tobacco cases to the NPA more than three years ago and has heard nothing on their progress
  • He proposed a shared national measure of the illicit economy and a principals committee to track priority cases
  • His testimony continues on Thursday, 26 November

Edward Kieswetter, who ran the South African Revenue Service from May 2019 until April this year, told the Madlanga commission on Wednesday that the state is losing ground to organised crime because its agencies do not work as one system. He put the size of the illicit economy at about R1.2 trillion, Business Day reported.

Kieswetter said the state is responding, but that its response is “inadequate to deal with the growing phenomenon,” according to the Mail & Guardian. Since 2021, he said, criminality has grown faster than the state’s ability to resist it, and South Africa has no single agency responsible for fighting organised crime.

He also cautioned that evidence heard in public at the commission should not be treated as proven findings, Daily Maverick reported. His testimony, based on a 16-page written submission and a presentation, will continue on Thursday.

Cases that went nowhere

The sharpest part of his evidence was about what happens after SARS finishes an investigation. SARS handed 80 illicit tobacco cases to the National Prosecuting Authority, and more than three years later it still has no idea how far they have got. He described the gap between the institutions as a “black hole”.

The problem, he said, is built into the law. SARS can prepare a case that is ready for prosecution but has no power to prosecute it itself, and the NPA has no legal duty to report back to SARS. In 2025/26 alone, SARS ran 42 projects and 534 investigations involving estimated losses of more than R42 billion.

Evidence leader Matthew Chaskalson pointed out that the institutions measure success in ways that do not connect: the police and the Hawks count trial-ready dockets, while the NPA reports conviction rates. The NPA has reported a 90% conviction rate in organised crime matters, but could not say how many of those cases came from SARS, police or Hawks referrals. Kieswetter warned this can push prosecutors towards simpler cases that are easier to win. “This is possibly the best example, commissioners, of how this becomes perverse and dysfunctional,” he said.

Follow the money

Using a packet of cigarettes as an example, Kieswetter walked the commission through how criminal networks can touch every step of a product’s journey, from farming and manufacturing to transport and retail, alongside a hidden chain of payments, front companies and money laundering. He listed tobacco, fuel and alcohol, illegal mining, drugs and precursor chemicals, customs fraud and counterfeit goods, tax fraud and the construction mafia as the priority illicit markets.

He told the commission that the state and criminal networks compete for the same skilled people and tools, but the networks “do not have to follow the rules”. Ports were singled out as vulnerable to smuggling and customs fraud, including false invoices and wrongly described cargo. The commission has previously heard evidence about two large drug consignments that passed through the Port of Durban.

Kieswetter did not call for new agencies or mergers. Instead, he proposed a national framework to measure the illicit economy and the harm it causes, led by Statistics South Africa and National Treasury, and a committee of agency heads to oversee priority organised crime cases. “We don’t need more institutions, but we don’t have an institutional ecosystem,” he said, according to the Mail & Guardian.
