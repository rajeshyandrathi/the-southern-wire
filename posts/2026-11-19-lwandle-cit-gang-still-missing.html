Crime & Courts

Everyone else got shot. The Lwandle cash-in-transit gang just vanished — with police guns.

Crime Desk

South Africa's cash-in-transit war has had an unusually clean month, if "clean" is the word for it: when a crew hit a Free State delivery and fled toward Gauteng, police caught up and shot two of them dead. When a Gqeberha gang opened fire on pursuing officers along Kempston Road, five of them didn't survive the chase. Two jobs, two shootouts, seven bodies. Then there's Lwandle, where five armed men walked up to two security officers delivering cash at the Somerset Crossing shopping centre, took the money, and simply disappeared — along with the officers' service firearms.

That last detail is the one the industry doesn't want repeated. A cash-in-transit robbery is, at its ugliest, a transfer of money from a company to criminals. A cash-in-transit robbery that also strips armed response officers of their weapons is something else: a transfer of firepower from the legal security industry into whatever network ordered the hit. Every pistol taken that way has a half-life measured in other crimes — hijackings, home invasions, the next CIT job down the line — and investigators know it, which is why the weapons, not just the missing rand value, are what the follow-up is actually chasing.

It also exposes an uncomfortable asymmetry in how this war is being won and lost. The crews that got caught this month were caught because they ran into a police response fast enough, and armed enough, to end the chase in a shootout. The Lwandle crew didn't get caught because there was no chase to catch them in — they were in and out before any response arrived, which is either extraordinary luck or, more likely, exactly the kind of planning and inside knowledge that has repeatedly been flagged in this industry. Police have themselves warned publicly that insider complicity — a guard, a scheduler, a company employee feeding timing information to a gang — remains one of the biggest open vulnerabilities in cash logistics, and a clean five-man job with no struggle and no witnesses talking fits that warning uncomfortably well.

No arrests have been made in the Lwandle case. That puts it in stark contrast with Gqeberha and the Free State, where the state doesn't need a trial to extract the obvious lesson for its own messaging: resistance gets you killed. The quieter, more dangerous lesson sitting in Lwandle's unsolved file is the opposite one — that the crews doing it right, with inside information and clean exits, are the ones nobody is currently catching at all.

Fidelity and the other cash-management firms have spent years promising hardened routes, randomised scheduling and better vetting of the people who move money for a living. Three cash heists and seven dead suspects into November alone, with one crew still walking around armed with police-grade pistols, is the most concrete evidence yet of how much of that promise remains unrealised — and of how much more there is to lose than the cash itself when it fails.
