A dam, a drone war and a blackout: Sudan's power grid becomes the newest front line

World Desk

Sudan's war has found a new target this week in Ed Damazin, the capital of Blue Nile state and home to the Roseires Dam, one of the country's most important sources of hydroelectric power. A sustained drone assault by Rapid Support Forces fighters struck the city overnight, triggering a wide power outage that the government in Khartoum has described as part of a deliberate campaign against Sudan's electricity infrastructure rather than an isolated strike.

Sudan's cabinet responded by ordering a "maximum state of alert" specifically over the drone strikes targeting power stations, language that signals how seriously officials are treating the threat to a grid that was already fragile well before this latest escalation. Years of conflict have left much of Sudan's infrastructure running on patched repairs and improvised workarounds; a coordinated effort to knock out generation and transmission capacity in a region as strategically important as Blue Nile threatens consequences that extend far beyond Ed Damazin's own residents.

The dam itself is the reason the location matters so much. Roseires has long been central to Sudan's power generation and irrigation capacity, supplying electricity and water management functions that ripple outward into agricultural regions dependent on the Blue Nile's flow. An attack that disrupts operations there is not simply a local blackout; it is a strike against infrastructure that other parts of the country rely on indirectly, which is almost certainly part of why the RSF has made it a target.

The assault on Ed Damazin fits into a broader pattern described by Sudan's army chief of staff, Yasir al-Atta, who has said government forces are pushing toward Darfur and Kordofan as part of a wider military effort. Blue Nile's drone strikes, in that framing, are not a separate front but one piece of a war that has spread across multiple regions simultaneously, with power infrastructure increasingly treated as a legitimate target by combatants on both sides rather than something kept outside the fighting.

For civilians in Ed Damazin, the immediate reality is simpler and harsher than any strategic framing: a power outage layered on top of a conflict that has already displaced millions nationally and strained humanitarian access across multiple regions. Each new front the war opens, including one fought with drones against dams and substations rather than soldiers on a battlefield, adds another layer of hardship to a crisis the international community has struggled to resource adequately for years.
