South Africa's film industry has given up on one department and gone over its head

Culture Desk

The Save SA Film and TV Jobs Coalition has run out of patience with a negotiating table it has sat at for the better part of a year. Talks with the Department of Trade, Industry and Competition over the stalled Film and Television Production Incentive have collapsed again, the coalition says, for what feels like the umpteenth time in a crisis that stretches back to the start of 2026. Rather than request another meeting, the coalition is now asking the Presidency to step in directly, a move that amounts to a public declaration that the department responsible for the incentive has stopped being a productive partner in fixing it.

The production incentive is not a minor line item. It is the mechanism that has made South Africa competitive as a filming location against other markets offering their own rebates, and its disruption has left productions and the crews who depend on them in financial limbo, waiting on payments and clarity that the department has repeatedly promised and repeatedly failed to deliver. Film workers have been blunt about the consequences in public testimony over the past year, describing an industry being brought "to its knees" by the funding uncertainty, language that is rarely used lightly by people whose livelihoods depend on productions actually shooting on schedule.

Layered onto the funding fight is a second, slower-moving anxiety about who actually owns South African storytelling now. Canal+'s acquisition of MultiChoice has already prompted warnings from industry commentators that the end of an African-controlled streaming giant leaves local content increasingly dependent on decisions made by foreign media executives with their own commercial priorities. A funding crisis and an ownership shift landing in the same year is not a coincidence the industry is inclined to read generously: both point toward a local production sector with less control over its own survival than it had five years ago.

Going to the Presidency is as much a statement as a strategy. Coalitions do not escalate past a government department lightly, since it risks souring the working relationship they will still need once the immediate crisis passes, but it also signals how little runway the sector believes it has left. Productions can only sit in financial limbo for so long before crews move to other markets, equipment houses lay off staff, and the skills base that took decades to build starts eroding in ways that don't come back quickly once the money returns.

What happens next depends on whether the Presidency treats this as a one-off escalation to be smoothed over, or as proof that the DTIC relationship needs a structural fix rather than another round of talks. The coalition's message, stripped of its diplomatic framing, is simple: the usual channel stopped working months ago, and the industry cannot afford to keep waiting for it to start working again.
