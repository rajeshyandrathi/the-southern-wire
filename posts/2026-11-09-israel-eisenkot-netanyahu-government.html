Israel's president bypasses Netanyahu entirely, handing his fiercest rival the first shot at forming a government

World Desk

Israeli President Isaac Herzog handed the mandate to form the country's next government to Gadi Eisenkot on Saturday, a decision that amounts to the most direct institutional rebuke Benjamin Netanyahu has faced in his long political career. Eisenkot, a former military chief of staff who has built his political identity substantially around opposing Netanyahu, received 67 recommendations for the premiership from members of the 120-seat Knesset against Netanyahu's 46 — a majority commanding enough that Herzog had little real alternative but to task him with the attempt, however uncomfortable that outcome is for the man who has dominated Israeli politics for most of the past two decades.

The arithmetic behind that majority is itself politically fraught. Eisenkot's own parliamentary bloc controls only 48 seats, well short of the 61 needed to govern, which means his 67 recommendations depended on the backing of Israel's two Arab-led parties, holding 19 seats between them. Eisenkot has publicly ruled out forming a government that includes those parties as coalition members, which leaves him searching for a path that uses their support to secure the mandate itself while building an actual governing majority from elsewhere — most plausibly either a minority government informally propped up by Arab-party abstentions, or a broader unity coalition that draws in major Jewish parties currently aligned with neither bloc.

Netanyahu's response has been to call publicly for exactly that kind of unity government, provided he remains at its head — a proposal Eisenkot dismissed almost immediately as political spin rather than a genuine offer. The rejection is unsurprising given how explicitly Eisenkot's political brand has been built in opposition to Netanyahu's leadership, particularly over the conduct and accountability failures associated with the war following the October 7 attacks, a critique that has resonated with a share of the Israeli public large enough to produce Saturday's Knesset arithmetic.

One potentially significant crack in Netanyahu's position came from United Torah Judaism, the ultra-Orthodox party that has been a reliable coalition partner for him in the past. The party declined to formally endorse Netanyahu for the mandate and signalled it could be open to joining an Eisenkot-led coalition instead — a shift that, if it holds through actual coalition negotiations rather than proving to be a temporary hedge, would represent exactly the kind of defection from Netanyahu's traditional base that could make an Eisenkot government durable rather than merely initially viable.

Eisenkot now has 28 days to assemble a coalition, with a possible 14-day extension available if talks are progressing but incomplete. Should he fail within that window, the mandate would most likely pass back to Netanyahu, whose political survival has outlasted enough previous predictions of its end that few analysts are treating this setback as necessarily terminal. But for a prime minister who has spent years insisting he remained indispensable to Israeli governance, watching the country's president hand his bitterest rival first crack at replacing him is a signal, regardless of how the coalition talks ultimately resolve, that a meaningful share of Israel's political establishment no longer agrees.
