South African fintechs are still raising money, just not the way they used to

Tech Desk

The headline numbers on African tech funding this year make for grim reading: total continental funding in the first half of 2026 rose only 1.4 percent year on year to $1.44 billion, even as the number of actual deals being struck fell sharply, a combination that points to money concentrating in fewer, larger rounds rather than a genuine broadening of investor appetite. Yet South African fintech founders tell a more nuanced story, one in which selective, well-structured deals are still getting done even as the easy venture capital of a few years ago has all but disappeared.

South Africa's fintech sector captured $125 million, or just over 37 percent of the country's total tech funding, in 2025 according to industry tracker Disrupt Africa, and the deals that have closed since suggest lenders and merchant-infrastructure platforms remain the segment investors trust most. Working-capital lender Bridgement secured a R330 million facility from Rand Merchant Bank and Standard Bank, a debt-style structure that lets established banks participate in fintech lending growth without taking on direct equity risk, a model that has become increasingly common as pure venture rounds have grown harder to close.

Merchant infrastructure platform Littlefish took a different route, closing a $9.5 million Series A led by Partech after moving past an earlier founder legal dispute, evidence that investors are willing to back a company through governance turbulence provided the underlying business case for connecting African banks to merchant payment rails remains intact. The firm's pitch, building infrastructure that banks can use rather than compete against, has resonated with investors wary of funding fintechs that position themselves as direct threats to incumbent lenders.

Buy-now-pay-later and early-wage-access products, long a crowded corner of the South African fintech market, have also kept attracting capital despite warnings from consumer debt counsellors about the risk such products pose to financially stretched households. The recent merger of early-wage-access platforms Paymenow and PayCurve into what the combined entity calls the country's first fully integrated employee financial wellness platform reflects the same consolidation logic playing out across the sector: with fresh capital harder to raise independently, scale increasingly has to come from combining with a rival rather than out-competing them.

What emerges from the current funding environment is less a fintech winter than a fintech sorting process, one separating businesses with defensible, bank-adjacent infrastructure plays from the more speculative consumer lending models that dominated the sector's earlier funding boom. For founders still trying to raise, the lesson from this year's deals is blunt: investors want proof of a sustainable unit economics story now, not a growth narrative to be proven later.
