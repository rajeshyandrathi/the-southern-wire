A year into Gaza's ceasefire, more than 100 aid groups say it exists in name only

World Desk

It has been just over a month since the formal one-year mark of the Gaza ceasefire passed, and the assessment from the humanitarian sector has only hardened in that time. More than 100 humanitarian and human rights organisations have put their names to a joint statement arguing the ceasefire exists in name only, saying Israeli forces continue to kill and injure Palestinians daily despite an arrangement that was supposed to have ended active hostilities a year ago.

The gap between the ceasefire's stated terms and its practical effect centres heavily on governance. The 20-point plan that underpinned the agreement envisioned a National Committee for the Administration of Gaza taking over civil administration of the territory, a body meant to provide exactly the kind of functioning local governance that a genuine end to the war would require. According to reporting on the plan's implementation, Israel has not granted that committee entry into Gaza at all — leaving the territory's day-to-day administration in a kind of institutional limbo that neither matches wartime occupation nor resembles the civilian self-governance the ceasefire promised.

Israel's position, as laid out by the Prime Minister's Office, places the blame for the plan's stalled implementation squarely on Hamas. Officials say Israel is doing what it can to allow winter equipment into Gaza ahead of the colder months, while calling on international mediators to press Hamas to disarm immediately throughout the territory — a precondition Israel maintains must be met before the broader 20-point framework, including the governance handover, can move forward as designed.

That dispute over sequencing — disarmament first, governance handover first, or some negotiated middle ground — is precisely the kind of disagreement that ceasefires are supposed to resolve through implementation mechanisms, and precisely the kind that, left unresolved for a full year, starts to look less like a temporary snag than a structural flaw in how the agreement was designed. Each side can point to the other's non-compliance as justification for its own, and humanitarian organisations working on the ground say it is Gaza's civilian population that absorbs the consequences of that stalemate.

A year on, the ceasefire's survival on paper — the absence of a declared return to full-scale war — has not translated into the lived reality it promised. For the aid organisations now speaking with one voice, that distinction between a ceasefire that technically holds and one that actually protects civilians has become the entire story of the year since it was signed.
