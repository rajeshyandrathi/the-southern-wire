After a landmark genocide ruling against Myanmar, Britain sanctions generals and South Africa’s own case watches closely

The world court has found a state responsible for genocide for the first time, ruling that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya. Britain has followed with sanctions and a planned jet fuel ban, and legal experts say the judgment will shape South Africa’s case against Israel.

World Desk·Wednesday, 25 November 2026·3 min read

Key points

  • The International Court of Justice ruled on 19 November that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya in 2017
  • The central genocide finding was 9-6, with South Africans Dire Tladi and Navi Pillay in the majority
  • Britain sanctioned five senior officers on Monday and plans to ban aviation fuel supplies to Myanmar
  • Fourteen countries, including The Gambia, issued a joint statement on the ruling
  • Legal analysts say the judgment will likely shape South Africa’s case against Israel

For the first time, the world’s highest court has found a state responsible for genocide. On Thursday, 19 November, the International Court of Justice in The Hague ruled in a case brought by The Gambia that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya during military “clearance operations” in Rakhine state in 2017, UN News reported.

The court found that Myanmar’s security forces killed members of the group, caused them serious harm and imposed measures to prevent births, and that the state failed to prevent and punish genocide. It estimated about 10,000 Rohingya were killed and nearly 400 villages partly or totally destroyed. Hundreds of thousands fled to Bangladesh.

Myanmar has been ordered to prosecute suspects, allow refugees to return safely and pay compensation, with the amount to be set later. It must report back within two years. The judgment is final and cannot be appealed. UN Secretary-General António Guterres called it a “powerful recognition” of the victims. Myanmar had rejected the allegations as unsubstantiated during hearings in January.

Britain moves first

On Monday, 23 November, British Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband told the House of Commons that the UK formally recognises the crimes as genocide. Britain froze the assets of and banned travel by five senior officers: Major Generals Kyaw Swar Oo, Kyaw Soe, Myo Min Tun and Myat Thet Oo, and Brigadier General Myo Htut Hlaing, the Burmese outlet DVB and LBC reported.

Some reports described military leader Min Aung Hlaing as among those sanctioned. LBC reported that he is not one of the five, as Britain already sanctioned him in 2021.

In the Commons, shadow foreign minister Wendy Morton pointed out that the judges had split nine to six on the central finding, LBC reported. Miliband replied that “a majority view is the view of the court”. Since the 2021 coup, Britain has imposed 19 rounds of sanctions on Myanmar covering 42 individuals and 39 entities, according to DVB.

Britain will also bring in legislation to ban the export and supply of aviation fuel to Myanmar, aimed at curbing air strikes on civilians, and has expelled Myanmar’s defence attaché. It has pledged £5 million in humanitarian aid and up to £3 million for justice and accountability work. Miliband said it was “simply the first part” of what Britain intends to do, and the UK has asked for an urgent UN Security Council session. Thirteen other countries, including The Gambia, Canada and Australia, joined a British-led statement on the ruling.

The South African connection

Two South Africans were among the nine judges in the majority on the central genocide finding: Judge Dire Tladi and Navi Pillay, who sat as a judge appointed for this case, according to an analysis on the legal site Just Security. The finding was carried 9-6, while the order to investigate and prosecute suspects passed 13-2.

The same analysis argues the ruling will likely shape South Africa’s pending genocide case against Israel. The authors say South Africa would not need to show a formal plan or policy, that official statements could carry real weight, and that following the laws of war would not on its own be a full defence. Every case turns on its own facts, though, and the court’s membership will partly change before that case is decided.
