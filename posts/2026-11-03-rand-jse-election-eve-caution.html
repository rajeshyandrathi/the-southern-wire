Rand holds steady on the eve of the vote as investors look past election-day noise

Business Desk·Tuesday, 3 November 2026

With South Africans heading to the polls on Wednesday for the country's most consequential local government elections since the Government of National Unity was formed, currency and equity markets have responded with something close to a shrug. The rand has traded in a tight band against the dollar through the past few sessions, hovering in the mid-16s, while the JSE All Share has held firm above the 107,000 mark — hardly the jittery run-up one might expect ahead of a vote widely tipped to produce a wave of hung councils.

Analysts attribute the calm less to confidence in any particular election outcome than to the market's accurate read of what municipal elections actually change, and don't. Local government results reshuffle mayors, councils and tender pipelines, but they carry none of the national policy risk that moves currency markets during general elections — the kind that rattled the rand sharply ahead of the 2024 vote that birthed the GNU in the first place.

Instead, traders say their attention is already fixed on 19 November, when the South African Reserve Bank delivers its next interest rate decision with the benchmark currently sitting at 7.25% after September's hike. Inflation data released in the coming weeks will shape expectations heading into that meeting far more than Wednesday's municipal results, however dramatic the coalition arithmetic turns out to be in Johannesburg, Tshwane or Cape Town.

That isn't to say local government outcomes are irrelevant to the economy. Persistent service delivery failures, especially around water and electricity reticulation at municipal level, have repeatedly dragged on business confidence in affected metros, and a wave of newly hung councils could, in a worst case, slow already-sluggish infrastructure spending even further while coalition talks drag on.

For now, though, South African markets are treating Wednesday as a political story rather than an economic one — a verdict that will be tested in the weeks after the count, once new councils, and the deals that form them, start making their first spending decisions.
