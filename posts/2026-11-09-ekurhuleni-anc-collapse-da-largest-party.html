The ANC's vote share collapses in Ekurhuleni, and the DA is the largest party in a metro it has never led

Politics Desk

Ekurhuleni has never been DA territory. For two decades the East Rand metro, built on the mining and manufacturing towns strung along the Reef, has been ANC country almost by default, the kind of place where the governing party's dominance felt less like a contest than a fact of geography. That fact stopped being true last week. With counting essentially complete, the DA has emerged as Ekurhuleni's largest party, a result so far outside the metro's recent history that even seasoned analysts describe it as a historic shift rather than a swing.

The scale of the collapse is what makes it remarkable. This is not a case of the ANC losing a close contest at the margins; its vote share has fallen far enough that a party which spent the last several elections treating Ekurhuleni as a stronghold now finds itself negotiating from a position of genuine weakness. The metro joins Johannesburg and Tshwane in Gauteng's new political geography, where every one of the province's three major metros now requires a coalition to govern, and where the ANC, rather than anchoring those coalitions as it has for most of the democratic era, is increasingly the party other parties choose whether to include.

For the DA, the result raises a question it has not had to answer in Ekurhuleni before: what does it actually do with the win. Being the largest party is not the same as having a majority, and the DA's path to governing the metro will still run through smaller parties and, almost certainly, through some accommodation with either the ANC it just displaced or ActionSA, which has been pressing its advantage hard in every Gauteng metro this cycle. The DA's federal leadership, already consumed by the standoff over Helen Zille's mayoral prospects in Johannesburg, now has a second, structurally different negotiation to run at the same time, with different local personalities and a different balance of smaller-party leverage.

For the ANC, the loss lands as part of a broader pattern rather than an isolated shock. The party's national leadership has spent the days since the election publicly weighing whether to pursue coalition seats at all or to position itself as opposition in metros it can no longer anchor, a debate that Ekurhuleni's result will only sharpen. Governing from outside, as the junior partner or as formal opposition, is not a role the ANC has had to rehearse in Gauteng's industrial heartland before, and how it adapts to that role over the coming fortnight will say as much about the party's national trajectory as anything that happens in Johannesburg's more closely watched standoff.

What happens next in Ekurhuleni will likely be decided less by ideology than by the same practical calculations playing out across Gauteng: who can assemble 50% plus one, and what each smaller party extracts in exchange for its votes. But the symbolism of the result itself is already fixed. A metro the ANC built its Gauteng project around for a generation now has a different largest party, and no amount of coalition arithmetic changes what that signals about where the province's politics are heading.
