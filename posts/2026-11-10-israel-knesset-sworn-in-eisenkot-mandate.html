Israel swears in its most right-wing parliament ever, and the real power play happens tomorrow

World Desk

Israel's 26th Knesset was sworn in today, opening with the formality of electing a speaker but carrying, beneath that formality, the weight of being described across multiple outlets as the most right-wing parliament the country has ever assembled. The composition of the chamber matters less for its own ceremony than for what it sets up over the next 24 hours: President Isaac Herzog is expected to announce by 11 November which party leader gets the formal mandate to try to form the next government, a decision that has effectively already been shaped by the numbers lawmakers have put on record.

Those numbers favour Gadi Eisenkot, the former military chief of staff turned political rival to Benjamin Netanyahu, who has secured recommendations from 67 of the Knesset's 120 members, well clear of Netanyahu's 46. Under Israel's system, a president is not strictly bound to hand the mandate to whoever has the most recommendations, but a gap of that size makes any other outcome politically difficult to justify, and Herzog's decision is widely expected to confirm what the recommendation count already signals.

Getting the mandate and successfully using it are two different problems, and Eisenkot's coalition arithmetic has a structural tension built into it. His 67-seat bloc depends in part on support from Arab parties who are backing him from outside any formal coalition rather than as governing partners, because several of the other parties inside his prospective bloc campaigned explicitly against governing alongside Arab-led factions. That leaves Eisenkot needing Arab-party votes to secure the mandate while needing to keep those same parties at arm's length to hold his own coalition together, a balancing act that has no obvious clean solution.

Netanyahu, for his part, has not conceded the field. He remains caretaker prime minister while the process plays out, and has been publicly pushing an alternative framing, describing Eisenkot's emerging coalition as a "left-wing government" in an attempt to pressure wavering lawmakers before Herzog's decision is finalised. It is a familiar tactic from a politician who has spent decades treating the labels attached to a coalition as a weapon in their own right, regardless of how accurately those labels describe the parties actually involved.

Once Herzog formally hands over the mandate, the clock for building an actual government starts running, and the structural tension in Eisenkot's numbers will stop being a talking point and start being the thing that determines whether Israel gets a functioning coalition at all. A parliament sworn in as the most right-wing in the country's history, handing power to a government that likely needs support it cannot formally include, is not a contradiction that resolves itself quietly.
