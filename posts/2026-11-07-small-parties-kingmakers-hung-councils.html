In a fractured election, South Africa's smallest parties have become its most powerful

Politics Desk

Parties that could never have dreamed of running a metro on their own are suddenly the most courted entities in South African politics. With neither the ANC nor the DA anywhere close to an outright majority in several of the country's biggest councils, the handful of seats held by ActionSA, the Freedom Front Plus, the Patriotic Alliance and a scattering of smaller outfits have become the currency that will decide who governs Johannesburg, Tshwane, Ekurhuleni and a long list of smaller municipalities over the next five years.

The mechanics are blunt. A party that won barely two or three percent of the vote nationally can hold the single seat that tips a council from one majority to another, and every one of those parties knows it. That has turned the fortnight between the election and the legal deadline for council sittings into a frantic round of private meetings, phone calls and, inevitably, leaks, as bigger parties try to lock in commitments before a rival gets there first. Several accounts of the post-election jostling describe offers of deputy mayoral positions, committee chairs and budget concessions being dangled in return for a handful of votes that would once have been considered politically irrelevant.

The deadline itself is unforgiving. Councils that fail to elect a speaker and a mayor within the window set by the Municipal Structures Act risk being placed under direct provincial scrutiny, and in the worst cases, under section 139 intervention, the same mechanism used in municipalities that have collapsed into financial or administrative dysfunction. Nobody wants a repeat of that outcome in a capital city or an economic hub, which is precisely the leverage smaller parties are using to extract concessions disproportionate to their vote share.

There is also a longer-term risk being priced in by political strategists on all sides: a coalition stitched together purely to win a mayoral vote, with no shared programme beyond keeping a rival out of office, has a well-documented history of collapsing within eighteen months once the spoils of office are divided and the next crisis over a budget or a bin-collection contract arrives. Johannesburg's last term offered a national case study in exactly that kind of instability, and several of the smaller parties now holding the balance of power say privately they intend to demand written commitments on service delivery before signing anything, precisely to avoid becoming the scapegoat the next time a coalition falls apart.

For now, the arithmetic rules. Whichever combination of unlikely partners can assemble a majority in the next two weeks will set the tone, for better or worse, for how coalition government works in South Africa's cities for the rest of the decade. The parties doing the counting know it, and they are not in a hurry to give that leverage away cheaply.
