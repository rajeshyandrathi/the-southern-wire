Two South African-rooted payments startups just raised $6 million each

Tech Desk

Cross-border payments have quietly become the most fundable idea in African fintech, and this week produced two fresh pieces of evidence. Cape Town-based Lumepay closed a $6 million pre-seed round from a mix of angel investors and local fintech backers, money the company plans to use to deepen a footprint that already spans eight African markets, including a recent launch in Kenya. Within days, Enza, a payments infrastructure company operating across Egypt, South Africa, Ghana and Nigeria, closed its own $6 million round, a Seed+ raise led by 4DX Ventures with British International Investment, Quona Capital and Algebra Ventures all participating.

The identical dollar figure is a coincidence of timing, but the underlying thesis both companies are selling to investors is the same one: African businesses lose a disproportionate share of revenue to the friction of moving money across borders, whether that's a South African retailer paying a Kenyan supplier or a Nigerian merchant settling with a partner in Ghana, and whoever builds the cleanest rails for that friction stands to capture a market that legacy banking correspondent networks have never served well. Lumepay's eight-market footprint and Enza's four-country infrastructure play are two different bets on the same underlying problem, built by teams that clearly believe the addressable market is large enough for more than one winner.

Context matters here. South African fintech attracted $125 million of the $335.9 million raised by the country's startups in 2025, more than a third of all funding and proof that payments remains the sector investors trust most when the rest of the venture market is cautious. That caution is real: across the whole continent, funding rose only 1.4% year on year to $1.44 billion in the first half of 2026, with early-stage rounds taking the hardest hit. Two $6 million raises landing in the same week, for companies solving a problem investors clearly understand, suggests payments infrastructure is one of the few categories still able to pull capital even while the broader funding environment tightens.

Backers matter as much as amounts. Enza's investor list, 4DX Ventures alongside British International Investment, Quona Capital and Algebra Ventures, reads like a roster of funds that specialise in exactly this kind of infrastructure play across emerging markets, the sort of institutional validation that tends to open doors with the banks and regulators a cross-border payments company eventually has to work with. Lumepay's funding leans more toward angel and local fintech money for now, which suggests a company still earlier in proving the model before the larger infrastructure funds commit.

Neither raise is large enough on its own to reshape the continent's payments landscape. Together, they are a signal worth watching: in a year when African venture funding overall has struggled to grow, the investors still willing to write cheques keep landing on the same bet, that the fees and friction of moving money across African borders are a problem worth solving at scale, and worth funding even when little else gets funded easily.
