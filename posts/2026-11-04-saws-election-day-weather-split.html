A split-screen election day: scorching heat in the west, storm warnings in the east

Weather Desk·Wednesday, 4 November 2026

South Africans queuing to vote on Wednesday are experiencing two entirely different weather stories depending on which side of the country they live on. The South African Weather Service has forecast fine, warm to very hot conditions across the central and western interior, with voters in Gauteng and the Northern Cape likely to spend long stretches in queues under harsh, direct sun, while the eastern provinces face a cooler but far less settled picture, with cloud cover and the renewed threat of severe thunderstorms.

The eastern warnings are not new so much as persistent. A surface trough that has been feeding isolated to scattered showers and thunderstorms across the central and eastern parts of the country for much of the past week remains active, and SAWS has kept yellow-level warnings in place over parts of Limpopo, Mpumalanga, KwaZulu-Natal, the Eastern Cape, the Free State and Gauteng. A separate yellow level four impact warning for sections of Limpopo, Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal carries the more serious risks: localised flooding, power outages and disrupted communications in areas already strained by weeks of intermittent severe weather.

For election logistics specifically, the weather split creates two very different sets of challenges for the IEC and the voters it is trying to accommodate. In the hot, dry west, the concern is heat exhaustion among voters standing in exposed queues for hours, particularly the elderly and those with young children in tow. In the stormier east, officials have had to plan around the possibility that a severe thunderstorm cell could force a temporary halt to voting at individual stations, something the IEC has contingency procedures for but would still rather avoid on a day already carrying enough operational complexity.

Fire danger adds a third dimension to the picture, with parts of the Northern Cape and Eastern Cape under extreme fire danger warnings even as other provinces worry about flooding from the same system. Springbok reached 35 degrees Celsius in recent days, a reminder that South Africa's spring weather this year has swung between genuine extremes rather than settling into a predictable seasonal pattern.

SAWS meteorologists say this volatility is likely to persist through the rest of the week, with little indication that either the heat in the west or the storm activity in the east will ease significantly before the weekend. For an election already defined by uncertainty on the political front, the weather has, fittingly, refused to offer voters a single, simple story either.
