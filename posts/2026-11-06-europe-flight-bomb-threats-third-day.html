A third day of bomb hoaxes grounds and diverts flights across Europe, and nobody can say who's behind it

World Desk

European aviation is heading into a third straight day of bomb-threat disruption, with planes diverted to military air bases and provincial airports across half a dozen countries even as every search so far has turned up nothing. What began as a handful of scattered hoaxes earlier in the week has widened into a pattern authorities cannot yet explain: dozens of flights targeted since the start of the month, no device ever found, and no single group willing to own the whole campaign.

The clearest single incident came when an Aer Lingus flight from Dublin to Rome received a threat while crossing French airspace and was diverted to a French military air base, where a bomb squad boarded and searched the aircraft before clearing it to continue. Spain had its own cluster of scares on the same day: police said three threats arrived by email within twenty minutes of each other, naming an Air Europa flight from Madrid to Paris and two inbound Iberia services from Rome and Milan. Two further aircraft were diverted into Milan's Malpensa airport after threats called in against flights still in the air, while a pair of Ryanair services bound for Sofia from Madrid and Copenhagen landed without incident after their own warnings were assessed mid-flight.

Finnair is among the carriers whose schedules have been knocked around by the wider wave, and Danish police have confirmed they are investigating threats made against air traffic passing through their airspace, even without a specific aircraft named. Taken together, the list of airlines and airports touched by the hoaxes now spans Ireland, France, Spain, Italy, Denmark and Bulgaria, with each new day adding fresh names rather than narrowing the list down.

Investigators have not tied the threats to a single actor. A Russian extremist group has claimed responsibility for two of the threats against Rome-bound flights, but security officials have been careful not to extend that claim across the rest of the pattern, and the European Union's transport commissioner said this week that it remains "too early" to say whether the incidents are coordinated at all. Aviation security analysts have instead described a suspected hoax campaign, one built to tie up air forces, bomb squads and airport security teams rather than to actually bring down an aircraft, pointing to the complete absence of any device found despite dozens of searches carried out since the threats began.

For passengers, the practical effect has been the same regardless of who is responsible: delayed connections, aircraft pulled out of rotation for searches, and the now-familiar sight of a jet sitting on an unfamiliar runway surrounded by emergency vehicles while its cabin is cleared row by row. European governments have so far resisted calls for blanket new security measures, wary of rewarding whoever is behind the hoaxes with the disruption they appear to be chasing, but officials have acknowledged that a third consecutive day of incidents without an arrest or a confirmed motive is testing that restraint.
