A R50 billion number, a new bus fleet and a refurbished station: Pretoria gets a look at PRASA's recovery in hard figures

Lifestyle Desk

For years, any story about South African passenger rail started from a position of apology — tracks stripped by cable thieves, stations gutted by fire, timetables that existed on paper more than in practice. The story Transport Minister Barbara Creecy told during a recent oversight visit to Pretoria was different in kind: not a promise of recovery, but a receipt for one already well underway, with a number attached that's hard to ignore. R50 billion is the figure now being cited for the Passenger Rail Agency of South Africa's upgrade programme, covering everything from a new bus fleet to a refurbished train station in the capital.

The bus fleet detail is easy to overlook next to a headline figure that large, but it's arguably the more telling piece. PRASA running its own buses is an admission that rail alone can't yet carry Gauteng's commuters through a recovery that is still, by the agency's own account, a work in progress — buses filling gaps on routes where rail capacity hasn't caught up with demand, rather than the agency waiting for every line to be fully restored before moving a single additional passenger.

That gap is most visible on the Mabopane line into Tshwane, which Creecy's visit flagged directly: the route currently runs only about three trains an hour, a frequency commuters living along one of Gauteng's most densely populated corridors say can't absorb the demand reliably. It's the paradox sitting underneath PRASA's recent run of good headlines — reopened corridors, refurbished rolling stock, new blue "People's Trains" replacing the old yellow sets — a recovery visible enough to draw a cabinet minister's attention, while the lived experience for commuters on specific routes is still standing room only during peak hours.

PRASA's parallel success running extra services around major sporting fixtures, coordinating with the Gautrain for rugby Tests at Loftus Versfeld and shuttling fans at a fraction of the usual fare, shows what the network can do when it's resourced and scheduled around a known surge. The harder test is doing the same for an ordinary Tuesday commute, where there's no stadium crowd forcing the extra trains onto the timetable, just the same tired frustration of standing on a Mabopane platform watching a packed train pull away.

Creecy's figures suggest the political will and the budget are both there in a way they haven't reliably been in a decade of rail decline. Whether R50 billion converts into frequency where commuters actually need it — not just shinier stations and newer buses, but more trains on the lines people use every day — is the question that will decide whether this oversight visit reads, in a year's time, as the turning point it was pitched as.
