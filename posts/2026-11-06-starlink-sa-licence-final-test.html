Starlink's long road to a South African licence narrows to one final regulatory test

Tech Desk

Few technology stories in South Africa have run longer, or generated more political heat, than Elon Musk's attempt to get Starlink legally operating in the country. Years after the satellite internet service first became available to South African users through grey-market resellers and roaming workarounds, the regulatory process that would actually legalise it has finally reached what officials now describe as its final stretch, with the telecoms regulator having set out in detail what the company must do to secure a licence.

The sticking point throughout has been South Africa's black economic empowerment rules, which have historically required telecoms licensees to cede 30 percent ownership to historically disadvantaged groups, a structure Starlink's ownership model was never designed to accommodate. The breakthrough, such as it is, came late last year when Communications Minister Solly Malatsi confirmed a policy shift allowing satellite operators to meet an equity-equivalent standard instead, through investment in initiatives such as rural school connectivity, rather than ceding direct equity in the local operating entity.

That concession alone was not the end of the story. The communications department has previously stated plainly that Icasa, the industry regulator, had not actually received a formal licence application from Starlink despite years of public back-and-forth over the equity question, a gap between political rhetoric and regulatory paperwork that frustrated officials on both sides of the debate. Only in the middle of this year did Icasa publish the specific conditions it expects any satellite operator, Starlink included, to satisfy, covering matters from spectrum coordination to local presence requirements.

For ordinary South Africans, particularly in rural areas where fixed broadband infrastructure remains patchy or non-existent, the practical stakes of this regulatory saga are considerable. Starlink's satellite terminals have already found their way into farms, lodges and rural schools across the country through informal channels, often imported via neighbouring states where the service is formally licensed, a workaround that leaves users without consumer protections or guaranteed service continuity should regulators ever move to enforce the law more strictly.

Whether this round of regulatory clarity finally produces a licensed, above-ground Starlink operation in South Africa, or simply another chapter in a years-long standoff, depends on steps neither the company nor the regulator has yet confirmed publicly: a completed application, a demonstrated equity-equivalent investment plan, and Icasa's sign-off against the conditions it has now put in writing. Industry watchers who have followed the saga closely say they have learned not to predict a timeline, given how many previous milestones in this process have turned out to be less final than they appeared at the time.
