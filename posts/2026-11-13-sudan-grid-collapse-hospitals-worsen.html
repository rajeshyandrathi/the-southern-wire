Fifteen substations down since October: Sudan's power collapse is now a hospital crisis with no fix in sight

Politics Desk

Sudan's electricity network, already the most battered piece of infrastructure in a war that has flattened almost everything else, has spent the past six weeks absorbing a campaign of drone strikes that Sudanese officials and independent monitors alike blame on the Rapid Support Forces. Rights monitors tracking the damage because the state itself can no longer keep up count fifteen substations hit since early October. Two entire states, White Nile and North Kordofan, have been severed from the national grid outright. A regional network director's own assessment is blunt: the damage will be felt for months.

What makes this phase of the war different is where the strikes are landing. Earlier bombardments targeted generating capacity — the plants themselves. The newer wave has gone after transformer stations and distribution hubs, the unglamorous infrastructure that moves power from source to street, which means even areas nowhere near the front lines are losing electricity because the node routing their supply sits in a drone's flight path. Khartoum's own control centre, destroyed earlier in the war, has left what remains of the grid being managed by improvisation rather than design, so each new strike doesn't just cut power locally — it makes the whole network harder to run.

Hospitals are absorbing the worst of it in ways that rarely make it into casualty counts. Sudanese doctors' groups have warned specifically about dialysis units, blood banks and the cold-chain storage that keeps vaccines and medicines usable — all of them dependent on electricity that is no longer reliable even where generators exist, because fuel to run those generators is itself rationed and scarce. A dialysis machine that loses power mid-treatment is not an abstraction; it is a patient. Markets and bakeries going dark compound a food crisis that was already severe before a single substation was hit this autumn.

The attribution fight running alongside the physical damage tells its own story about how unaccountable this war has become. Sudan's government and a chorus of rights groups point at the RSF and, beyond it, at foreign backers — Khartoum's foreign ministry has specifically accused the United Arab Emirates of supplying the drones and alleged they were launched from Ethiopian territory. The UAE has repeatedly denied arming the RSF. An RSF source dismissed the targeting allegations as army propaganda. None of the parties doing the accusing has produced anything resembling independent verification, which means the strikes keep happening in a fog where nobody is made to answer for them.

What's left is a country watching its own infrastructure disappear in real time, with no credible process to stop it and a reconstruction bill that grows every week the drones keep flying. Proposals for solar capacity in Omdurman, Dongola and El Fasher have circulated for months without a single plant materialising — unsurprising, given that two of those three cities sit inside or beside active conflict zones. Sudan's war has already produced one of the world's largest displacement crises; a grid in terminal decline, with hospitals first in line to feel it, is the kind of slow-motion catastrophe that rarely gets the attention its scale deserves.
