The AKA murder trial grinds on, one adjournment at a time

Crime Desk·Monday, 2 November 2026

Kiernan "AKA" Forbes was one of South African hip-hop's defining voices when he was gunned down outside a Durban restaurant in February 2023, alongside his friend Tebello "Tibz" Motsoane. Nearly four years on, the trial of the seven men accused in connection with the double killing is still working through the kind of procedural grind that has become painfully familiar to anyone who has followed a major South African murder case through its pre-verdict years.

The most recent sitting was adjourned until the following Monday so the defence could properly prepare to cross-examine a car-tracking expert, whose evidence is expected to speak to the movements of vehicles connected to the night of the killing. It is exactly the kind of granular, unglamorous evidence that rarely makes headlines on its own but often ends up mattering enormously by the time a case reaches its conclusion — and exactly the kind of evidence that takes real time to pick apart properly, which is precisely why the defence asked for the extra week.

This isn't the first time the Durban High Court has had to pause proceedings for reasons that have nothing to do with the substance of the case. An earlier session was cut short entirely by extreme heat inside the courtroom, forcing a halt to testimony that had nothing to do with legal argument and everything to do with the physical conditions of an aging court building struggling to cope with a Durban summer. For a trial already stretched thin by its sheer scale — seven accused, years of investigation, and a case that has drawn national attention from the moment Forbes was killed — even the building itself has become an obstacle to progress.

None of this is unusual by the standards of complex, multi-accused South African murder trials, where procedural delay is less an aberration than a structural feature of a court system handling cases with this many moving parts. But unusual or not, the accumulated effect is the same for everyone with a stake in the outcome: Forbes' and Motsoane's families, the accused themselves, and a public that has waited years for a case this high-profile to reach any kind of resolution.

Barring further delay, cross-examination of the car-tracking expert resumes next week — one more incremental step in a trial that has, so far, measured its progress in such steps rather than in any decisive breakthrough.
