A new award show wants to honour the people behind African music who never get called on stage

Lifestyle Desk

The inaugural Music Business Awards Africa will be held at Mercedes-Benz Sandton in Johannesburg on 21 November, built around a premise that distinguishes it from almost every existing music award ceremony on the continent: it exists to recognise the managers, distributors, publishers and label executives whose work underpins an artist's career, rather than the artists themselves. For an industry whose awards shows have historically trained every spotlight on performers, the new ceremony is a pointed acknowledgement that none of those careers happen without a largely invisible layer of business infrastructure behind them.

The timing situates the ceremony inside a broader, unusually dense stretch of recognition events for South African and African music this November, from a biographical documentary premiering at a Johannesburg film festival later in the month to a separate awards show focused specifically on AI applications in music set for Cape Town at month's end. Taken together, the cluster suggests an industry actively building out categories of recognition that simply didn't exist in a South African context a few years ago, rather than continuing to rely solely on artist-focused ceremonies like the long-running South African Music Awards.

For the managers, A&R executives, distributors and label heads the new ceremony is designed around, the symbolic value of a dedicated awards platform may matter as much as the trophies themselves. Music industry professionals in these roles have long described a career path with little public visibility and correspondingly limited leverage when negotiating their own value within an industry that still measures success almost entirely through an artist's chart performance or streaming numbers, metrics that say little about the business decisions and relationships that made those numbers possible.

Whether the ceremony achieves the kind of lasting industry weight its organisers are clearly aiming for will depend on who shows up, both as nominees and in the room, and whether the inaugural event can establish credibility quickly enough to attract sponsorship and media attention beyond a first-year novelty cycle. African music industry events have a mixed record on that front, with some new ceremonies fading within a few editions while others, built around a genuinely underserved niche, have found durable audiences.

For now, the Sandton ceremony represents a bet that the business side of African music is ready for its own moment of public recognition, separate from and complementary to the artist-focused platforms that have always existed. If it succeeds, it may do more to professionalise perceptions of the industry's back office than any single artist's chart success could.
