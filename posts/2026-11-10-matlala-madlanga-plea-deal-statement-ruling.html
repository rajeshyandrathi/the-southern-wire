Cat Matlala's commission testimony collides with the plea deal he thought he'd buried

Crime Desk

Fezekile "Cat" Matlala has spent the past two days in a witness chair he wasn't supposed to occupy for another five weeks. The tender businessman at the centre of South Africa's police-corruption inquiry was recalled to testify before the Madlanga Commission on Monday and Tuesday, moved up from his original slot after his guilty-plea arrangement in the separate attempted-murder case against him collapsed back in June. That acceleration has now produced a legal flashpoint that has nothing to do with the assassination charges he still faces, and everything to do with what he said while trying to make them go away.

On Monday, Commission chair Justice Mbuyiseli Madlanga ruled against Matlala's own legal team on a question that goes to the heart of how plea negotiations are supposed to work: whether a statement he gave to prosecutors as part of that now-dead plea deal can be put to him and heard in the Commission's proceedings. Madlanga found that it can. For Matlala's lawyers, that ruling undoes the basic premise under which such statements are normally made — in confidence, with the understanding that they vanish if the deal falls through — and they argued in submissions that using it against him here amounts, in their words, to a threat to his life.

The objection is not merely tactical. A plea statement is typically the most candid account an accused person will ever give, offered precisely because its holder believes it will never resurface outside the deal it was meant to secure. Matlala's team's position is that allowing the Commission to hear it regardless, after prosecutors walked away from the agreement it was tied to, strips away the protection that makes any future accused willing to negotiate in good faith — and, they contend, exposes their client to real danger given who and what his testimony touches.

Madlanga's response was narrower than an outright rejection of that concern. The Commission will proceed with Matlala's testimony, he indicated, but will halt immediately if his lawyers succeed in obtaining an interdict from the High Court. That leaves the fight to move to a different forum entirely: Matlala's legal team has signalled it will take the ruling on urgent review, meaning a Johannesburg or Pretoria judge, rather than the Commission itself, may end up deciding within days whether the plea statement stays in play.

The timing compounds the pressure on Matlala from multiple directions at once. The attempted-murder trial that produced the collapsed plea deal in the first place — the one in which forensic pathologist Professor Gert Saayman has been testifying over claims that co-accused Musa Kekana was assaulted into a confession — did not sit on Monday and has been postponed to Thursday, 12 November. That means Matlala is currently fighting a procedural battle over his own words at the Commission while the criminal case those words were meant to resolve sits paused just three days away from resuming.

Whichever way the High Court rules, the stakes extend well past Matlala's own case. The Madlanga Commission exists to establish how deep political interference reached into South African policing, and a ruling that plea-deal statements are fair game once a deal breaks down would reshape how every future accused in that inquiry — and in prosecutions more broadly — thinks about cooperating at all.
