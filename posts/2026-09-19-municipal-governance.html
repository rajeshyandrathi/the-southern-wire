Governance strains surface across municipalities

Politics Desk·Saturday, 19 September 2026

Zoom out far enough from any single municipal scandal in South Africa and you start to see the same shape repeating itself, metro after metro, year after year: a serious problem surfaces, an accountability process is triggered, and then that process grinds along so slowly that by the time it concludes — if it ever concludes — the original scandal has been overtaken by two or three new ones. This week's crop of local government stories is a near-perfect illustration of that pattern, playing out simultaneously in three different provinces as though scripted to make the same point three times over.

Start in Ekurhuleni, where the municipal manager — one of the metro's most senior administrative officials, the person theoretically responsible for keeping the machinery of local government running — has been dismissed after being implicated in multiple criminal cases. This isn't a minor administrative reshuffle. Removing a municipal manager mid-term is disruptive by design; it signals that whatever was happening was serious enough to outweigh the very real costs of leadership instability in a metro of Ekurhuleni's size and complexity. And yet dismissal, satisfying as it might look on paper, doesn't answer the harder question of how someone facing multiple criminal implications held that position for as long as they did in the first place.

Head south to Nelson Mandela Bay and the picture gets murkier, not clearer. There, a suspended city manager continues to draw a salary understood to run into the millions of rand, month after month, while the disciplinary process meant to resolve his status sits stalled — not because the underlying allegations have been resolved, but for reasons that appear to be almost entirely procedural. This is the part of municipal governance that erodes public trust fastest: not the existence of allegations, which happen everywhere, but the sight of a suspension mechanism that seems built to protract rather than resolve, quietly bleeding ratepayer money into a limbo that serves nobody except, perhaps, the suspended official still collecting a paycheck. Unsurprisingly, ratepayers in the metro have grown louder and angrier about exactly this dynamic, and it's not hard to see why.

Then there's the Eastern Cape, where the friction isn't administrative but political. The ANC's provincial secretary has threatened legal action over how the party's candidate list was compiled — a dispute that, on its face, is an internal party matter, but one that carries real consequences for how the province positions itself ahead of future local contests. Candidate list disputes have a way of metastasizing quickly in South African party politics; what starts as an internal grievance about process can within weeks become a full-blown factional battle that distracts an entire provincial structure from the actual business of governing.

None of these three stories, taken in isolation, would be big enough to dominate a national news cycle on its own. A dismissal here, a stalled disciplinary process there, a candidate list dispute somewhere else — this is the unglamorous, granular texture of South African local government, the kind of story that rarely makes a splash but never really stops happening either. That, in a sense, is exactly the point. It's the accumulation that matters, not any single incident.

Taken together, these stories describe something more structural than a bad week for local government: a pattern in which the actual mechanisms designed to hold officials accountable — disciplinary hearings, internal party processes, oversight committees, criminal investigations — consistently move slower than the problems they're meant to address. It's not that South African local government lacks accountability structures on paper. It's that those structures, in practice, seem engineered — whether by design or by inertia — to outlast public attention rather than resolve the underlying dysfunction. Until that changes, expect more weeks that look exactly like this one.
