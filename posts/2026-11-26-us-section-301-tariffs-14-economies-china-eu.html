US proposes new tariffs of up to 35% on 14 economies, from China to the EU

Washington wants to replace the tariffs the Supreme Court struck down with new duties on most of Asia, the EU and Switzerland. Tech stocks slid as the plan landed on the same day as hotter US inflation data.

World Desk·Thursday, 26 November 2026·4 min read

Key points

  • The US Trade Representative proposed extra tariffs on 14 economies on Wednesday after investigations into “structural excess capacity” in manufacturing
  • Proposed rates range from 15% for the EU, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Switzerland to up to 35% for Vietnam
  • The duties are seen as a replacement for the tariffs the US Supreme Court struck down in February; South Africa is not on the list
  • Comments are due by 5 January 2027 and hearings start on 25 January, so nothing takes effect immediately
  • Nvidia and other chip stocks fell sharply as the plan landed alongside hotter US inflation data

The United States has proposed a new round of tariffs on 14 of its trading partners, including China, the European Union, Japan, India and Taiwan. The Office of the US Trade Representative (USTR) announced the plan on Wednesday, 25 November, at the end of investigations that began in March into what it calls “structural excess capacity” in manufacturing.

The 14 economies are Bangladesh, Cambodia, China, the EU, India, Indonesia, Japan, South Korea, Malaysia, Singapore, Switzerland, Taiwan, Thailand and Vietnam. Two others that were investigated, Mexico and Norway, were left out. South Africa was not part of the investigations.

Trade lawyers at Baker McKenzie say the new duties are widely seen as a way to replace the emergency tariffs the US Supreme Court ruled unlawful in February 2026, along with the temporary tariffs imposed after that ruling. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said many trading partners had built industrial strategies aimed at manufacturing, and that the action could “level the playing field for American workers”.

Who would pay what

According to the USTR, the EU would face a 15% duty, and Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Switzerland up to 15%. Bangladesh, Cambodia, Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand face up to 18%, Singapore up to 20% and India up to 25%. Vietnam faces the highest proposed rate, up to 35%. Some goods are exempt, and several countries could see lower rates if they sign trade agreements with Washington: India’s could fall to 17%, Vietnam’s to 20% and Singapore’s to 10%.

For China, the USTR proposes up to 20% on most goods and up to 10% on a separate list, with some products exempt. Baker McKenzie notes that the current US-China trade truce runs until 10 January 2027. China’s foreign ministry rejected the overcapacity argument as “a false issue” but did not threaten to retaliate, The Next Web reported. A senior US official said Washington was “seeking stability” with Beijing.

The Next Web also reported that the US singled out Brazil, where trade talks have stalled, with higher tariffs on sugar and a new 10% duty on aircraft that would hit the planemaker Embraer. None of the tariffs are final. Written comments are due by 5 January 2027, public hearings start on 25 January, and a final decision is expected early next year.

Markets take fright

The announcement landed on a nervous day for markets. New figures showed the Personal Consumption Expenditures price index, a key US inflation measure, rose 3.3% in October from 3.1% the month before, and some analysts now expect the Federal Reserve to raise interest rates in December, Fast Company reported.

Technology shares took the biggest hit. Yahoo Finance reported that the Nasdaq fell more than 4% at one point and Nvidia dropped nearly 9%, wiping out much of the rally that followed Jensen Huang’s return as chief executive. Memory chip makers Micron and SK Hynix each fell about 10%. Buyers came back later in the day, and by the afternoon Fast Company reported the Nasdaq was down less than 1% and Nvidia about 5%.

One portfolio manager told Fast Company that the tariffs could make US-designed chips made abroad more expensive to bring into the country, which helps explain why chip stocks came under pressure. Asian markets also fell sharply on Wednesday, and MSCI’s global share index was down 0.5%, according to The Next Web.
