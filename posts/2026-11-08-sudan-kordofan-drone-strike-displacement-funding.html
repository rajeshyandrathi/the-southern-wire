A drone strike on a Kordofan fuel station adds to Sudan's toll, as the UN warns displaced millions could lose their shelters entirely

World Desk

A suspected drone strike on a fuel station in South Kordofan killed at least seven people, the latest in a string of attacks that have extended Sudan's civil war into territory beyond the Darfur region most associated with the conflict's worst atrocities. The strike bore the hallmarks of the drone warfare that has become one of the defining features of the war between the Sudanese army and the Rapid Support Forces, a capability that has allowed both sides to strike targets well beyond active front lines with a precision, and a civilian cost, that ground fighting alone rarely produced.

The attack lands alongside a starker warning from the United Nations, which says funding shortfalls now threaten the basic shelter that millions of displaced Sudanese depend on. Aid officials describe a crisis in which the emergency has run long enough, and drawn attention thin enough across competing global conflicts, that even the tents and temporary structures housing displaced families are now at risk of going unfunded, a threshold that aid organisations say would mark a significant deterioration from merely inadequate aid to no shelter at all for some of the war's most vulnerable survivors.

South Kordofan's drawing into this pattern of strikes matters because it signals the conflict's geography continuing to widen rather than consolidating around the areas, chiefly Darfur, that have dominated international coverage. Displacement data has already concentrated overwhelmingly in Darfur, and a war that keeps opening new fronts in regions like Kordofan complicates humanitarian planning that has, out of necessity, been built around where the crisis has been worst rather than where it might spread next.

The combination of drought, drone warfare and state repression that has characterised Darfur's crisis for much of this year is now being echoed, in smaller scale, in Kordofan, raising the question of whether aid organisations already stretched thin in one region can realistically absorb a second zone of comparable need without a significant increase in funding that donor fatigue has made increasingly difficult to secure.

For displaced families already surviving on inadequate aid, the UN's shelter warning represents a particularly stark marker of how close the response to this war is to breaking down entirely. A conflict that international attention has struggled to sustain interest in for over two years now risks compounding military escalation with a humanitarian funding collapse at precisely the moment the fighting's geography is expanding rather than narrowing.
