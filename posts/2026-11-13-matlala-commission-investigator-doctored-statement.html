Crime & Courts

Cat Matlala accuses his own investigator of rewriting the statement that was supposed to convict him

The Southern Wire Desk·Johannesburg

For most of this year, the Madlanga Commission's engagement with Vusimuzi "Cat" Matlala has followed a predictable shape: investigators press him on his links to serving police officers, and his lawyers push back on process. This week, in testimony commissioners themselves seemed unprepared for, Matlala flipped that dynamic entirely. He told the commission that an IDAC investigator repeatedly altered his Section 204 statement — the document in which an accused person agrees to testify against others in exchange for consideration — and inserted allegations implicating senior police officials that Matlala insists he never made in the first place.

The claim, if it holds up, is not a minor procedural quibble. A Section 204 statement is meant to be the accused's own account, offered voluntarily in exchange for a measure of protection from prosecution; if an investigator is shown to have shaped its contents rather than simply recorded them, it doesn't just taint Matlala's statement, it threatens every case built on the assumption that the document reflects what he actually said. Matlala's lawyers appear to have recognised the opportunity this presents and leaned into it hard, following up by demanding that the investigator's own notes from a separate, allegedly confidential meeting with the commission's evidence leaders be struck from the record entirely.

The commission has not made this easy for him. It has already rejected one attempt by Matlala's legal team to block questioning over his plea-agreement documents, ruling that using them did not bring the administration of justice into disrepute, the formal standard his lawyers had invoked to keep the material out. That ruling matters here because it signals the commission is willing to let uncomfortable material through rather than shield witnesses from their own paper trail — a posture that now has the investigator's conduct under equally public scrutiny.

What makes this moment genuinely combustible is that Matlala is not merely a cooperating witness; he is simultaneously standing trial on attempted murder charges in a separate Johannesburg courtroom, with that trial having resumed earlier this month after its own recess. Every word he gives the commission about police misconduct is being weighed against the fact that his incentive to discredit the officials building a case against him has never been stronger. Commissioners know this. It is precisely why his allegation cannot simply be waved away as a defendant's desperation, nor can it be accepted at face value — it has to be tested against whatever documentary trail exists of the statement's drafting.

The commission is working against its own deadline too: its final report to President Cyril Ramaphosa is due within days, on 16 November, leaving vanishingly little room to resolve a dispute this serious through anything but a rushed finding or a deferral that undercuts the report's credibility before it is even delivered. Whichever way the commission rules, it will shape how seriously anyone treats Matlala's broader testimony about police capture — testimony the whole commission was convened to take seriously in the first place.
