South Africa cracks the world's top 10 food destinations, and Cape Town's kitchens are the reason why

Lifestyle Desk

South Africa has been named among the world's top ten countries for food in Condé Nast Traveller's latest Readers' Choice Awards, a ranking built directly from the votes of the magazine's international readership rather than a panel of critics, which gives the result a different kind of weight than a typical restaurant guide accolade. The same reader poll singled out Cape Town's restaurant scene specifically, praising both the atmosphere and the cooking on offer in a city that has spent years building a reputation well beyond its original calling card of scenic backdrops and wine-country day trips.

A separate ranking from Time Out's own readers' poll reinforced the message, naming South Africa the only African country to feature on its list of the world's best food destinations. Taken together, the two results suggest the country's culinary reputation has moved past the stage of being a pleasant surprise for first-time visitors and into something closer to an established, globally recognised selling point, one that tourism bodies are increasingly leaning on in international marketing campaigns.

What the rankings do not fully capture is how much the domestic dining scene has been quietly reshaping itself at the same time it has been winning international praise. Industry reporting on local dining trends has pointed to a shift toward authenticity and affordability, as South African consumers navigate a tougher economic climate and increasingly favour restaurants built around honest, well-executed regional cooking over the more formal, imported fine-dining formats that once dominated the country's upper tier. That shift, if anything, may be helping rather than hurting the country's international standing, since the dishes most likely to impress an international reader's poll are often the distinctly local ones rather than a city's attempt to replicate European fine dining.

Cape Town's restaurants have been particularly well positioned to benefit from that tension between affordability and ambition, drawing on the Cape's wine country, the city's Cape Malay and Cape Dutch culinary heritage, and a coastline that keeps seafood genuinely fresh rather than merely marketed as such. International chefs and food writers who visit the city consistently point to this combination of accessible pricing relative to comparable global food capitals and genuinely distinctive local flavour as the thing that keeps drawing them back for repeat coverage.

For the broader tourism sector, a ranking like this one lands at a useful moment, just ahead of the peak summer season and the city's first edition of the international Taste Festival later this month. Whether the Condé Nast recognition translates into a measurable bump in food-focused travel bookings is something tourism bodies will only be able to assess once the summer season's numbers are in, but for an industry that has spent years trying to convince international visitors that South African food deserves its own travel itinerary rather than a side note to safaris and scenery, the validation itself is being treated as a win in its own right.
