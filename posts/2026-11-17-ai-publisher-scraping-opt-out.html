The world's biggest AI companies just agreed on one thing: publishers can say no

Tech Desk

For an industry that spends most of its public life fighting over model architecture, training data and whose chatbot answers a question better, a rare moment of consensus has broken out. OpenAI, Google, Microsoft and Anthropic have each backed a shared technical signal that lets website publishers opt out of having their content scraped and used to train AI models — a standardised, machine-readable way of saying no that, if it works as described, replaces today's patchwork of robots.txt conventions and legal threats with something closer to an actual industry norm.

Anthropic is already honouring the signal in full, according to the companies' own disclosures, putting the company that just filed for a Nasdaq listing north of a trillion dollars in the position of being the most compliant big lab on an issue that has generated lawsuits against nearly every other major AI developer. OpenAI has committed to implementing the signal within the coming months rather than immediately, while Google and Microsoft have both indicated they expect to follow suit sometime next year — a staggered rollout that leaves publishers waiting on exactly the companies with the largest existing scraping footprints.

The timing is not coincidental. Publishers, news organisations and authors have spent the past three years filing an escalating wave of litigation against AI developers over training data scraped without permission or payment, arguments that have forced courts in multiple jurisdictions to grapple with questions copyright law was never written to answer. A credible, shared opt-out mechanism gives the AI industry something to point to in those cases: evidence of good-faith technical cooperation that might, if nothing else, soften the sharpest edges of the legal exposure still working through the courts.

Whether the signal actually protects anyone in practice depends on enforcement details that remain thin in what has been disclosed so far. A standard that major labs can choose to honour, on their own announced timeline, is a meaningfully weaker commitment than one with external verification or penalties attached. Smaller AI developers and the growing ecosystem of scrapers that feed open datasets used across the industry are under no obligation to respect a signal the biggest four companies adopted voluntarily, which means publishers opting out of Anthropic's crawlers may still find their content absorbed into a dataset two steps removed from any of the four firms making this announcement.

Still, a shared signal that even four of the industry's largest players have agreed to honour is a genuine first for a sector that has, until now, treated training data acquisition as a competitive advantage worth fighting for rather than a practice worth standardising. For publishers who have spent years with no consistent way to even register an objection, having one default mechanism recognised across the industry's biggest names is progress, even if it is progress measured in inches rather than the mile many of them were hoping for.
