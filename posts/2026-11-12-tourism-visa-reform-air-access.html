South Africa wants 15 million visitors a year. Its own visa rules are the obstacle.

Lifestyle Desk

Tourism Minister Patricia de Lille has made air access and visa reform the centrepiece of her pitch for South Africa's travel ambitions, a push that has grown more urgent as the country sets its sights on 15 million international visitor arrivals a year by 2030. The numbers moving in the right direction, more than 4.2 million arrivals in the first five months of this year alone, a 12.8% increase, suggest the demand side of that target is achievable. Whether South Africa's own entry systems can keep up with that demand is a different question entirely.

The electronic travel authorisation system that Ramaphosa launched at OR Tambo International Airport earlier this year was meant to be part of the answer, digitising a visa process that has long frustrated travellers from markets South Africa is trying hardest to grow, particularly in Asia and parts of the Middle East where visa friction has historically pushed holidaymakers toward competing destinations with simpler entry requirements. Early feedback on the system's actual rollout has been mixed, with industry figures continuing to flag processing delays as a persistent bottleneck even after the new system went live.

Air access carries its own set of structural constraints that no visa reform alone can solve. New long-haul routes have strengthened connectivity into South Africa over the past year, but industry voices have continued warning that both airport capacity and visa bottlenecks need to be addressed together if the country wants to convert growing interest into the booked seats the 2030 target actually requires. A traveller deterred by a complicated visa process never becomes a passenger in the first place, no matter how many new routes an airline adds.

The international recognition has at least been flowing in the right direction. South African properties and destinations claimed multiple titles at the 2026 World Travel Awards Africa ceremony, a continental endorsement that tourism boards can use to market the country's lodges, reserves and coastal destinations to exactly the kind of high-value international travellers the 15 million target depends on. Awards alone will not move the needle on arrival numbers, but they do give the marketing push something concrete to point to.

What happens next depends less on any single announcement than on whether government departments responsible for different pieces of the puzzle, Home Affairs on visas, airports on capacity, tourism on marketing, can actually move in sync. South Africa has set an ambitious target and shown it can attract rising numbers of visitors when conditions allow. Converting that momentum into a sustained march toward 15 million arrivals will require exactly the kind of coordinated bureaucratic execution the country's tourism sector has struggled to see consistently delivered.
