Rivals splits its second season in two, and the gamble is paying off

Culture Desk

Disney and Netflix's glossy 1980s-set drama Rivals has returned for the back half of its second season this week, and the decision to split the run in two rather than dump every episode at once looks increasingly deliberate rather than cautious. Three new episodes landed together, with the remaining instalments following weekly, a release pattern that runs directly against the binge-everything model streaming built its reputation on, and one that only works if a show has already earned the kind of audience loyalty that will show up again next Wednesday.

Based on Jilly Cooper's novel of the same name, Rivals trades on a very specific pleasure: the spectacle of wealthy, glamorous people behaving appallingly to each other against a backdrop of English countryside excess, television rivalries and sexual intrigue that never quite tips into parody. The first season's success came less from prestige-drama ambitions than from leaning hard into that soapy register and refusing to apologise for it, a tonal confidence that is rarer in big-budget television than the genre's detractors tend to admit.

The weekly rollout format carries real commercial logic behind the creative choice. Staggered releases keep a show in the cultural conversation for weeks rather than the single frenzied weekend a full-season drop typically generates, giving marketing teams repeated opportunities to resurface the show in recommendation algorithms and social feeds. For a title competing against an increasingly crowded November slate of big streaming premieres, that sustained visibility matters more than it might have a few years ago when the format wars were still being fought on volume alone.

Whether the strategy pays off commercially will depend on whether casual viewers tolerate the wait, something streaming audiences trained on instant gratification have not always rewarded. Shows that have tried weekly drops before have had mixed results, succeeding when the material generates genuine week-to-week conversation and faltering when audiences simply lose momentum between episodes and quietly drift away before the finale.

For now, Rivals is betting that its particular brand of heightened melodrama, equal parts boardroom knife-fighting and bedroom scheming, generates exactly the kind of appointment viewing that rewards patience rather than punishing it. If the back half sustains the same energy that made the first season a surprise hit, the weekly format may end up looking less like a risk and more like the smartest decision the show's handlers made all year.
