COP31 has a question every climate summit dodges, and overshooting 1.5°C just made it louder

World Desk

COP31 is entering its decisive stretch in Antalya with negotiators racing toward a first draft of a final text, and the question that sank momentum at last year's summit in Belem has followed them to Turkey. The adaptation-finance gap, the unresolved fight over how much money wealthy nations owe poorer ones to cope with climate impacts they did little to cause, was passed to Antalya without an agreed text when June's preparatory talks in Bonn ran out of road. It remains exactly as unresolved now as it was then, and rich and poor negotiating blocs are no closer to a shared number than they were five months ago.

Layered on top of that familiar standoff is a newer, harder admission working its way into the summit's draft language: growing scientific consensus that the world has already overshot the 1.5°C warming threshold the Paris Agreement was built around. That is not a future risk being negotiated against anymore; it is closer to an accepted present fact, and it changes the emotional and political register of every adaptation-finance argument in the room. Money that was once framed as insurance against a future breach is now being argued over as compensation for a breach that has, in practical terms, already happened.

Fossil fuels remain the summit's other unfinished business. Last year's COP30 text in Belem made no mention of a fossil fuel phase-out, with the presidency instead promising that roadmaps for fossil fuels and deforestation would be developed through processes outside the formal UN climate process altogether, effectively punting the hardest political fight to another year. Antalya's presidency has signalled its draft text will grapple with the transition away from fossil fuels more directly, though previous summits have made similar signals before producing texts that quietly walked them back under pressure from oil and gas-producing delegations.

The summit's own governance arrangement adds a layer of complexity rarely seen at this scale: Australia is leading the core negotiations while Turkey, as host, leads and manages the broader action agenda, a split-leadership structure born out of an earlier hosting dispute between the two countries. Whether that arrangement produces sharper focus or simply more coordination overhead is one of the quieter storylines diplomats in Antalya are watching alongside the headline finance fight.

What "overshoot" changes in the negotiating room

The shift from treating 1.5°C as a line not yet crossed to one already breached is not merely semantic inside a negotiating hall; it restructures the entire argument developing nations bring to the table. A future-tense warning, framed as prevention, invites a different kind of diplomatic response than a present-tense admission of harm already done. Negotiators representing climate-vulnerable nations have increasingly shifted their language accordingly, moving away from appeals to avert a future crisis and toward demands that acknowledge an ongoing one, a rhetorical change with real consequences for how finance commitments get framed, justified and, crucially, enforced once a text is agreed. Wealthy nations that might once have offered adaptation finance as forward-looking insurance now face arguments framed closer to liability for damage already measurable on the ground, a harder position to negotiate down from without appearing to deny the underlying science.

That reframing also sharpens an older and more technical argument inside the UN climate process: the distinction between adaptation finance, money spent helping vulnerable nations cope with climate impacts already underway, and the separate, even more contested category of loss-and-damage finance, compensation for harm that adaptation measures arrived too late, or with too little funding, to prevent. COP27's landmark agreement to establish a loss-and-damage fund was itself hard-won, and Antalya's negotiators are working against the backdrop of that fund's own slow, underfunded rollout even as the overshoot admission adds fresh urgency to arguments that it needs far more capital than it has so far received.

The fossil fuel question nobody wants to own

Belem's decision to push fossil fuel and deforestation roadmaps outside the formal UN process was, by most accounts from observers who tracked that summit, less a resolution than a deferral dressed up as progress: a way to avoid a direct confrontation between oil and gas-producing nations and the countries pushing hardest for a phase-out commitment, without either side having to accept defeat in the official text. Whether Antalya's presidency genuinely intends to reopen that fight, as its early signalling suggests, or whether this round of signals meets the same fate as Belem's once oil and gas-producing delegations apply pressure behind closed doors, is likely to become clear only in the summit's final, most contentious days, when draft language typically gets tested hardest against exactly the blocs most invested in watering it down.

For African nations, including South Africa, the adaptation-finance question is not an abstraction to be debated from a comfortable distance. With 2026 on track to rank among the warmest years on record, the money at stake in Antalya's unresolved finance fight is the same money that funds drought response, flood infrastructure and agricultural resilience across a continent that contributed the least to the emissions now overshooting the threshold the talks were meant to prevent breaching in the first place.
