The industry that argued about The Polygamist all year now has to vote on it

Culture Desk

Tonight the argument gets a scoreboard. For months, The Polygamist has been less a television drama than a referendum — on modern marriage, on what patriarchy looks like when it wears a wedding ring, on whether a story can dramatize something without endorsing it. Stained Glass Productions built a hit nobody could watch quietly, and at this year's National Film & TV Awards it arrives as the clear frontrunner, carrying nominations across Best Scripted TV Series, Best TV Drama Series and the acting categories built around its combustible central performances.

Sdumo Mtshali's nomination for Best Actor in a TV Series sits at the center of that case. His performance has been the engine of every one of those dinner-table arguments — not because the character is likeable, but because he isn't, and audiences kept watching anyway. That is the harder trick in actable television, and it's the one the National Film & TV Awards exist to notice: not who played the easiest role best, but who made an unbearable one unmissable.

The Polygamist isn't walking into tonight unchallenged. Skeem Saam's fifteenth season picked up three nominations of its own, a reminder that the awards were built to reward longevity and craft as much as controversy — a soapie that has run for over a decade competing for recognition against a freshman drama that arrived mid-debate and never left it.

What makes tonight different from the nomination announcement a month ago is that nominations only measure conversation. Wins measure consensus — and consensus is exactly what The Polygamist has never had. Audiences split hard on whether the show was a serious examination of coercive relationships or a glossy excuse to film them. A win tonight doesn't resolve that argument, it just puts a trophy in the middle of one.

That's the bet the industry is making by nominating it so heavily in the first place: that the show that made people the most uncomfortable this year deserves the most credit, not the least. By the time the ceremony wraps, South Africa will know whether The Polygamist's year of arguments ends in a trophy case or an asterisk. Either way, the show has already done the one thing hardest to fake in this industry: it made an entire country keep talking about television it couldn't stop arguing about.
