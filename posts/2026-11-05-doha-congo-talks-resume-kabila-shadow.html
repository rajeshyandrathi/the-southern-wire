Doha talks resume on Congo's war, but Kabila's shadow leaves little room for a breakthrough

Politics Desk

Delegations from Kinshasa and the Rwandan-backed M23 alliance have resumed talks in Doha this week, the latest round in a Qatari-mediated process that has lurched from breakthrough to breakdown and back again since it began. The two sides have already produced one signed framework for peace in the mineral-rich east of the Democratic Republic of Congo, only to see a follow-up signing ceremony collapse when Kinshasa's delegation failed to appear, and to watch violence flare again in the interim on the ground the framework was meant to quiet.

The talks resume this time with a complication that did not exist when the process began: the formal alignment of former president Joseph Kabila with the rebel side. Kabila, who ruled Congo for 18 years before being succeeded by Félix Tshisekedi and who has spent the years since in an increasingly open rupture with his former protégé, has lent the M23-led alliance something it lacked at the negotiating table, a figure with genuine standing inside Congo's political establishment and a claim, however contested, to represent an alternative to the current government. For Kinshasa, that makes any concession at Doha look less like a deal with a foreign-backed militia and more like a deal with a domestic rival, a far harder sell to a Congolese public already furious over the loss of territory in the east.

Qatar's mediators, who have invested significant diplomatic capital and the personal involvement of the emir in keeping the process alive, are working against a backdrop that keeps undercutting their own momentum. Humanitarian groups operating in the contested eastern provinces continue to report displacement and clashes even as negotiators sit down hundreds of kilometres away, the same pattern that scuttled the earlier signing ceremony and that threatens to do so again if a durable ceasefire mechanism cannot be agreed this round.

For the wider region, the stakes extend well beyond Congo's borders. Rwanda's alleged backing for M23 has strained its relations with much of the Southern African Development Community, and a credible peace deal brokered in Doha rather than through African Union or SADC channels would be a quiet rebuke to the continent's own conflict-resolution architecture. A collapse, conversely, would leave the eastern DRC's war, already one of the deadliest and least-covered conflicts in the world, grinding on with no clear diplomatic path left to try.

Negotiators from both sides have given no public timeline for this round of talks, and officials close to the process say expectations have been deliberately lowered after the false dawn of the aborted signing. What diplomats are quietly hoping for is something narrower than a final settlement: a workable arrangement on prisoner exchanges and humanitarian access that could rebuild enough trust to make a fuller peace framework worth attempting again.
