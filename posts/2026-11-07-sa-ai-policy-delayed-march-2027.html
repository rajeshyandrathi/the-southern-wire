South Africa's national AI policy slips again, even as a Finnish supercomputer offers a shortcut around the compute gap

Tech Desk

South Africa's long-promised national artificial intelligence policy has missed another deadline, with the redrafted framework now not expected to reach Cabinet until March 2027, months later than officials had originally promised when the process first began in earnest last year. For a government that has repeatedly described AI readiness as an economic imperative, the repeated slippage has become something of an embarrassment, and a reminder of how far policy machinery in Pretoria still lags the pace at which the technology itself is moving.

The policy's troubled history helps explain the latest delay. Communications Minister Solly Malatsi spent much of the first half of this year trying to rescue an earlier, heavily criticised draft, before a revised version was approved for publication for public comment in April. Officials working on the policy are said to be targeting a 30 November deadline for finalising input from that comment process, after which the document still has to clear internal government review before it can go anywhere near Cabinet, a sequence of steps that has repeatedly proven slower in practice than on paper.

While the policy framework stalls, the more concrete problem it is meant to address, South Africa's lack of domestic computing capacity for training and running AI models, has not waited for government to catch up. A proposed partnership involving a Finnish supercomputer has emerged as one potential shortcut, offering South African researchers and companies access to high-performance computing infrastructure without the years and billions of rand it would take to build equivalent capacity domestically. For a country that has watched global AI investment concentrate overwhelmingly in the hands of a handful of well-capitalised nations and companies, external compute partnerships of this kind are increasingly being treated as a pragmatic necessity rather than a stopgap.

The stakes of getting this right are not merely academic. Independent surveys have tracked a steady rise in AI adoption among South African adults, and separate data from Discovery Bank found that a significant share of its Visa clients were already paying for chatbot access in 2025, evidence that consumer appetite for the technology is running well ahead of the regulatory and infrastructure environment meant to support it responsibly. Industry bodies have repeatedly warned that without a coherent national strategy, the benefits of that adoption curve risk flowing mostly to foreign platforms rather than building any domestic capability.

The inaugural South African AI Awards, scheduled for this month in Johannesburg, will offer an early test of how the local industry feels about its own progress in the absence of policy certainty. Organisers and entrants alike say they are proceeding on the assumption that clearer rules will eventually arrive; what they cannot yet say with any confidence is when.
