South African markets are sitting near their best levels in years, election uncertainty or not

Business Desk

For all the uncertainty swirling around South Africa's municipal election count this week, traders in Johannesburg and offshore have treated the result as background noise rather than a reason to reprice local assets. That calm only really makes sense in the context of the year the local market has already had: the JSE's benchmark All Share Index posted a 38 percent gain in 2025, its best calendar year since 2005, and the rally has carried through into 2026 with enough force that a messy, multi-metro coalition negotiation has barely registered on the charts.

The engine behind that run has been a familiar South African combination: a stronger rand and a boom in mining and precious-metals prices, working together rather than against each other for once. The rand's roughly 14 percent gain against the dollar through 2025 turned the JSE's already strong local-currency performance into a 57 percent advance in dollar terms, a figure that caught the attention of global emerging-market fund managers who had largely written South African equities off as a value trap in prior years. By January, the index's total market capitalisation had pushed above R8.17 trillion, its highest level since 2019, with Bloomberg-sourced coverage attributing the move directly to rand strength and soaring metal prices.

The rand itself has had a genuinely strong run by its own recent history. It moved below R16 to the dollar for the first time since 2022 at the peak of the rally, a psychologically significant level for a currency that spent much of the preceding three years trading in the high teens. By August, it was holding near R16.02, with gold trading above $4,600 an ounce, a level that has kept South African gold and platinum producers reporting some of their strongest earnings in years and kept paying through into the broader equity market via dividends and reinvestment.

Whether that strength has staying power is a genuinely open question among the analysts who track it closely. One widely quoted view going into the year was that the rand could plausibly strengthen further, potentially testing a "15 handle" against the dollar, while more cautious voices have noted that the entire rally rests on two legs, the metals boom and the rand's own momentum, that are not obviously permanent features of the local economy. A sustained downturn in global metals prices, or a reversal in the rand driven by local political instability, would test how much of the market's current confidence is structural versus simply a favourable run of global conditions.

That tension is exactly why this week's election outcome matters more to the medium-term picture than the market's muted reaction suggests. A genuinely dysfunctional set of coalition governments in Johannesburg and Tshwane would, over months rather than days, feed into service-delivery and investment-confidence metrics that eventually show up in how foreign capital treats South African risk. For now, though, the rally that began with metals and currency strength has given local markets enough of a cushion that even a fractured municipal vote has not been enough to knock it off course.
