Sudan’s army rejects Ethiopia’s offer of peace talks as drones kill 19 in Blue Nile

Drone attacks on Damazin and Roseires killed at least 19 people overnight. A day later the army-aligned government turned down Abiy Ahmed’s offer to host talks, saying Ethiopia cannot be neutral, while the RSF side welcomed it.

World Desk·Thursday, 26 November 2026·4 min read

Key points

  • Drone attacks late on Tuesday, 24 November, killed at least 19 people and injured at least 20 in Damazin and Roseires in Sudan’s Blue Nile state
  • A Khartoum rights group says power facilities in Damazin and the large dam at Roseires were among the targets, and six women were among the dead
  • Ethiopia’s Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed offered on 24 November to host inclusive talks between Sudan’s military, political and civilian groups
  • The army-aligned government rejected the offer on 25 November, accusing Ethiopia of letting RSF-linked drones fly from its territory, which Ethiopia denies
  • The RSF-aligned administration and Abdalla Hamdok’s civilian coalition welcomed the Ethiopian initiative

At least 19 people were killed and at least 20 injured when drones struck Damazin, the capital of Sudan’s Blue Nile state, and the nearby town of Roseires late on Tuesday, 24 November. A Sudanese military source told Al Jazeera that the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (RSF) carried out the attacks, and that three families died when a residential area of Damazin was hit.

Emergency Lawyers, a rights group based in Khartoum, said the drones also targeted electricity facilities in Damazin and the large dam at Roseires, one of Sudan’s biggest hydroelectric plants on the Nile. It said six women were among the dead. The claims could not be independently verified.

The strikes came two days after drones hit Khartoum and Omdurman on 22 November, killing at least four people and damaging a United Nations building, as reported here on Tuesday. Al Jazeera’s correspondent in Omdurman, Almigdad Alruhaid, said there was “no safe zone for civilians” in either city.

Ethiopia’s offer, and Khartoum’s answer

On the same day as the Blue Nile attacks, Ethiopian Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed invited Sudan’s military, political and civilian groups to an inclusive dialogue hosted and sponsored by Ethiopia. According to Addis Standard, he said the aim was to end the war, build national consensus and open the way to a unity government, and that Ethiopia would stand “at the same distance as all parties”.

Sudan’s army-aligned government formally rejected the offer on Wednesday, 25 November, in a statement carried by the state news agency SUNA. It said Ethiopia could not be a neutral host because of allegations that it supports the RSF, including by allowing RSF-linked drones to be launched from its territory. It demanded an independent investigation and urged regional and international actors to keep Ethiopia out of any hosting or mediation role. Ethiopia denies backing the RSF and accuses Sudan of supporting armed groups opposed to its government.

The other side responded very differently. The RSF-aligned Transitional Peace Government said it was ready to engage, with its information minister, Khalid Ahmed Dana, praising Ethiopia’s solidarity, Capital Ethiopia reported. Somoud, the civilian coalition led by former prime minister Abdalla Hamdok, also welcomed the initiative and called for a “genuinely Sudanese-owned peace process”.

Relations between the two neighbours were already badly strained. Earlier in November Ethiopia closed Sudan’s consulate in Assosa and expelled its diplomats, and Sudan responded by closing Ethiopia’s consulate in Gedaref. Separately, a senior delegation from the RSF-aligned Ta’asis alliance was due to meet the five-party mediation mechanism of the African Union, UN, EU, Arab League and IGAD at AU headquarters in Addis Ababa on Wednesday.

A war of many fronts

Analysts quoted by The National say the RSF is opening new fronts far from the main battlefields in the west to stretch the army’s resources. In Blue Nile, the RSF and allied rebel groups have taken a string of small towns and villages near the borders with South Sudan and Ethiopia, about 65 to 70 kilometres from Damazin, although the army still holds all the state’s major cities.

Retired brigadier general Jaafar Hassan told the paper that single big battles have been replaced by “a multitude of fronts”. Meanwhile the army is slowly pushing the RSF out of western Kordofan, with an eye on a later campaign in Darfur. Army chief Gen Abdel Fattah al-Burhan says the war will go on until the RSF is defeated, while RSF leader Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo appears more open to talks.

The war, now in its fourth year, has displaced about 13 million people and pushed parts of the country into famine. With one side welcoming Ethiopia’s offer and the other refusing even to accept it as host, the latest attempt at dialogue looks likely to deepen the divide rather than close it.
