Sport

The Springboks have outscored Europe by 50-odd points this month. Dublin is where that stops mattering.

Sport Desk

South Africa's opening statement in rugby's new Nations Championship could not have been louder. The world champions travelled to Turin on 7 November and put 66 points on an injury-hit Italy side, conceding only a single consolation try to Federico Ruzza in reply. Sacha Feinberg-Mngomezulu finished with 26 points of his own, the Springboks led 45-0 at halftime, and by full-time nine tries had been scored against an Italian defence that simply had no answer. It was the kind of result that makes a global competition's group stage feel like a formality.

It wasn't one, and a trip to Paris in between reminded everyone why. France at the Stade de France is never the fixture a touring side treats lightly, whatever the scoreline elsewhere in the group says about form. The Springboks have made a habit in recent seasons of winning precisely the French Tests that looked hardest on paper, often playing a man down for long stretches and finding a way through anyway — and whatever unfolded this time, the result there will shape South Africa's seeding heading into the competition's business end far more than the Italy scoreline will.

That business end arrives on 21 November at the Aviva Stadium in Dublin, against an Ireland side that has built its own case for being the tournament's most dangerous unbeaten team. Ireland at home is a different proposition entirely from either Italy or France: a pack that competes at the gain line rather than conceding it, a kicking game designed to strangle exactly the kind of expansive rugby South Africa ran up against Italy, and a crowd that has turned Aviva Stadium into one of the hardest venues in the sport for a touring side to silence.

For a Springbok group built around Siya Kolisi's leadership and a squad depth that let them rotate heavily through the November window, Dublin is the Test that actually tests something. Beating a short-handed Italy by 59 points proves a touring squad's gap in resources against a tier-two European side. Beating Ireland away, against a team with genuinely nothing to lose and a result of their own to defend, proves whether this current Springbok group's status as back-to-back world champions still means what it is supposed to mean on the days the opposition is actually good enough to test it.

Beyond the result itself, Dublin also sets the seeding for the Nations Championship's Finals Weekend, three days of fixtures at Allianz Stadium in London from 27 to 29 November that will crown the competition's first-ever champion. A commanding win in Ireland would hand South Africa the kind of seeding advantage that turns a knockout fixture list from a lottery into a formality. A defeat, even a narrow one, could mean the opposite — a reminder that a tournament designed to replace the old scattergun November internationals still punishes exactly the same old mistake: peaking in Turin and arriving in Dublin already running on fumes.
