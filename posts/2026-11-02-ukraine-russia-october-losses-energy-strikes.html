Russia's bloodiest month of the war yet hasn't slowed its grinding advance

World Desk·Monday, 2 November 2026

Ukraine's General Staff says October was the costliest month of the entire war for Russian forces, with total Russian losses since the full-scale invasion now estimated at around 1.59 million troops. It is the kind of casualty figure that, if accurate, would suggest a Russian military machine grinding itself down even as it continues to push forward — a paradox that has defined much of this war's final, exhausting phase.

That push has not let up. Nightly strikes have continued to batter Ukraine's energy infrastructure, leaving hundreds of thousands of people without power on some days and turning winter preparation into as urgent a national task as anything happening on the front line itself. Ukrainian air defences reported intercepting more than a hundred drones on a single overnight wave, out of well over a hundred launched — a ratio that speaks to both the scale of Russia's bombardment campaign and the increasing sophistication Ukraine has developed in countering it.

Ukraine has answered in kind. Long-range drone strikes against Russian energy infrastructure, including a refinery fire reported at the Kstovo facility, are now a routine feature of the war's deeper phase — a sign that both sides have settled into a grinding exchange aimed less at decisive battlefield breakthroughs than at degrading the other's capacity to sustain the fight through another winter.

Diplomatically, the picture remains as unresolved as the fighting itself. The Kremlin has signalled openness to a call between Putin and Zelensky without committing to one, while senior American officials have been candid about their uncertainty over whether a negotiated peace is even achievable in the current climate. A separate $2.1-billion US arms sale, including Patriot missile systems, has been approved — though Kyiv has been warned the deliveries could take years, a timeline that underscores how far off any resolution, military or diplomatic, genuinely looks.

Nearly four years into a war that was originally expected by many observers to last weeks rather than years, the current phase increasingly resembles a contest of endurance: whose infrastructure fails first, whose public will frays first, and whose casualty figures become politically unsustainable first. October's numbers suggest that contest is nowhere close to a conclusion.
