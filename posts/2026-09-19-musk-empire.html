SpaceX's record IPO anchors a sprawling Musk empire

World Desk·Saturday, 19 September 2026

$1.77 trillion. Sit with that number for a moment, because it's easy to let figures this large slide past without really registering. That's the valuation SpaceX commanded when it priced shares at $135 in one of the largest public listings in corporate history, and months later, the aftershocks of that moment are still rippling through how the world thinks about Elon Musk's sprawling, sometimes bewildering business empire. This wasn't just a good day for a rocket company. It was the moment a private space venture, founded on the almost absurd premise of making humanity multi-planetary, became one of the single most valuable companies — public or private — on the face of the Earth.

What makes the number genuinely staggering, though, isn't just its size in isolation — it's what it sits alongside rather than replaces. SpaceX's trillion-dollar-plus valuation didn't arrive because Musk stepped back from his other ventures to focus on rockets. It arrived while he continued running Tesla, the electric vehicle giant that redefined an entire industry; X, the social platform formerly known as Twitter, still generating controversy and headlines in roughly equal measure; xAI, his artificial intelligence venture racing to compete with the very companies dominating this week's other tech headlines; Neuralink, quietly pushing the boundaries of brain-computer interfaces; and The Boring Company, still tunnelling away at infrastructure projects most people forgot existed. Five companies. One person. Simultaneously. It's a portfolio that would represent a demanding full-time job for most executives several times over — and Musk treats it as his baseline operating mode.

This current chapter follows one of the stranger detours in recent business history: a brief but intensely scrutinised stint leading the Trump administration's Department of Government Efficiency earlier this year, a role that put the world's most prominent entrepreneur directly inside the machinery of government policy. That period generated exactly the kind of scrutiny you'd expect when someone with Musk's business footprint takes on a formal government role — endless speculation about conflicts of interest, about which of his companies stood to benefit from which policy decisions, about where the line between public service and private empire actually sat. His eventual step back from that role and return to a purely corporate focus has been widely read as a deliberate attempt to redraw that line more clearly, even if the underlying tension between his business interests and his public influence hasn't gone anywhere.

And that tension is really the story underneath the story here. It's not just that Musk runs multiple massive companies — plenty of holding-company executives technically oversee diverse portfolios. It's that he runs them as an unmistakably hands-on operator, not a passive overseer, showing up in engineering meetings, product decisions, and public statements across all five ventures with a level of personal involvement that would be exhausting to track even as an outside observer, let alone to actually execute. The SpaceX valuation now gives him a financial foundation that dwarfs even his other already-massive holdings, concentrating an almost unprecedented amount of capital and influence into a single individual's hands.

Which brings us to the question that has trailed Musk for the better part of a decade now, one that keeps resurfacing every time a new valuation milestone or headline forces the world to recalculate just how much this one person is actually juggling: how does anyone govern that much, that closely, at the same time? It's a question that has never really been answered — not by Musk himself, not by the boards nominally overseeing these companies, not by the market, which keeps rewarding the arrangement with ever-higher valuations regardless of how implausible the underlying workload sounds. For now, the trillion-dollar answer the market seems to be giving is simple: it doesn't matter how, as long as it keeps working. Whether that remains true indefinitely is a different question entirely — and one this latest valuation milestone only makes more urgent to ask.
