While the east floods, the west is baking: SAWS warns of 40-degree heat and hyperthermia risk this week

Weather Desk

South Africa is living through two entirely different weather stories at once this week, split almost cleanly down the middle of the country. In the Namakwa district of the Northern Cape and the interior of the Western Cape, the South African Weather Service is warning of temperatures reaching 36 to 40 degrees Celsius on Monday, hot and uncomfortable enough that forecasters have issued specific guidance urging residents to stay hydrated because of the real risk of hyperthermia. Meanwhile, in the country's east, an entirely different hazard has been playing out: an orange level-5 flooding warning for central and southern KwaZulu-Natal, following days of severe thunderstorms that battered Gauteng, Mpumalanga, the Free State and North West with hail, damaging winds and excessive lightning.

The two patterns are connected rather than coincidental. A surface trough sitting over the country's central and western interior has been pulling hot, dry air up from the Northern Cape's desert reaches even as a separate moisture-laden system continues feeding thunderstorm activity further east, producing the kind of simultaneous extremes — scorching heat on one side of the country, flood risk on the other — that have become a recognisable feature of South African spring weather in recent seasons. A hailstorm in the Free State earlier in the week left damaged homes and vehicles in its wake, a reminder that the eastern system's severity has had real, costly consequences well beyond the headline flood warning.

For communities in the Namakwa district, where summer heat is a familiar seasonal reality but rarely arrives with quite this intensity this early, the warning carries practical urgency. Hyperthermia — the body's inability to regulate its core temperature in extreme heat — poses the greatest danger to the very young, the elderly, outdoor workers and anyone without reliable access to shade, water or cooling. Municipal and provincial health authorities in affected areas have historically used exactly this kind of warning to activate heat-response protocols, from extended hours at public cooling facilities to targeted outreach in informal settlements where indoor temperatures can climb well above whatever the outside thermometer reads.

SAWS has tied this week's extremes to a broader seasonal pattern rather than treating it as an isolated event. The forecaster's longer-range outlook, shaped by a developing El Nino pattern, points to a summer running hotter and drier than usual across large parts of the country, even as isolated severe storm systems continue to produce the kind of flash flooding and hail damage seen in Free State and KwaZulu-Natal this week. The apparent contradiction — a drier-than-normal summer overall, punctuated by locally severe flooding — is consistent with how El Nino-influenced seasons have behaved in South Africa before: less total rainfall, but delivered in more concentrated, more damaging bursts when it does arrive.

For now, the practical advice splits along the same geographic line as the weather itself. In the west, hydration, shade and caution around prolonged outdoor exposure; in the east, vigilance around flood-prone roads and waterways as the KwaZulu-Natal warning remains active. Both halves of the warning are, in their own way, previews of a summer SAWS has already flagged as likely to test the country's capacity to manage weather extremes at both ends of the spectrum.
