SAWS has a new word for this summer's weather: volatile, and it means heatwaves as much as hail

Weather Desk

The South African Weather Service's most recent warning, issued this week, describes scorching, El Niño-driven heat pushing into Mpumalanga, KwaZulu-Natal, the Free State and Gauteng, with hot, humid conditions over KwaZulu-Natal in particular and veld fire danger flagged across the affected provinces. It is a notably different message from the one SAWS was issuing barely a week earlier, when a level 4 destructive weather warning covering the same broad swathe of the country was about hail, flooding and damaging winds rather than heat.

That whiplash between hazard types is, forecasters say, exactly the character of this El Niño-tilted summer rather than a contradiction in the data. El Niño's warming of the central and eastern Pacific tends to produce drier, hotter conditions over much of southern Africa, but it does so unevenly, interrupted by the kind of intense convective storm systems that build rapidly over a heated, moisture-laden interior and then discharge violently. The same atmospheric setup that bakes the Highveld for days can, within the same week, organise into the thunderstorm cells that triggered last weekend's hail and flooding warnings.

For the provinces now under heat warning, the practical risk has shifted from storm damage to fire. Veld fires spread fastest and burn hottest in exactly the conditions SAWS is now forecasting — dry vegetation, high temperatures, low humidity in the hours before a front moves through — and fire services across the affected provinces have been placed on alert accordingly. Farmers watching both the fire risk and the memory of last weekend's hail damage to standing crops face a planting season defined by extremes rather than a predictable middle ground.

Cape Town and the Western Cape, by contrast, have largely sat outside both the storm and heat warnings issued over the past fortnight, a reminder that this is very much a summer-rainfall-region story rather than a nationwide one. The coastal city's own weather has tracked a steadier, more typically Mediterranean pattern through the same stretch that has seen the interior swing between extremes.

What forecasters are not offering is a sense that either the storms or the heat will settle into a calmer, more predictable pattern as summer proper arrives. If this El Niño season behaves the way previous ones have, the alternation between destructive rain events and scorching, fire-prone heat is likely to continue defining South Africa's summer weather right through to autumn, rather than resolving into one or the other.
