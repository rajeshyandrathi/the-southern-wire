Hermanus's whale season enters its final weeks, with sightings still close to guaranteed

Lifestyle Desk

For visitors weighing whether there is still time this year to see southern right whales from the cliffs above Walker Bay, the answer from Hermanus's tour operators and guides is a cautious yes, but not for much longer. The town's whale season typically runs from June through to around December, with September and October standing as the reliable peak when hundreds of whales crowd the bay to mate and calve, and November marking the point where sightings, while still likely, begin to taper as the animals start their journey back out to deeper water.

What makes Hermanus distinctive, even among South Africa's other coastal viewing spots, is how little effort watching actually requires. Unlike boat-based whale-watching destinations elsewhere in the world, Hermanus's geography allows whales to be spotted directly from shore, along cliff paths and lookout points scattered through the town, a quirk of coastline and water depth that earned the town its long-standing reputation as the whale-watching capital of South Africa. Tour operators have, in recent years, added a City Sightseeing bus route connecting Cape Town directly to Hermanus for winter and spring whale-watching day trips, widening access for visitors without their own transport for the roughly 120-kilometre drive.

The biological rhythm behind the season is itself worth the trip to understand, not just witness. Southern right whales migrate to the sheltered, relatively warm waters of Walker Bay specifically to calve and nurse their young away from the open-ocean predators and rougher conditions further offshore, a pattern that has made the bay one of the most reliable land-based whale nurseries anywhere in the world. By November, many of the calves born earlier in the season have grown strong enough to begin the return journey south with their mothers, which is both the reason sightings start to thin out and, for those who do catch a glimpse, often the reason the encounters feel more charged, mother and calf pairs preparing for a long open-water journey together.

Hermanus's broader tourism economy leans heavily on this seasonal rhythm, and local operators describe November bookings as something of a final push before attention shifts toward the summer's other coastal draws, beach tourism, wine-route day trips and the Western Cape's long, dry summer stretch. The town's annual Whale Festival, usually held earlier in spring to coincide with peak sighting season, has already passed for this year, meaning November visitors are chasing a quieter, more improvised version of the experience rather than the festival's organised crowds and programming.

For anyone still weighing the trip, local guides offer the same advice they give every year at this point in the season: come soon, manage expectations around sighting frequency compared to September's peak, and treat any encounter as a genuine privilege rather than a guaranteed photograph. Marine life, as every operator is careful to caveat, behaves on its own schedule, not a tourism calendar's.
