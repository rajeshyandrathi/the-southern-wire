After a one-cent save, December is shaping up as petrol's best month in years — diesel is heading the other way

Business Desk

Days ago, households across the country were still absorbing the news that a R10 billion fuel-levy relief package had translated into a one-cent saving at the pump, a number so small it barely registered against a December increase everyone had already been warned to expect. That warning now looks out of date. The latest projections built from Central Energy Fund data point not to a hike in December's fuel price adjustment but to a cut, and not a token one: petrol 93 is tracking toward a drop of somewhere between R1.50 and just over R2 a litre, with 95 unleaded falling by a similar margin.

The reversal has nothing to do with government generosity and everything to do with global oil prices doing what domestic tax policy could not. South Africa's fuel price formula is driven primarily by international product prices and the rand-dollar exchange rate, and when both move in a motorist's favour at the same time, even a government quietly scaling back its own tax relief cannot keep the pump price from falling. That is the quiet irony sitting underneath December's numbers: petrol is about to get meaningfully cheaper in the same month that the temporary fuel-levy cut shrinks from R1.50 to R1 a litre, a reduction in relief that would ordinarily push prices up rather than down.

Diesel tells the opposite story, and the reason is structural rather than coincidental. Diesel's levy relief is contracting by a larger margin than petrol's, dropping from R3 to R1.50 a litre, which means the underlying cost relief diesel has enjoyed since the government's temporary measure took effect in early November is being withdrawn twice as fast. Even with international diesel costs easing alongside petrol's, the shrinking levy cut is large enough to push pump prices for both grades of diesel higher in December, by anywhere from roughly R1.15 to just over R2 a litre depending on the grade and which forecast proves closest to the eventual official announcement.

For households, the practical effect is a rare bit of relief at exactly the time of year fuel costs usually bite hardest, as families drive further for the festive season and retailers lean on diesel-powered logistics to stock shelves for it. For the logistics and agricultural sectors that run disproportionately on diesel rather than petrol, the picture is less comfortable: a cost that was already elevated through most of 2026 is about to climb again just as peak-season freight volumes demand the most from it. The split outcome captures something true about how fuel pricing actually works in South Africa — international markets and domestic tax policy pulling in different directions, with ordinary consumers caught in whichever direction wins on any given month.

Nothing is final until the Department of Mineral and Petroleum Resources publishes its official adjustment on 2 December, and recent months have shown how much projections can still shift in the final days before that announcement. But for a government that spent the first week of November explaining why its flagship fuel relief measure had delivered so little to ordinary drivers, a genuine double-digit cent drop at the pump next month would be the first piece of fuel news in a while that actually lands the way it is meant to.
