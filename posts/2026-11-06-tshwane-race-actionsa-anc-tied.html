In Tshwane, the next government may come down to a single percentage point

Politics Desk

Of all the municipalities South Africa's electoral commission is still working through, none is producing a closer or more consequential finish than the capital. In partial results out of Tshwane, ActionSA and the ANC are separated by less than a single percentage point, with the DA sitting a clear but insufficient third, a scoreline that would have seemed implausible to most observers on election night and almost unthinkable five years ago.

The scale of the shift is the real story. ActionSA fought the 2021 election as a two-year-old party with no councillors to defend and little name recognition outside a handful of Johannesburg wards. In Tshwane this week it is running neck and neck with the ANC for outright control of a metro that manages the seat of the national government, a billion-rand-plus budget and some of the country's most contested service-delivery backlogs. Party strategists on all sides say the result reflects less a positive endorsement of ActionSA's platform than a protest vote against both established parties' records in the capital, but the practical effect is the same regardless of motive.

Whichever of the two front-runners ends up marginally ahead when the IEC makes its declaration, neither will have anything close to a governing majority on its own. That makes the DA's third-place finish, usually a disappointing outcome, into genuine leverage: with ActionSA and the ANC locked so tightly together, the DA's councillors could plausibly swing the mayoral vote toward either side, depending on which coalition offer proves more attractive. Smaller parties holding single-digit percentages, including the Patriotic Alliance and the Freedom Front Plus, are also likely to find themselves fielded calls from all three bigger players in the coming days.

The timing adds pressure to an already delicate negotiation. The commission's legal deadline to declare final results nationally falls next Wednesday, and officials have indicated they intend to make their Tshwane declaration as part of Sunday's broader announcement, which leaves coalition talks compressed into a matter of days rather than the weeks some previous post-election negotiations have dragged on for. Whoever ultimately takes the mayoral chair will inherit a city where water and electricity infrastructure failures have become a weekly occurrence in several regions, giving the incoming administration little room for a prolonged honeymoon.

For ActionSA, a share of power in the capital would be the clearest validation yet of its strategy of positioning itself as a disciplined, service-delivery-focused alternative rather than a protest party built around a single personality. For the ANC, losing its grip on the city that houses the Union Buildings, even to a fractional margin, would be a symbolically painful coda to a count that has already gone worse than the party hoped nationally. Both outcomes remain live until the final slip is captured.
