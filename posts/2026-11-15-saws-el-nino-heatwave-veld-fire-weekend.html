SAWS's forecast for this summer keeps arriving in the same two words: hotter, drier

Weather Desk

The South African Weather Service has stopped varying its message much from one bulletin to the next, and that consistency is itself the story. Its outlook for November 2026 through January 2027 reads almost identically to the one it issued for October through December: drier and warmer conditions across most of the country, driven by an El Nino pattern the Service expects to shape the entire 2026/27 summer season rather than deliver one bad week and move on. For a country whose summer weather usually swings hard between extremes within the same fortnight, a forecast this stable is its own kind of warning.

That pattern has already produced exactly the volatility SAWS predicted, just compressed into shorter cycles than usual. Gauteng, the Northern Cape and the Free State spent the back half of this past week under heat warnings that forecasters said would persist into Friday, carrying an explicit veld fire risk alongside the discomfort of the temperatures themselves. A week earlier, the Western Cape and Northern Cape were under a separate warning for daytime highs between 36 and 40 degrees Celsius, hot enough to qualify as genuinely dangerous for outdoor workers and vulnerable residents, not merely uncomfortable.

The veld fire dimension of this pattern deserves particular attention heading into the weekend. Dry vegetation left over from a winter that produced less rainfall than usual, combined with sustained high temperatures and the wind patterns that typically accompany this kind of heat system, creates close to ideal conditions for fires to spread quickly once started, whether by lightning, agricultural burning gone wrong, or simple human carelessness. Provinces under active heat warnings are also, almost by definition, provinces where a single spark has more room to become a genuine emergency.

What makes El Nino summers specifically tricky to forecast around is the way they trade extremes rather than settling into one. The same broad pattern that produces a scorching, fire-prone heatwave in one week can produce a level-4 storm warning with damaging hail the next, as this season has already demonstrated once. Residents and disaster management officials alike are having to plan for both outcomes simultaneously rather than assuming the current heat is simply this summer's fixed state, because the same system that built the heat is just as capable of swinging back toward severe storms with little warning.

For now, the practical guidance has not changed from one bulletin to the next either: conserve water, respect fire bans where they are in place, and treat any heat warning in Gauteng, the Northern Cape, the Free State or the Western Cape as a signal to adjust plans rather than push through. SAWS's own seasonal outlook suggests this is not a passing spell. It is the operating condition for the next three months, with the only real uncertainty being which extreme shows up on which particular week.
