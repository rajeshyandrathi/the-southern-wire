Eskom quietly closes in on 500 days without a single rotational power cut

Business Desk

There was a time, not so long ago, when a milestone like this would have dominated the national conversation for days. Eskom confirmed this week that South Africa is closing in on 500 consecutive days without national load shedding, a streak that has run quietly in the background of a news cycle dominated by elections, trade disputes and political violence, but one that represents one of the more remarkable turnarounds in the utility's recent history.

The improvement traces back to a combination of factors Eskom and independent energy analysts have been pointing to for the better part of two years: better planned maintenance discipline at the coal fleet, the return to service of previously unreliable units, and a steady expansion of private and utility-scale renewable capacity that has added genuine headroom to the grid rather than merely patching over its weaknesses. None of those fixes arrived overnight, and none of them fully resolve the structural problems, chiefly an ageing coal fleet well past its intended lifespan, that caused the crisis in the first place.

What has not disappeared is load reduction, a narrower and far less discussed cousin of load shedding that targets specific, localised networks rather than the national grid. Eskom's own figures show hundreds of feeders still subject to scheduled outages because of illegal connections, overloaded transformers and ageing local infrastructure, concentrated disproportionately in townships and informal settlements. The utility has set a target of clearing the majority of affected feeders by the end of this year, with the remainder expected to take until 2027 to resolve.

For businesses that spent the early 2020s budgeting for diesel generators and backup power as a cost of doing business in South Africa, the shift has been significant enough to show up in investment decisions, with several manufacturers citing grid stability as one factor, among several, behind decisions to expand domestic capacity rather than relocate it. Economists remain cautious about overstating the effect, noting that structural constraints, logistics bottlenecks, policy uncertainty, and now the loss of AGOA access, still weigh more heavily on growth forecasts than electricity supply alone.

Eskom's leadership has been careful this week not to declare victory, warning that winter 2027 will be the next real test of whether the improvement reflects a durable fix or a fortunate run helped by lower-than-usual demand and a currently cooperative generation fleet. For a utility that spent a decade apologising for the state of the grid, even that cautious framing marks a striking change in tone.
