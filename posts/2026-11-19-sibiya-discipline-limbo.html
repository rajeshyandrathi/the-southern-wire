Crime & Courts

The commission says Sibiya should face criminal charges. Then it told him it’s done asking questions.

Crime Desk

There is a particular kind of South African limbo reserved for officials accused of serious wrongdoing: formally flagged, informally untouched. Shadrack Sibiya, suspended as deputy national police commissioner, is living in it. The Madlanga Commission's own interim findings recommended that he face a criminal investigation and disciplinary proceedings, alongside three other officials, over allegations that sit at the center of the inquiry's entire mandate — political interference in policing, the suppression of organised-crime investigations, and the networks KwaZulu-Natal police commissioner Nhlanhla Mkhwanazi spent over a year describing in testimony the commission's own chair eventually called grave enough to require no further embellishment.

And yet the commission has now told Sibiya it does not intend to recall him for further testimony. On paper that is a procedural footnote — commissions of inquiry eventually have to stop taking evidence and start writing conclusions. In practice it closes the one forum where Sibiya has been directly, repeatedly confronted with the specific allegations against him, including the claim that he made a threatening call to a witness's wife to choke off testimony before it happened. He used that forum to deny everything, to accuse Mkhwanazi and the MK Party of plotting against him, and to insist his suspension was a political hit job rather than an accountability measure. None of those denials have been tested anywhere else yet, because nowhere else has moved.

That is the gap this story actually sits in. A commission of inquiry can recommend a criminal investigation; it cannot open one. It can recommend disciplinary proceedings; it cannot convene the disciplinary board itself. Both of those steps belong to institutions — prosecutors, SAPS's own internal discipline structure — that answer to a far slower, far more politically exposed process than a commission hearing room, and months after the recommendation landed, there is no public sign either has actually started in earnest. Sibiya remains suspended, which costs him his functions but not, as things stand, his rank, his pension accrual, or his presumption of innocence in any forum that could actually convict him of something.

The timing compounds the problem. Just as the commission is winding down its direct engagement with Sibiya, it is still working through a roster of other subpoenaed witnesses — among them cabinet-level and senior SAPS figures — whose own testimony could yet corroborate or complicate what's already on the record against him. Closing the door on Sibiya's cross-examination before that testimony is complete risks locking in his version of events as the last word he personally gave, even as the broader picture around him keeps shifting.

For a commission whose entire premise was that South Africa's police service had been compromised from the inside, declining to press a key suspect any further — while the actual mechanisms for holding him accountable sit idle — is not a neutral administrative choice. It is a decision about where accountability for one of the most serious policing scandals of the decade is actually going to happen: everywhere, it turns out, except where the person accused is required to answer for it under oath.
