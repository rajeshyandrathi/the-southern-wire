Business & Economy

Transnet is about to be split into pieces. The rail network is the piece everyone is watching.

Business Desk

Transnet's breakup is no longer a policy aspiration sitting in a state-of-the-nation speech. A technical task team is being set up before the end of the year with a specific, narrow job: finalise how the Transnet Rail Infrastructure Manager, the division that owns and maintains the actual rail network, gets carved out of the Transnet Group and stood up as its own entity. A parallel transaction will do the same for the Transnet National Ports Authority, unbundling it from the group and splitting the conglomerate's liabilities equitably between the pieces that emerge. For a state logistics company that spent the better part of a decade being treated as a single, failing whole, breaking it into parts that can be fixed individually is the clearest sign yet that government has stopped hoping for a single turnaround and started managing a structured one.

The rail infrastructure piece is the one with the most riding on it. South Africa's freight rail network has lost volumes for years to a combination of cable theft, locomotive shortages and maintenance backlogs that successive turnaround plans never quite fixed, and the logic behind separating the infrastructure manager from the operator is familiar from electricity reform: let one entity focus purely on keeping the physical network in working order, while opening the operating side to competition from players who actually want to run trains on it. Grindrod running the first private train operating company test on the network this month is an early, tangible sign that the competitive-access model is moving from policy document to actual rolling stock.

Ports are following a similar script, just one step behind. Transnet Port Terminals is reportedly weighing further private partnerships to accelerate volume growth, building on an existing push to modernise the Durban Container Terminal with private capital, the single busiest and most chronically congested port facility in the country. Durban's container queues have been a recurring embarrassment for South African trade logistics for years, the kind of bottleneck that shows up in exporters' margins long before it shows up in a government press release, which is precisely why private investment there carries more symbolic weight than the rand value of any single contract.

None of this is happening in a vacuum. Eskom's own unbundling, which produced an independent transmission company, is being held up by officials as the template Transnet is now following, sequenced carefully over roughly eighteen months rather than rushed through in one disruptive cut. The lesson from Eskom's experience, still playing out as the state tries to stabilise generation while opening the grid to new players, is that structural separation buys time and focus, but does not by itself fix decades of underinvestment. Transnet's task team inherits that same tension: a cleaner organisational chart is not the same thing as more locomotives, fewer cable thefts, or a port that clears ships on schedule.

What the coming months will actually test is whether splitting Transnet into a rail infrastructure manager, a ports authority and whatever remains of the core group produces the private investment and operational discipline the reform is banking on, or simply rearranges which state entity gets blamed when the trains are still late and the ships are still queued. The task team's mandate is to finalise the approach before the year is out. Whether that approach moves freight any faster is a question only 2027 can actually answer.
