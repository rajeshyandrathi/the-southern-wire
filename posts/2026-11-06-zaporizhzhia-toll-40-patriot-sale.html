The Zaporizhzhia school-shelter death toll climbs to 40 as Washington approves a $2.1-billion Patriot sale to Kyiv

World Desk

The death toll from a Russian strike on a school-turned-shelter in the Zaporizhzhia region town of Komyshuvakha has climbed to around 40, with dozens more still listed as missing beneath rubble that rescue crews have continued working through in the days since the attack. The building had been converted into both a civilian shelter and a humanitarian aid distribution point, making it one of the deadlier single strikes on non-combatants in a war that has produced a grim and lengthening list of such incidents.

A fragile, localised truce elsewhere along the front has offered a sharp contrast to the Zaporizhzhia devastation. In the town of Oleshky, a ceasefire specific to that area has held for a third consecutive day, long enough for an aid mission to enter and begin distributing supplies to a population that has endured repeated rounds of fighting. The juxtaposition, a functioning local truce in one town and a catastrophic strike on a shelter in another, captures how unevenly any de-escalation has actually spread across the broader conflict.

Away from the front lines, Ukraine has intensified its own campaign against Russian energy infrastructure after a brief pause in late October. Strikes this week hit a fire at the Ryazan refinery, one of Russia's largest, while Russia said it struck a Nova Poshta logistics terminal near Kharkiv and more than 80 drones hit the surrounding region over a single day. Ukrainian air defence said it had neutralised 105 drones in roughly 24 hours, a volume that illustrates how much of the war's current intensity is being fought through long-range strikes on infrastructure rather than classic front-line manoeuvre.

The clearest sign of Washington's current posture came in the form of money rather than diplomacy. The United States approved a $2.1 billion sale of Patriot air-defence interceptors to Ukraine this week, a transaction that will take time to translate into deployed systems but that signals no imminent reduction in American military support regardless of the domestic political shifts that followed last week's midterm elections. President Zelensky has argued publicly that the new composition of Congress could, over time, push Russian President Vladimir Putin to reconsider the war's trajectory, though nothing in this week's fighting suggested Moscow sees the midterms as reason to change course.

Ukrainian government estimates, while difficult to verify independently, continue to describe a war of attrition at extraordinary human cost on the Russian side specifically: cumulative Russian combat losses are now put at roughly 1.6 million personnel since the February 2022 invasion, with daily reported losses still running in the thousands. Whatever the precise accuracy of those figures, the scale they describe, combined with continued large-scale Western arms transfers and no visible progress toward talks, points to a conflict still a long way from any negotiated resolution.
