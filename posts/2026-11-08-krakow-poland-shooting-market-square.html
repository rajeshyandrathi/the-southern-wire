A gunman opens fire in Krakow's historic main square, killing six in one of Poland's deadliest attacks in years

World Desk

A shooting in Krakow's Rynek Główny, the largest medieval town square in Europe and ordinarily filled with tourists and weekend market stalls, left at least six people dead and fifteen wounded on Saturday. Police say officers shot and detained the attacker at the scene and believe he acted alone, though the investigation into his motive and any possible accomplices remains active. The square, a UNESCO World Heritage site at the heart of one of Poland's most visited cities, was placed under lockdown as emergency services worked to clear the area and treat the wounded.

Polish authorities have been careful in early statements not to characterise the attack's motive while forensic and ballistic work continues, a caution that has not stopped speculation given the square's symbolic weight and its popularity with both domestic visitors and foreign tourists. The scale of casualties, among the highest from a single attacker in Poland in recent memory, has prompted an immediate review of security arrangements at similarly open public spaces in other major Polish cities, where large pedestrian squares have historically relied on visible but comparatively light policing.

The attack lands at a moment when European security services have already been on heightened alert, following a wave of bomb hoaxes and infrastructure disruptions across the continent in recent weeks that analysts have linked to a broader pattern of low-cost, hard-to-attribute disruption. Whether Saturday's shooting in Krakow connects to any of that activity or represents an entirely separate, isolated act of violence is a question investigators have not yet answered publicly, and officials have been explicit that no link should be assumed without evidence.

For Krakow itself, a city that has built much of its modern economy around tourism to its historic centre, the attack strikes at the square's core identity as a safe, open gathering place. Local businesses around the Rynek, many of them cafes and restaurants that depend on exactly the kind of weekend foot traffic the shooting disrupted, now face the harder task of reassuring visitors that the square remains the welcoming landmark it has been for centuries, even as the city absorbs the shock of Saturday's violence.

International reaction has been swift, with condolences arriving from European leaders and renewed calls for coordinated continental responses to the kind of public security threats that have become harder to predict and prevent. For now, Polish investigators are focused on the narrower task of establishing exactly what drove one man to open fire on a crowd in one of the country's most recognisable public spaces, and what, if anything, could have stopped him sooner.
