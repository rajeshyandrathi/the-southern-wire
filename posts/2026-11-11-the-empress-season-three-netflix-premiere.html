The Habsburg court's most unruly empress returns, and the crown still doesn't fit her

Culture Desk

Netflix's German-language costume drama about Empress Elisabeth of Austria returns this week for a third season, continuing one of the streamer's most unlikely international hits — a lavishly mounted, resolutely non-English-language period piece that has nonetheless built a devoted global audience far beyond the German-speaking markets it was originally made for. The show has always sold itself less as a history lesson than as a character study of a woman who never wanted the throne she was handed, and three seasons in, that tension still hasn't resolved.

Elisabeth's appeal as a dramatic subject has always rested on the mismatch between her era's expectations of a Habsburg empress — decorous, decorative, politically invisible — and a woman whose real historical reputation was for restlessness, rebellion against court protocol, and a famously fraught relationship with the rigid Viennese aristocracy she married into. The series has leaned hard into that mismatch from its opening episodes, treating Elisabeth less as a monarch than as a young woman trapped inside an institution built centuries before she was asked to embody it.

That framing has made the show a particularly good fit for a streaming audience that has shown, across multiple period dramas in recent years, a strong appetite for royal and aristocratic settings read through a distinctly modern lens — stories that use the trappings of empire and court ritual to explore very contemporary anxieties about identity, autonomy and the cost of institutional belonging. A third season renewal, especially for a non-English production, signals that Netflix sees the format working as intended rather than simply riding a first-season novelty.

The production values that helped the show travel internationally despite the language barrier — elaborate court settings, meticulous costuming, a visual richness that reads well even to viewers with no particular interest in nineteenth-century Austrian history — remain central to its pitch. Subtitled historical drama rarely crosses over into mainstream global conversation the way this series has managed to, and that crossover owes as much to its craft as to its subject.

Three seasons in, the question the show keeps circling is whether Elisabeth ever actually escapes the gilded cage the series has spent two seasons establishing, or whether her story is really about learning to wear it differently. For a character whose real historical legacy includes a reputation for restlessness that never fully left her, a satisfying ending may be the one thing the format cannot deliver — and the one thing viewers keep returning to find out.
