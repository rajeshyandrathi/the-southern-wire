Transnet's rail arm is finally being cut loose. A task team will decide how.

Business Desk

A technical task team is being assembled before the end of the year to oversee one of the most significant structural changes South African logistics has seen in decades: the separation of Transnet's Rail Infrastructure Manager from the rest of the Transnet Group, alongside the parallel unbundling of the Transnet National Ports Authority. For an economy that has spent years watching export volumes bleed out through rail bottlenecks and port congestion, the creation of a formal body to manage the split is the clearest sign yet that reform has moved from policy paper to implementation.

The logic behind separating the Rail Infrastructure Manager is straightforward in theory and politically fraught in practice. By hiving off the entity that owns and maintains the physical rail network from the entity that actually operates trains on it, government aims to open the tracks to competing operators without forcing Transnet to compete against companies running on infrastructure it still controls. Grindrod's recent test train, run as the first private operating company to use the network under the new access framework, is being treated inside the industry as the first tangible proof that the model can work in practice rather than remaining a theoretical fix.

None of this addresses the scale of what still needs fixing. Transnet has previously outlined plans for R127 billion in infrastructure upgrades, a figure that reflects just how badly deferred maintenance and underinvestment have degraded a network that once moved the bulk of the country's mineral exports reliably. Mining companies that shifted tonnage onto road transport during the worst of the rail crisis have been reluctant to shift it back until they see sustained evidence that locomotive availability and line reliability have actually improved, not merely that the corporate structure around them has changed.

The ports side of the reform carries its own urgency. Transnet Port Terminals is reportedly exploring further private-sector partnerships specifically to accelerate volume growth, an acknowledgement that container and bulk handling capacity has lagged behind what exporters need even as global shipping patterns shift. Every week a vessel sits waiting offshore for a berth is a cost passed directly on to South African producers trying to compete in international markets already squeezed by freight rates and currency volatility.

Whether the new task team can move faster than South Africa's state-owned enterprise reforms typically do remains the open question hanging over all of this. Eskom's own unbundling has been sequenced over an eighteen-month runway that has already tested the patience of businesses waiting for a genuinely open electricity market. Transnet's reformers will be watched just as closely, by exporters, by mining houses, and by a Treasury that has staked real credibility on logistics reform actually translating into growth rather than becoming another slow-moving restructuring exercise.
