Sudan's maximum alert has a quieter casualty: the hospitals the grid used to power

Politics Desk

A maximum alert declared after a drone strike on Kurmuk county was always going to dominate the headlines out of Khartoum this week. Less visible, but arguably more dangerous, is what happens inside the fourteen power stations the strikes have already knocked offline. Sudan's grid does not just light homes; it keeps water pumps running, cold-chain storage for vaccines intact, and the ventilators in what remains of the country's hospital system switched on. Each additional station that goes dark pushes more of that infrastructure onto diesel generators that aid agencies say are running out of fuel faster than they can be resupplied.

Khartoum's cabinet has spent the week pointing fingers at Ethiopia and the United Arab Emirates, accusing both of enabling the drone campaign that is dismantling the grid piece by piece. Neither government has offered a substantive public rebuttal, and independent verification of the specific supply chains behind the RSF's drone fleet remains difficult from outside the country. What is not in dispute is the physical damage: a nationwide alert declared over infrastructure, not territory, is itself a sign of how central the power network has become to both sides' strategic calculations in a war that has already displaced millions.

For hospitals still functioning in and around Khartoum and the Blue Nile region, the blackouts translate into decisions no medical staff should have to make. Generator fuel gets rationed toward operating theatres and neonatal units first, with wards further down the priority list going dark for hours at a time. Aid organisations operating in the country have described similar triage happening at water treatment plants, where pumps that would normally run continuously are being cycled on and off to conserve what diesel remains, risking contamination in supply lines that millions of displaced people depend on.

The war's broader toll is already staggering by any measure. More than 200,000 people are estimated to have been killed since fighting broke out, and the United Nations has repeatedly described Sudan's displacement crisis as the largest in the world, eclipsing even Syria's at its peak. A grid collapse layered on top of that does not show up in casualty counts in the way a drone strike does, but aid workers warn it compounds every other crisis the country is facing, from cholera outbreaks to acute malnutrition in displacement camps that now stretch across multiple provinces.

There is no clear mechanism on the table to stop the attacks on infrastructure, let alone end the war itself. Diplomatic efforts led by regional and international mediators have repeatedly stalled, and neither the Sudanese army nor the RSF has shown sustained willingness to accept a ceasefire that does not also hand them a decisive military advantage. For now, Sudan's maximum alert is as much about the lights staying on in an intensive care ward as it is about the next drone in the sky.
