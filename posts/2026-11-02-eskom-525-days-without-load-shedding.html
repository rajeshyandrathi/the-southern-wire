Eskom marks 525 days without load shedding, but summer's real test still looms

Business Desk·Monday, 2 November 2026

It has now been 525 days since Eskom last implemented load shedding, a run stretching back to May last year that would have sounded fantastical to anyone living through the rolling blackouts of 2023. The utility's latest update points to a reserve margin of nearly 5,700 megawatts of surplus capacity, a buffer that just a few years ago would have been unthinkable for a power system that spent the better part of a decade running on fumes.

The financial turnaround underpinning that stability is just as striking as the outage-free streak itself. Eskom's diesel spending — the expensive stopgap fuel burned by open-cycle gas turbines whenever the grid came under strain — fell by R4.79 billion, a drop of more than 80%, between April and October this year. Less diesel burned means less emergency generation needed, which in turn means a utility that is finally managing supply through planning rather than crisis response.

None of this means the lights-out era is fully behind every South African household. Load reduction — a separate, more targeted measure used in areas with illegal connections or overloaded local infrastructure — still affects parts of the country, even as seven provinces have now achieved its complete elimination. The distinction matters: national load shedding and localised load reduction are different problems with different causes, and conflating them risks understating just how much has genuinely improved at the national grid level while still leaving real frustration for residents in affected pockets.

The open question, and the one utility executives have been noticeably quiet about, is what happens when summer demand peaks. Eskom has yet to publish its outlook for the December-to-February period, the stretch when air conditioning load and industrial demand have historically tested the grid's limits even in relatively healthy years. A 525-day run is a genuine achievement; it is not yet proof that the underlying fleet can handle sustained peak demand without the kind of unplanned breakdowns that triggered previous crises.

For now, though, South Africans are heading into a festive season with a level of grid confidence that would have seemed implausible only two summers ago — proof, if nothing else, that the country's most chronic infrastructure failure of the past decade is, for the moment, genuinely in retreat.
