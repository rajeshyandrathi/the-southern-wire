Polls open across South Africa in the most unpredictable local election in a generation

The Southern Wire Desk·Johannesburg

Voting stations across South Africa opened their doors at 7am on Wednesday, and by mid-morning the queues outside them told their own story: patient, long, and in many places unusually mixed in age and allegiance. Some 23,696 stations are operating across all 257 municipalities, staffed by roughly 188,000 electoral officials, for a ballot the Independent Electoral Commission says will be decided by a record 29.2 million registered voters. Nothing about the scale of this election is modest. What makes it genuinely unusual is how little anyone can confidently predict about where it ends.

That uncertainty is the real story of the day. Four parties with a credible claim on real power — the ANC, the DA, the EFF and a rapidly growing MK Party — are all contesting the same pool of urban voters in Johannesburg, Tshwane and Ekurhuleni, and polling in the run-up to the vote found no outright winner in any of the three. In Johannesburg, DA candidate Helen Zille is projected to take the largest single share of the vote without coming close to a majority, which would leave the country's biggest city facing fresh coalition talks within days of counting. Tshwane looks similarly fractured, with the DA and ANC running close enough that the gap could flip on late-reporting wards.

The IEC insists the mechanics are holding up. Officials say the logistical, security and operational arrangements needed to run a vote of this size are in place, with integrated security plans covering every province and extra deployments protecting voting stations, materials and staff. That reassurance comes against an uneasy backdrop: special voting earlier in the week was marred by delivery delays in parts of Cape Town, NATJOINTS logged 21 criminal cases and six arrests linked to the process, and the campaign's final days were scarred by political violence serious enough that it has become its own running story this week. All campaigning, by law, stopped at midnight on Tuesday, and no party regalia or canvassing is permitted anywhere near a polling station.

For the Government of National Unity, formed uneasily after the 2024 national election, the result carries weight well beyond town-hall politics. A poor municipal showing for either the ANC or the DA would sharpen tensions that have simmered inside the coalition over the budget, foreign policy and, most recently, Washington's decision to strip South Africa of its AGOA trade benefits. A strong EFF or MK Party performance in the metros that matter economically could complicate governing arrangements just as the country tries to present a united front to international partners and investors.

By Wednesday night, once stations close at 9pm, the first trickle of results should begin arriving from smaller, simpler-to-count municipalities. The metros, with their size and ward-by-ward complexity, will take longer, and the IEC has indicated final results could take up to seven days. For a country that has grown accustomed to hung councils as the rule rather than the exception, the only real surprise by week's end would be a party winning outright anywhere that matters.
