Spain's housing crisis just became an election. The right smells a majority.

World Desk

Pedro Sanchez has begun campaigning for a vote he did not have to call this year, and the subject dominating the trail is not the economy, immigration or any of the usual flashpoints of Spanish politics. It is housing. The snap election, set for 29 November after a 24-hour general strike shut down ports, factories and rail lines earlier this month, is unfolding almost entirely in the shadow of nationwide protests over rents and property costs that have made life in Spain's major cities unaffordable for a growing share of its population.

Opinion polls taken as the campaign opened have not been kind to the prime minister. They show the opposition Partido Popular ahead of Sanchez's Socialist Workers' Party, with the PP likely needing its hard-right ally Vox to cross the 176-seat threshold required to govern outright. A PP-Vox coalition would mark one of the sharpest rightward turns in recent Spanish political history, and the possibility is close enough that it has become the central strategic calculation for every other party contesting the vote.

Sanchez's problem is not confined to his conservative opposition. The campaign has also exposed fractures within Spain's progressive camp, after an earlier effort to unite the parties to the left of the Socialists collapsed before it could present a united front. A divided left translates directly into wasted votes under Spain's electoral system, compounding the difficulty Sanchez already faces trying to hold together the coalition arithmetic that has kept him in office through several previous close calls.

That the housing crisis has become the defining issue says something about how acute the problem has grown. Strikes over wages or working conditions are a familiar feature of Spanish political life; a strike broad enough to halt ports, factories and rail networks simultaneously, explicitly over the cost of putting a roof over one's head, signals a crisis that has moved well beyond the usual activist and renter-advocacy circles into something closer to a genuine cross-section of the electorate's daily anxiety.

Why Spanish housing costs escalated this fast

Spain's housing affordability problem has built over several years through a combination of pressures that compound rather than simply add together: short-term holiday-rental platforms converting long-term residential stock in tourist-heavy cities like Barcelona and Madrid into higher-yield short-stay units, foreign buyers purchasing property as investment assets rather than homes, and a construction sector that has not kept pace with urban population growth since the aftermath of Spain's earlier property-market crash left developers and lenders cautious about large-scale new residential projects for years afterward. Each pressure alone might have been manageable; together, concentrated heavily in the cities where Spain's economic opportunity is also concentrated, they have pushed rents and purchase prices well beyond what wage growth has supported, especially for younger Spaniards trying to form independent households for the first time.

That combination also explains why the issue has proven so difficult for any single government to resolve quickly. Short-term rental regulation, foreign investment rules and construction incentives fall under different layers of Spanish governance, from national housing policy down to municipal zoning decisions, meaning a prime minister promising fast relief on housing costs is promising something that genuinely requires coordinated action across multiple levels of government that do not always move at the same pace or share the same political incentives.

What a PP-Vox government would actually change

A coalition between the Partido Popular and Vox would mark a definitive end to the left-leaning governing coalitions that have, in various configurations, run Spain for most of the period since Sanchez first became prime minister. Beyond the specific policy shifts on migration and social issues, the more structural change would be Spain's positioning within the European Union's internal balance between member states pushing for more unified EU-level responses on issues like migration and climate policy, and a growing bloc of states electing governments more sceptical of exactly that kind of centralisation. A rightward shift in Spain, one of the EU's four largest economies by population, would meaningfully strengthen that sceptical bloc's numbers at the European Council table, with consequences for EU policy debates well beyond Spain's own borders.

What happens on 29 November will ripple beyond Spain's own borders. A PP-Vox government would shift one of the European Union's larger economies toward positions on migration and social policy that diverge sharply from the current government's, at a moment when several other EU states are already navigating similar rightward pressure. Sanchez entered this campaign betting that forcing an early vote was better than limping toward a later one on worse terms; whether that bet pays off now rests on whether Spanish voters blame him for the housing crisis or trust him to fix it.
