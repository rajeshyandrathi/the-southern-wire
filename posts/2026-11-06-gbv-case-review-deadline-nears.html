The clock is running out on Ramaphosa's promise to clear South Africa's gender-violence case backlog

Crime Desk

Roughly seven weeks remain before President Cyril Ramaphosa's 90-day deadline for police and prosecutors to review every unresolved murder, attempted murder, rape and other gender-based violence case on their books quietly expires, and advocacy groups who pushed for the order are already asking what happens if the review simply produces another list of cases still going nowhere. The instruction, issued in late September after a string of killings reignited public fury, was unusually specific for a presidential directive: a hard 90-day window for SAPS and the National Prosecuting Authority to account for cases that had stalled, in some instances for years.

The order came with an admission rarely heard from the country's head of state. Ramaphosa acknowledged that government had failed women, a striking concession given gender-based violence had already been formally declared a national disaster the previous year, a designation that was supposed to unlock faster funding and coordination but has, by the president's own account, not translated into cases actually being solved.

The scale of what the review has to work through is difficult to overstate. Police statistics cited in recent reporting put the daily toll at more than 100 reported rape cases and roughly 15 women killed, figures that have led UN Women to describe South Africa's femicide rate as among the highest recorded anywhere in the world. A Reuters analysis found that only around one in ten murders in the country ends in a conviction, a conversion rate that, applied to gender-based violence cases specifically, helps explain why so many families of victims describe the justice system as something to be endured rather than relied upon.

The immediate trigger for Ramaphosa's order was a cluster of killings in and around Johannesburg in which police confirmed nine women had been found dead within a short span, most of them also sexually assaulted, in the same part of the city. Memorial marches and vigils followed, with speakers repeatedly invoking names of victims from years past whose cases had never resulted in an arrest, let alone a trial, a pattern organisers say illustrates precisely the backlog the president's review is meant to confront.

Minister of Women, Youth and Persons with Disabilities Nomakhosazana Meth and Police Minister Firoz Cachalia have both spoken publicly since the order was issued, with Cachalia at one point stating plainly that men should not resort to violence when relationships end badly, a line that drew criticism from activists who argued the moment called for operational commitments rather than moral appeals. With the 90-day window now closing in on its final stretch, the test that matters is not another statement but a public accounting of how many of the stalled cases on police and prosecutors' desks have actually moved, and how many families are still waiting for a phone call that never comes.
