A judge invokes the Senzo Meyiwa trial as a warning — then postpones another murder case by nine months anyway

Crime Desk

The murder trial of music producer Katiso "KT" Molefe has been postponed to July 2027, a delay a Johannesburg High Court judge openly linked to the cautionary example of the Senzo Meyiwa case, even while acknowledging that the postponement was unavoidable. Molefe faces charges in connection with the 2024 killing of Oupa Sefoka, better known as DJ Sumbody, in a case that shares some of the same accused and the same prosecution team as the long-running Meyiwa matter, a structural overlap the court said made simultaneous scheduling impossible.

In explaining the delay, the judge warned against the justice system producing "another Senzo Meyiwa," a case that has become shorthand in South African courtrooms for the damage repeated postponements do to public confidence in prosecutions. The Meyiwa trial, now well into its twelfth year since the footballer's 2014 murder, has been held up by disputed evidence, cellphone record disputes and scheduling conflicts involving the accused, and its reputation as a cautionary tale was invoked here even as the court concluded a delay in the Molefe case was the only realistic option.

The irony is not lost on legal observers tracking both cases. A judge citing one trial's dysfunction as a reason to avoid repeating it, while simultaneously pushing a second trial back by nine months because the first trial's schedule has not cleared, illustrates how thinly stretched the court system and prosecuting authority are when multiple accused and witnesses overlap across cases. Shared prosecutors cannot be in two courtrooms running parallel proceedings, and a shared pool of accused facing related charges complicates any attempt to simply separate the cases and let them proceed independently.

For the Sefoka family, the postponement extends an already painful wait for resolution in a killing that drew national attention both for the victim's prominence in Johannesburg's music scene and for the web of associated cases it has since been tied to. Molefe's legal team has previously argued that the overlapping charges and shared evidence base make a fair, independent hearing of his case difficult to guarantee until the related proceedings have progressed further, an argument the court's own scheduling decision now appears, in practice, to have accepted.

What the case ultimately underscores is a justice system straining under its own caseload of high-profile, interconnected prosecutions, where the machinery meant to deliver timely accountability in one case is being delayed by the unresolved machinery of another. For a public already watching the Meyiwa trial drag toward a second decade, the Molefe postponement is a reminder that the risk the judge warned against is not hypothetical. It is already shaping how long other victims' families will have to wait.
