Cape Town's countdown to AfricaCom tightens as exhibitor numbers swell

Tech Desk

AfricaCom's return to Cape Town is now less than a fortnight away, with organisers confirming the Africa Tech Festival, the broader umbrella event that now houses AfricaCom alongside AfricaTech and a dedicated satellite-connectivity track, will run from 17 to 19 November at the Cape Town International Convention Centre. For an event that has grown steadily since its early years as a telecoms-industry trade show, this year's edition carries an added weight: it lands just as South Africa's own digital infrastructure ambitions, from data centre investment to submarine cable capacity, are under closer scrutiny than usual.

Exhibitor registrations are tracking ahead of last year's event, according to organisers, with growth concentrated less in the legacy telecom operators that have historically anchored the show and more in satellite connectivity providers, fintech infrastructure firms and artificial intelligence startups looking to pitch African deployments of technology built and tested elsewhere. That shift mirrors a broader change in what African digital investment has come to mean over the past two years, less about laying new mobile network coverage and more about what gets built on top of connectivity that, in most major markets, now exists.

For Cape Town and the Western Cape more broadly, the event has become an informal measure of the city's standing as the continent's preferred technology and investment gathering point, a reputation built as much on logistics and hospitality infrastructure as on any specific policy advantage. Provincial and city officials have leaned into that framing in the run-up to this year's event, highlighting the city's role as host for the third consecutive year against competition from Kigali, Lagos and other African cities all actively courting similar gatherings.

The policy conversations expected to dominate side sessions this year include the regulatory treatment of satellite broadband providers now operating across the continent, data localisation requirements that vary sharply between African jurisdictions, and the question of how African governments should approach AI infrastructure investment at a moment when global compute capacity is both scarce and concentrated overwhelmingly outside the continent.

None of that is likely to produce dramatic, headline-grabbing announcements on its own, but dealmaking at AfricaCom has historically happened quietly, in meeting rooms and side conversations rather than on the main stage, and organisers say this year's larger exhibitor footprint has already generated more pre-event meeting requests than any previous edition. For an industry still working out what African digital infrastructure investment looks like in an AI-driven decade, that quiet momentum may matter more than any single plenary session.
