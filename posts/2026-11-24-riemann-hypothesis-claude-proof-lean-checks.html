A computer-checked proof of the Riemann hypothesis, made with an AI, passes its first big test

An Anthropic mathematician says he and the company’s Claude model have proved one of mathematics’ most famous unsolved problems. The proof passes machine checks, but human experts are not yet convinced they understand it.

Tech Desk·Tuesday, 24 November 2026·2 min read

Key points

  • Levent Alpöge announced on 20 November that he believes he and Claude have proved the Riemann hypothesis
  • The proof was released in the Lean proof assistant: about 175,000 files and 17 million lines
  • Lean and Mathlib community leaders said on 21 November they had full confidence it checks
  • Many number theorists are withholding judgment until there is a readable human account

One of the oldest open questions in mathematics may have been answered, and an AI system was part of the work. On Friday 20 November, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, announced that he believes he and the company’s AI model Claude have proved the Riemann hypothesis, IBM Think reported.

The hypothesis, first stated in 1859, concerns the pattern behind the prime numbers. It is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute’s seven Millennium Prize Problems, which carry a $1 million prize. Alpöge says the result also proves a broader version, the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis.

A proof written for computers

Instead of a traditional paper, Alpöge released a formal proof written in Lean, software that checks every logical step. It runs to 175,187 files and about 17 million lines, according to a page maintained by Michael Rubinstein of the University of Waterloo. He also posted a four-page note.

Since the weekend, the proof has passed important checks. MIT’s Andrew Sutherland compiled it first, Storm Media reported, and on 21 November the Lean and Mathlib communities issued a joint statement of full confidence, confirming that the theorem matches the library’s previously vetted statement of the hypothesis. Rubinstein reports that all modules compile, the main theorems use only Lean’s three standard axioms, and an independent checker found no errors in about 1.48 million declarations.

Why mathematicians are cautious

A machine check confirms that the logic holds, not that anyone understands why. Fields Medal winner James Maynard said that if it fully checks out, it is clearly a historic milestone. But NYU’s Maksym Radziwiłł told Quanta that the four-page note contained statements that did not make sense, and Andrew Granville told Nature that a clearer write-up should have come first, according to Storm Media.

Rubinstein’s own human-readable explanation is a working draft that does not yet prove the key step; it reduces the argument to 13 hypotheses. Reports say attempts to have Claude produce a readable account ran past 250,000 pages.

What it means

If the proof stands, it would be one of the biggest results in the history of mathematics, and a striking example of AI doing original research rather than repeating known work. It would also raise a new question for the field: what counts as understanding a proof that no person can read in full. The coming weeks of expert review will decide how it is remembered.
