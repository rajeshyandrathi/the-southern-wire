The AKA murder trial reaches its most combustible stage yet: the defence says the CCTV footage itself can't be trusted

Crime Desk

Nearly four years after Kiernan "AKA" Forbes and his friend Tebello "Tibz" Motsoane were shot dead outside a Durban restaurant, the trial of the seven men accused in their killing has moved from eyewitness recollection into something more adversarial: a direct challenge to the forensic backbone of the state's case. Having spent weeks hearing from club promoters, a road manager and a bodyguard who described the chaos of the shooting itself, the Durban High Court is now hearing defence lawyers argue that the surveillance footage prosecutors say shows one of the accused tailing Forbes from King Shaka Airport cannot be relied upon at all.

The challenge has two prongs. One targets the authenticity of the CCTV itself, with defence counsel pressing on how the footage was obtained, stored and verified before being presented to the court. The other goes after the credibility of the detective who led key parts of the investigation, with an earlier clash between the officer and defence lawyers over the alleged recovery of a firearm already exposing friction in how the evidence trail was documented. Taken together, the two lines of attack are designed to give the court reason to doubt not just individual pieces of evidence but the investigation's overall integrity.

For the state, the stakes in holding this evidence together are considerable. Much of the earlier eyewitness testimony described the chaos and aftermath of the shooting rather than definitively identifying who pulled the trigger, which places unusual weight on the forensic and surveillance evidence to establish the sequence of events that led from the airport to the restaurant on Florida Road. If the CCTV's authenticity is successfully undermined, prosecutors would be left leaning more heavily on witness accounts that the defence has already begun probing for inconsistencies.

The trial's slow grind has itself become part of the story. What began as a case defined by extradition battles and repeated postponements has, over the past two months, settled into a more conventional rhythm of witness testimony and cross-examination, even as the defence's current strategy threatens to drag proceedings back into procedural argument. For a case this closely watched, any ruling on the admissibility of the CCTV evidence will shape not just the remainder of this trial but the standard other high-profile prosecutions in the province are measured against.

Family members and fans who have followed the case since Forbes's death in February 2023 are confronting an uncomfortable reality: a trial finally producing real testimony is also a trial where every piece of that testimony can still be picked apart in open court. Whatever the judge ultimately decides about the footage, the current phase has made clear that convicting the accused will require the state's evidence to withstand exactly the kind of scrutiny it is now facing.
