Putin and Trump sit down in Shenzhen. Ukraine’s war doesn’t pause for it.

World Desk

Away from the main hall of the APEC summit in Shenzhen, Vladimir Putin and Donald Trump found time this week for the conversation that matters most to the rest of the world: what, if anything, ends the war in Ukraine. It is the highest-profile encounter between the two leaders since a trilateral round in Abu Dhabi brought Russian, Ukrainian and American negotiators into the same room for the first time since February, and expectations on both sides have been carefully managed downward.

The gap that talks have to close has not narrowed in any way that shows up in the public record. Russia's delegation in Abu Dhabi insisted that a security-architecture settlement come before any discussion of territory, a sequencing choice that lets Moscow avoid conceding anything concrete while still appearing to negotiate. On the core demand, Putin has not shifted: Russian forces should receive the parts of Luhansk, Donetsk, Zaporizhzhia and Kherson they have not managed to seize in nearly four years of fighting, handed over rather than fought for.

The fighting has made the backdrop to Shenzhen impossible to ignore. Russia sent a wave of roughly three dozen ballistic missiles into Ukraine earlier this month, some reportedly North Korean-manufactured, aimed squarely at energy infrastructure as temperatures drop. Ukraine, for its part, claimed a single-day record of roughly 3,000 Russian casualties in fighting around the eastern front, a figure impossible to verify independently but consistent with the grinding, high-casualty character the war has taken on.

That is the gap Trump and Putin were trying to talk across in Shenzhen: a battlefield that keeps producing casualties and infrastructure damage at a steady rate, and a negotiating table where the two sides are not yet discussing the same question. Washington wants a framework it can call a deal. Moscow wants territory it could not take by force, delivered instead by diplomacy. Kyiv, absent from the room in Shenzhen, has to live with whatever its two more powerful interlocutors decide is an acceptable starting point.

Nothing from the Shenzhen sidelines suggests that dynamic broke in a new direction. The likeliest outcome, on the evidence so far, is a vague commitment to keep talking, dressed up as progress, while Russia's winter campaign against Ukraine's grid continues on its own schedule regardless of what gets said in conference rooms.
