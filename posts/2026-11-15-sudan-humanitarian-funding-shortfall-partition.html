Sudan's war now has two governments and one underfunded rescue plan

World Desk

Sudan's de facto partition into rival governments has not paused the humanitarian emergency underneath it; if anything, the split has made coordinating a response harder. The country's 2026 humanitarian response plan calls for $2.9 billion to reach people displaced and starved by a war now well into its third year. As of the start of this month, it had received just 47% of that total, leaving aid agencies trying to stretch barely half the money they say the crisis actually requires across a country where the front lines, and now the governing authorities, have split in two.

The funding gap is not an abstraction measured only in spreadsheets. It shows up in ration cuts at camps already overcrowded by people who fled the Rapid Support Forces' sweep through Darfur's state capitals, in clinics that cannot restock basic medicine, and in a aid pipeline that has to negotiate access separately with authorities on each side of a partition line that didn't exist when this year's response plan was first drafted. Agencies built their funding ask around the war as it looked at the start of the year. The war that exists now, with two governments each claiming legitimacy over different stretches of territory, is a different operating environment entirely, and the money has not caught up to that change.

El-Fasher's fall to the RSF three weeks ago remains the clearest marker of how the ground has shifted. Its capture completed the RSF's sweep of Darfur's state capitals and, by most accounts, hardened the practical reality of partition rather than created it outright; the fighting had already been trending toward a split country for months. What El-Fasher changed was the humanitarian math specifically, adding another displaced population to camps that were already over capacity and already running short of the aid this underfunded response plan was supposed to provide.

International donors have their own reasons for the shortfall, few of them unique to Sudan. Humanitarian budgets worldwide are stretched across Gaza, Ukraine, and a growing list of crises competing for the same finite pool of government aid money, and Sudan, despite the scale of displacement and the documented atrocities on both sides of the conflict, has struggled all year to command the sustained international attention that tends to precede sustained international funding. A war without a single clear villain, now without even a single government to negotiate with, is a harder story to keep in front of donors than one with a clean narrative.

None of that changes what the 53% funding gap means on the ground: fewer supplies, further stretched, for a population that did not choose the partition now complicating every attempt to reach them. Aid agencies will keep making the case for the remaining $1.5 billion through the end of the year. Whether that case lands any differently than it has for the past eleven months is, at this point, mostly a question of whether the rest of the world is still listening.
