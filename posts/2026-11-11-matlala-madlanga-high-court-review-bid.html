Two clocks, one Thursday: Matlala's lawyers race to the High Court before his trial resumes

Crime Desk

Advocate Annelene van den Heever is now formally preparing the papers that will decide whether Fezekile "Cat" Matlala's collapsed plea statement can ever be used against him at the Madlanga Commission. Her instruction is to take Justice Mbuyiseli Madlanga's ruling — that the statement can be put to her client despite the plea deal behind it having fallen through — on urgent review before a High Court judge, and to do it fast enough to matter before the Commission hears any more of his testimony.

Speed matters because Madlanga built an unusual safety valve into his own ruling. The Commission will keep going, he said, but it will stop the moment a court either reviews and sets aside his decision or interdicts the hearing outright, and it will not weigh the disputed evidence at all until that review is finally resolved. That leaves Matlala's legal team racing not against a deadline the Commission set, but against the Commission's own continuing momentum — every day without a court order is a day the testimony keeps being heard, even if it can't yet be used.

Van den Heever's team has added a second, less noticed request alongside the review application: that the proceedings touching on the plea statement be heard in camera, away from public and media view. It is a request that only makes sense if the underlying fear is less about legal principle than about what the statement itself says, and who it implicates — a detail that has done more to fuel speculation around the Commission's corridors than the review application itself.

Evidence leader Advocate Mahlape Sello has pushed back hard on the defence's framing of the whole episode, arguing in her own submissions that Matlala's team never lodged a formal objection or approached a court when the National Prosecuting Authority first handed the statement over to the Commission. If that timeline holds up under scrutiny, it undercuts the idea that Matlala's camp has been fighting this fairly from the start, rather than only once the material started to look damaging.

All of this is unfolding in the same week that Matlala's actual criminal case — the attempted-murder trial over three assassination attempts, the one whose own collapsed plea deal is now at the centre of the Commission fight — resumes at the High Court on Thursday. Forensic pathologist Professor Gert Saayman's evidence on co-accused Musa Kekana's assault allegations will pick back up exactly as the review application is supposed to be landing in front of a judge, meaning Thursday now carries two separate, intertwined reckonings for Matlala rather than one.

Whichever judge draws the review application will be deciding something well beyond one man's discomfort at having his own words read back to him. A ruling that plea statements survive a collapsed deal and can still be used against the person who gave them would change the calculation for every accused person weighing whether to cooperate with prosecutors in South Africa — in the Madlanga Commission's own long list of outstanding witnesses, and far beyond it.
