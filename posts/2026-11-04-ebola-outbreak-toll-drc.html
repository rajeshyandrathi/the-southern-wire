Africa's latest Ebola outbreak crosses 5,300 deaths, with Congo bearing the brunt

World Desk·Wednesday, 4 November 2026

The death toll from the current Ebola outbreak sweeping parts of Central Africa has passed 5,300, health officials confirmed this week, making it one of the deadliest filovirus events the region has recorded in years. The Democratic Republic of the Congo has borne the overwhelming majority of cases, a cruel coincidence for a country already absorbing the human cost of M23's advance through its eastern provinces and the political chaos unleashed by Joseph Kabila's new alliance with the rebel group.

Public health responders describe a familiar and frustrating pattern: the outbreak has spread fastest in areas where conflict has already degraded health infrastructure, disrupted supply chains for medical equipment, and made contact tracing dangerous or outright impossible in rebel-held territory. Treating Ebola requires rapid isolation of cases and careful tracing of everyone an infected person has contact with, work that becomes exponentially harder in regions where armed groups control movement and aid organisations cannot guarantee safe access.

International health agencies have mobilised emergency funding and personnel, drawing on lessons from previous DRC outbreaks, including the country's own prior experience containing the virus in less turbulent periods. But officials on the ground say the scale of displacement caused by the eastern conflict has complicated even basic containment measures, with populations moving unpredictably across unstable front lines in ways that make modelling the outbreak's spread far less reliable than in previous responses.

The toll has also reignited longstanding criticism of how slowly international resources mobilise for health crises in active conflict zones, compared with outbreaks in more stable settings. Aid organisations have pointed out that every week of delayed access allows the virus more opportunity to move between communities that already have reason to distrust outside intervention, after years of armed groups operating with relative impunity in the same areas.

With no clear end in sight to either the fighting or the outbreak it is intertwined with, health workers are bracing for the death toll to climb further before it stabilises. For a region already carrying the weight of Africa's largest active armed conflict, the Ebola crisis has become a second catastrophe layered directly on top of the first, each one making the other harder to resolve.
