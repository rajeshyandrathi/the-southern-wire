Poland prepares six murder charges as prosecutors call the Krakow square shooting an act of terrorism

World Desk

Polish prosecutors opened a formal terrorism investigation into Saturday's mass shooting in Krakow's historic Rynek Glowny, the main market square, and are preparing to file six murder charges against the 24-year-old suspect, who police shot and wounded before taking him into custody. National Prosecutor's Office spokesperson Tomasz Waszczuk said early findings support a motive investigators had already begun to suspect: that the attacker, a Polish national with documented ties to a legal shooting club, acted out of far-right ideological conviction rather than any personal grievance against those he killed.

The toll from Saturday's attack stands at six dead and fifteen wounded, figures that make it one of Poland's deadliest acts of mass violence in years, striking at a location whose symbolic weight is difficult to overstate. Krakow's main square sits at the historical and tourist heart of one of Europe's best-preserved medieval cities, filled on any given Saturday with a mix of locals, visitors and vendors who had no reason to expect anything beyond an ordinary weekend afternoon. Among the dead is a 32-year-old British national, the only victim whose identity authorities had confirmed and released publicly by Sunday; at least one other victim, a woman, remained unidentified as investigators worked to notify families.

What has deepened the horror of the case is what investigators found at the suspect's family home in Ostrowiec Swietokrzyski, roughly 150 kilometres from Krakow: the bodies of his father, mother and sister. Local officials said seven additional weapons were recovered from the property, bringing the total number of firearms seized in connection with the case to eleven — a cache that raises uncomfortable questions about how a private individual accumulated that many weapons without triggering any apparent intervention, and about whether the killings at home preceded or were somehow connected to the planning of Saturday's attack in the square.

Poland's political leadership moved quickly to frame the attack in the starkest available terms. Prime Minister Donald Tusk called it terrorism without qualification, and the country's defence minister used identical language, a rare instance of immediate, unanimous political framing that reflects both the scale of the casualties and the symbolic target chosen. That consensus will likely shape how the case proceeds through Poland's courts, where a terrorism designation carries different evidentiary standards and sentencing implications than an ordinary multiple-murder charge would.

For a country that has not faced an attack of this scale and apparent ideological motivation in recent memory, the shooting has prompted the kind of national reckoning that mass violence in public space tends to provoke everywhere it occurs: renewed scrutiny of gun-club vetting procedures, questions about whether warning signs around the suspect's radicalisation were visible and missed, and a period of public mourning in a city more accustomed to tourists photographing its medieval architecture than laying flowers at a crime scene.
