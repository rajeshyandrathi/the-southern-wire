That automatic 10% on your restaurant bill? The tribunal says it breaks the law.

The National Consumer Tribunal has ruled that a Pretoria restaurant’s compulsory 10% service charge breached the Consumer Protection Act. Here is what it means the next time you eat out.

Lifestyle Desk·Friday, 20 November 2026·2 min read

Key points

  • National Consumer Tribunal ruled against a compulsory 10% service charge
  • The case involved a restaurant in Pretoria
  • The charge breached the Consumer Protection Act
  • Tipping is still allowed; it just cannot be forced

Many diners have seen it: a bill that arrives with a 10% service charge already added, sometimes labelled discretionary, sometimes not. The National Consumer Tribunal has now ruled that this practice, at least as one Pretoria restaurant applied it, is against the law.

The case involved the Tribeca Standard restaurant in Pretoria. The tribunal found that automatically adding the charge breached the Consumer Protection Act and declared it prohibited conduct. It pointed to a section of the Act that stops a supplier from making a consumer pay more than the displayed price for goods or services.

In plain terms, the price on the menu is the price. A business cannot quietly add an extra percentage on top and treat it as compulsory. The restaurant in this case cooperated with the tribunal process.

This does not ban tipping, and it does not stop restaurants from suggesting a tip. What it does is put the choice back with the customer. If a charge is on your bill that you did not agree to, you are entitled to ask for it to be removed and to tip what you think the service deserves. Many waiters rely heavily on tips, so the ruling is about fairness and transparency rather than paying less for good service.

With the festive season and year-end functions about to start, it is a good time to check your bill before you pay. If a business refuses to remove a charge you did not agree to, complaints can be taken to the Consumer Goods and Services Ombud or the National Consumer Commission.

What you can do

Read the bill before you pay, especially for large groups, where many restaurants have traditionally added a set percentage. If there is a service charge you did not agree to, politely ask for it to be removed. You can then add a tip yourself, at whatever level you think is fair.

Look at the menu as well. If a restaurant wants to apply a charge, the clearer and more upfront it is, the less likely it is to cause disputes. A charge added without warning is the kind of practice the tribunal has now said is not allowed.

Why it matters for waiters

In South Africa, many waiters earn low basic wages and depend heavily on tips. Automatic service charges were often defended as a way to protect staff income, especially with large tables. The ruling does not change how much diners can choose to tip, and in practice most South Africans still tip around 10% to 15% for good service.

Restaurants that relied on automatic charges may now need to look at other ways of making sure staff are fairly paid, such as building it into menu prices.
