From a R7 ride to a 32-train fleet: Gauteng's commuter rail is quietly having its best year in a decade

Lifestyle Desk

For commuters who have spent years defaulting to minibus taxis and e-hailing apps simply because the trains could not be relied upon, Gauteng's rail network is having an unusually good year. Transport Minister Barbara Creecy took a well-publicised R7 ride on PRASA's Mabopane line last month to showcase upgrades to the corridor, and the numbers behind the photo opportunity are genuinely striking: trips on the Mabopane-to-Pretoria route rose to 11,144 in the 2025/26 financial year, up from just 613 trips five years earlier, a revival that reflects both restored infrastructure and a public slowly willing to trust the service again.

The Gautrain, long the more reliable but far more expensive half of the province's rail story, is undergoing its own transformation after the completion of a new 15-year operator appointment process. The fleet is set to expand from 24 to 32 four-car trains, a roughly one-third increase in capacity that should ease the overcrowding commuters have complained about on peak-hour services for years, particularly on the Sandton-to-Marlboro and airport-bound routes that carry the bulk of daily business travel. Planned service improvements also include more frequent off-peak weekday trains and longer daily operating hours, changes aimed squarely at commuters whose work schedules do not neatly match the system's traditional peak-hour focus.

What makes this moment notable is the contrast with where both services were only a few years ago. PRASA's long-distance Shosholoza Meyl network remains effectively suspended, with a limited restart on four routes not expected until 2027 and funding for new locomotives still unresolved, a reminder that the recovery underway on commuter corridors has not yet extended to the long-distance services that once connected smaller towns to the country's major centres. The Mabopane revival and the Gautrain expansion are real, but they are also happening against a backdrop of a rail operator still working through decades of accumulated underinvestment elsewhere in its network.

For ordinary commuters, the practical appeal is straightforward: a R7 train ride that actually runs on schedule is dramatically cheaper than the taxi fares and fuel costs that have filled the gap left by unreliable rail over the past several years, at a moment when households are already absorbing rising transport inflation elsewhere in the economy. Every passenger who shifts back to rail from a minibus taxi or a private car also takes pressure off Gauteng's congested road network, a secondary benefit provincial transport planners are counting on as they push the recovery further.

Whether the improvements hold is the real test still ahead. South African commuters have been promised rail revivals before, only to watch them stall once the initial investment and political attention moved elsewhere. For now, the Mabopane numbers and the Gautrain's expanded fleet order represent the most concrete evidence in years that this time, the recovery might actually be built to last.
