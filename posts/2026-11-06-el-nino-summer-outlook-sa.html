Forecasters warn a drying El Nino is building for South Africa's summer, just as farmers banked on a wet season

Weather Desk

Just a year after a La Nina pattern delivered the kind of widespread, welcome rain that helped South African farmers recover from a punishing earlier drought, seasonal forecasters are now warning that the climate system is swinging the other way. The latest outlook linked to the South African Weather Service points toward a hotter-than-normal summer, with an increasingly strong El Nino raising the likelihood of drier conditions across large parts of the country through the critical October-to-December planting window and beyond.

The regional picture reinforces the local one. A consensus forecast produced through the Southern African Regional Climate Outlook Forum process expects a generally drier-than-normal start to the season across much of the Southern African Development Community region, with below-normal rainfall odds persisting into early 2027 for parts of southern Africa. For a region where agricultural output and water security are both tightly coupled to a handful of summer rainfall months, a regional consensus pointing toward dryness carries weight well beyond any single country's borders.

Academic modelling adds further detail to the picture. Seasonal forecasting work out of the University of Pretoria has pointed to the likely development of a very strong El Nino state during the 2026/27 summer, with below-normal rainfall emerging as the dominant signal across much of South Africa's summer rainfall region by early summer. Earlier modelling from April had already flagged the transition into El Nino as likely, in line with similar signals from the Australian Bureau of Meteorology and other international forecasting centres that track the same Pacific Ocean temperature patterns driving the shift.

The stakes of that transition are concrete rather than abstract for South African agriculture. The El Nino drought that struck in the 2023/24 growing season slashed white maize output by roughly a quarter, a shock that rippled through food prices and rural livelihoods for well over a year afterward. The La Nina that followed in 2025/26 was, in that context, as much a recovery event as a routine weather pattern, replenishing dam levels and supporting a far stronger harvest than the drought years that preceded it. A swing back toward El Nino so soon after that recovery raises the uncomfortable possibility of South African farmers facing another bad cycle before fully banking the gains of the good one.

None of this means a dry summer is guaranteed. Seasonal outlooks describe probabilities and dominant signals rather than certainties, and South Africa's summer rainfall has surprised forecasters in both directions before. But with the regional consensus, the university modelling and the SAWS-linked outlook all currently pointing in the same direction, farmers' organisations say they are already factoring the risk into planting decisions for the season ahead, a hedge against a repeat of the kind of shortfall the country is still only a couple of years removed from.
