South Africa's greatest Olympian is swimming for someone else now

Sport Desk

Tatjana Smith, the breaststroke swimmer who gave South Africa four Olympic medals across two Games, has switched her competitive allegiance to the Netherlands. The announcement landed this week and confirms what had been rumoured for months: the country's most decorated Olympian will no longer swim under the green and gold, with World Aquatics already signing off on the change of sporting nationality. She is set to debut for her new country at the World Short Course Championships in Beijing from 1 to 6 December.

Smith has not hidden her reasoning. She has pointed to the financial and practical difficulties of sustaining an elite swimming career from inside South Africa's funding structures, and to a running dispute with Swimming South Africa over bonus payments she says were never properly settled after her Paris performances. Put plainly, the arithmetic of staying stopped making sense. The switch is possible because Smith holds dual citizenship, Dutch as well as South African, which let her make the change without the yearslong residency fights that usually trap athletes who want to swim for a new flag.

What she's leaving behind is a career few South African athletes in any sport can match. Competing under her maiden name, Schoenmaker, she won 100m breaststroke silver and 200m breaststroke gold at the Tokyo Olympics in 2020, breaking the world record in the 200m final. In Paris four years later, now racing as Smith, she added a 100m breaststroke gold and another silver, taking her tally to two golds and two silvers across two Games and cementing her as South Africa's single most successful Olympian of any era.

The practical details of the switch are less dramatic than the symbolism. Smith will represent the Netherlands in competition but intends to keep living and training in South Africa, alongside the coaching team that built her career, rather than relocating to chase her new flag. The timing points squarely at Los Angeles 2028, where she would arrive as a Dutch athlete inside the same training environment that produced four Olympic medals the first time around.

The federation she is leaving has had a rough year by most accounts, and South Africa's Department of Sport, Arts and Culture has already said it is aware of problems within Swimming South Africa, without yet detailing what, if anything, it intends to do about them. For now the department's acknowledgement sits alongside Smith's departure as the two public signals that something in the sport's administration needs fixing, with no resolution yet in sight for the swimmers still racing under the federation she just left.
