Crime & Courts

Nine men in a Kempton Park courtroom are the visible edge of a much larger pipeline

Crime Desk

The nine men arrested at OR Tambo and a Bruma hotel now face the Kempton Park Magistrates' Court under the Regulation of Foreign Military Assistance Act, charged over an alleged attempt to travel from South Africa to fight in Russia's war on Ukraine. But investigators and regional reporting increasingly point to something larger than nine individual decisions: a decentralised recruitment network spanning Zimbabwe, South Africa and Russia that has, by Zimbabwean authorities' own estimates, moved far more than a handful of men toward the front lines over the past year.

Zimbabwe's own courts have been processing a parallel stream of recruitment cases. A Harare man was arrested earlier this year while escorting a recruit toward South Africa as the first leg of a journey meant to end in Russia, and a separate case in Hwange involved a man accused of trafficking five Zimbabweans under the pretext of firefighting jobs, only for the recruits to be subjected to a week of firearms training and deployed to fight. Zimbabwean authorities have said at least fifteen of their citizens have been killed in the war, with dozens more unaccounted for and believed to still be serving.

What makes these networks hard to shut down is precisely their decentralised structure. Recruiters operate through informal contacts rather than a single organised chain of command, offering desperate men construction, security or firefighting contracts that only reveal their true destination once travel documents and visas are already in motion. By the time recruits realise what they have agreed to, freedom of movement has often already been restricted, leaving them with little practical ability to back out even if they wanted to.

South Africa's own investigations have occasionally brushed up against far more prominent names, including scrutiny of whether politically connected figures played any role in facilitating travel for South African men allegedly recruited to fight for Russia. None of those broader allegations have produced charges to match the nine men now before the Kempton Park court, and officials have so far declined to confirm which side of the war the group was allegedly headed toward, a detail that continues to frustrate reporters covering the case.

The scale of the repatriation problem sitting behind all of this is what should worry policymakers most. Zimbabwean authorities have said more than a thousand of their citizens are awaiting repatriation from various points along these routes, a number that dwarfs the handful of criminal cases that have actually made it to court. For every recruit intercepted at an airport or a hotel room, the networks behind him appear to keep operating, adapting their cover stories and their routes faster than prosecutors in either country have managed to build cases against the people actually running them.
