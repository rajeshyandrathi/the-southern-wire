TikTok arrest in KwaMashu lands in the middle of a bigger fight over who leads the Hawks

Crime Desk·Monday, 2 November 2026

A 38-year-old man was arrested in KwaMashu on Monday after police say he posted a TikTok video accusing KwaZulu-Natal police commissioner Lieutenant-General Nhlanhla Mkhwanazi of standing by while officers allegedly assaulted protesters at a march. Provincial police spokesperson Colonel Robert Netshiunda said investigators traced the man to Princess Magogo Stadium after identifying what they called possible criminal elements in the clip — language directed at Mkhwanazi that police are treating as both incitement to violence and crimen injuria. He was due to appear in the Durban Magistrate's Court later on Monday.

On its own, it's the kind of case that passes through the courts with barely a ripple: a provincial task team, a single suspect, a video pulled offline. What makes it land harder is the timing. It arrives less than a week after acting Police Minister Firoz Cachalia formally asked Cabinet to approve Mkhwanazi as the new national head of the Hawks, the Directorate for Priority Crime Investigation, a post that has sat empty since Lieutenant-General Godfrey Lebeya retired in May 2025. Cachalia has said he wants Mkhwanazi's experience trained on the country's crime cartels rather than wait for a Cabinet sitting expected only toward the end of January.

That nomination has split opinion sharply. Scopa chair Songezo Zibi has called Mkhwanazi potentially the best DPCI appointment yet, while parliament's police oversight chair Ian Cameron has urged that his fit be judged against the Hawks' specific mandate rather than his general reputation. Others, including criminal law expert Dr Lennit Max, argue his skills would be better spent as national police commissioner when General Fannie Masemola's term ends in 2027. Security consultant Paul O'Sullivan, acting for Forensics for Justice, has gone further still, threatening to challenge the appointment in court on the grounds that Mkhwanazi fails the SAPS Act's "fit and proper person" test — a claim tangled up in a long-running personal feud between the two men that has already produced competing defamation suits.

It's against that backdrop that a single TikTok clip becomes a flashpoint rather than a footnote. The video's core accusation — that Mkhwanazi failed to rein in his own officers during a protest — speaks directly to the question Parliament is now being asked to answer: whether the man set to lead South Africa's most prominent crime-fighting unit is also, in practice, accountable for how his province's police conduct themselves in the field. Police have not disclosed which march the video refers to, but KwaZulu-Natal has seen a string of contested demonstrations in recent weeks, including protest action connected to the "March and March" movement that has already drawn court applications seeking to restrict it.

For now, the case against the KwaMashu man is a straightforward one on the police's account — identify, trace, charge. But it lands in a province, and a week, where almost nothing involving Mkhwanazi's name is being read as straightforward. With his Hawks appointment still awaiting Cabinet sign-off and a legal challenge openly threatened, how this prosecution unfolds — and how visibly it is pursued — will be read by many as a signal of how the next phase of South Africa's crime-fighting leadership intends to treat criticism of its own conduct.
