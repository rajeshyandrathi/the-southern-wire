Netflix's FTX drama lands this month, and it's asking audiences to find Sam Bankman-Fried sympathetic

Culture Desk

The Altruists, Netflix's eight-episode dramatisation of the FTX collapse, premieres on 19 November, and the casting alone tells you how the show intends to be read. Anthony Boyle, coming off a run of roles playing men whose charm outpaces their judgement, takes on Sam Bankman-Fried. Emmy winner Julia Garner plays Caroline Ellison, the former Alameda Research chief executive whose testimony helped convict him. The production company behind it is Higher Ground, founded by Barack and Michelle Obama, a pedigree that signals prestige-drama ambitions for a story that, three years on from FTX's implosion, could easily have been told as straightforward tabloid spectacle instead.

That choice of title is doing real work. "The Altruists" is not a neutral label for a group of people who presided over the loss of roughly $8 billion in customer funds; it is lifted from the effective altruism movement Bankman-Fried publicly aligned himself with, the philosophy that argued extreme wealth accumulation could be justified, even morally required, if enough of it was eventually redirected toward solving the world's biggest problems. The show's bet, in other words, is that the most interesting version of this story is not simply "fraud happened" but "people convinced themselves their fraud was a form of good," and that the gap between those two framings is where the drama actually lives.

Filming began in Vancouver in mid-2025, and the show arrives into a culture that has already consumed the FTX collapse from almost every other angle: congressional hearings, a bestselling book, a trial covered gavel-to-gavel by financial journalists, and endless explainer content attempting to untangle how a crypto exchange run largely by people in their twenties and thirties came to hold custody of other people's billions with so little apparent oversight. What a scripted drama can do that straight reporting cannot is inhabit the interior logic of the people involved — not excuse it, but make legible how someone arrives at decisions that look, from the outside, like obvious fraud.

Whether audiences extend Bankman-Fried any sympathy at all remains the show's central gamble, and an uncomfortable one. Victims of the collapse, many of whom lost savings they will likely never fully recover, have been vocal in past cycles of FTX-related content about resenting any framing that risks humanizing the people responsible, and a high-profile Netflix drama with sympathetic casting and a title drawn from the perpetrators' own self-justifying vocabulary is likely to reignite exactly that tension when it streams.

Regardless of how it lands critically, The Altruists slots into a crowded November for Netflix, which is simultaneously pushing a rare multi-week theatrical run for Greta Gerwig's Narnia adaptation and continuing its steady drumbeat of true-crime and finance-scandal programming — a genre the platform has leaned on heavily enough that this is, by some counts, its fourth or fifth major scripted treatment of a real-world financial collapse in as many years.
