The first results land, and South Africa's municipal map comes back in pieces

The Southern Wire Desk·Johannesburg

By the early hours of Thursday morning, with only a few percentage points of South Africa's 23,696 voting districts captured on the Independent Electoral Commission's results system, the one thing the country's municipal election had definitively produced was uncertainty. Early proportional-representation figures showed the ANC and the DA separated by only a few points nationally, but that topline number masked a far messier picture underneath it: a patchwork of metros and towns where no single party was anywhere close to the 50 percent that would spare them a coalition negotiation.

Johannesburg, the country's largest and most closely watched municipality, offered the clearest early signal of how fractured the result would be. The DA was ahead in the city's partial count, but nowhere near a majority, which all but guarantees another round of the horse-trading that has defined Johannesburg's council since 2016. Tshwane produced the night's biggest surprise: ActionSA, which spent the campaign positioning itself as a disciplined, service-delivery-focused alternative to the bigger parties, was running well ahead of its 2021 showing in the capital's early returns, enough to make it a genuine kingmaker rather than a minor player in whatever coalition eventually forms there.

KwaZulu-Natal told a different story again. The MK Party, barely two years old as a political force, was leading the count in the province in the small sample of districts counted so far, a result that would have been unthinkable before Jacob Zuma's party upended the province's politics in 2024. If that lead holds as more rural and township districts report, it would hand MK Party genuine control over metro and district councils in the province for the first time, reshaping a political landscape the ANC dominated for three decades.

The IEC has been at pains to stress that all of this is provisional. Counting officers are working through urban wards with complex, multi-page ballots last, which means metros will always report slower and later than smaller municipalities, and early leads built on a handful of districts can evaporate or reverse as the count deepens. The commission has until next Wednesday, 11 November, to declare final results, a legal deadline it has never missed, though officials have quietly acknowledged that close results in several of the metros that matter most economically could stretch the process close to that limit.

What is already clear, whatever the final arithmetic, is that South Africa is entering another cycle of coalition government in precisely the municipalities with the biggest budgets and the highest stakes. For the Government of National Unity in Pretoria, a weak showing by either of its two largest partners in cities each is relying on to prove it can govern would sharpen tensions that have simmered since the coalition's founding. For residents of Johannesburg, Tshwane and Ekurhuleni, it means the familiar, exhausting ritual of mayoral votes, motions of no confidence and multi-party deals is about to begin all over again, just as it has after almost every election since 2016.
