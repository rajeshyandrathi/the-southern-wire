A trial-within-a-trial swallows another week of the Matlala hitman case

Crime Desk

The Johannesburg High Court case against Peter "Cat" Matlala has not reached a single charge on its own indictment yet, because it is still arguing about how some of the evidence against him was obtained. Matlala, his wife Tsakani Matlala, Tiego Floyd Mabusela, Musa Kekana and Nthabiseng Nzama face a combined 25 charges tied to three separate assassination attempts between 2022 and 2024, targeting Matlala's ex-girlfriend Tebogo Thobejane, businessman Joe Sibanyoni and music producer Seunkie Mokubung, known professionally as DJ Vettys. All five remain in custody; bail has been refused across the board. But before any of that can be tested, the court is working through a trial-within-a-trial triggered by co-accused Musa Kekana, who alleges police assaulted and suffocated him during his arrest in order to extract a confession.

That side proceeding is not a formality. If Kekana's account holds up, any statement extracted under those conditions becomes inadmissible, and the state's case against him — and potentially against his co-accused, depending on how the evidence is linked — could lose some of its foundation before the main trial even properly begins. Forensic pathologist Professor Gert Saayman has been giving evidence on the physical findings relevant to the assault claim, in what amounts to a medical cross-examination of how an arrest was actually conducted rather than what the accused are alleged to have done afterward.

The stakes for the five accused are hard to overstate. Prosecutors have indicated the charges carry a combined exposure of more than 300 years in prison if all five are convicted on every count, a figure that reflects not one crime but three coordinated attempts on three different lives over two years. That is the case the state eventually wants to put before the court. For now, though, the proceedings remain fixed on a narrower and more technical question: whether the manner of Kekana's arrest contaminated the evidence that followed it.

It is the kind of pre-trial argument that rarely makes for dramatic headlines but routinely determines how a case plays out once it finally reaches the merits. South African courts have grown more willing in recent years to exclude evidence obtained through improper arrest procedures, and prosecutors know it; a successful challenge here would not collapse the case outright, but it could force the state to rebuild parts of it around whatever evidence survives untainted.

There is no date yet for when the trial-within-a-trial will conclude, let alone when the main proceedings against Matlala and his co-accused will formally open. For three people who survived attempts on their lives and are still waiting for a verdict, and for five accused who have now spent years in custody without one, the court's calendar remains the only clock that matters, and it is moving slowly.
