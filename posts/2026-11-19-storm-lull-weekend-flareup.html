Weather & Snow

The storms go quiet today. SAWS says don’t get comfortable.

Weather Desk

For five straight days the Highveld has been taking turns in the blast zone. Gauteng, the northern Free State, the south-eastern North West and Mpumalanga each spent part of this week under a Yellow Level 2 severe thunderstorm warning, the kind that comes with explicit language about localised flooding, hail, damaging winds and the sort of lightning that cuts power to a suburb for an afternoon. Wednesday's system was the roughest of the run, rolling through Gauteng's central and southern municipalities with a high chance of the kind of storm that strips roof sheeting and drowns an underpass in twenty minutes.

Thursday brings something South Africans have had little of lately: a pause. No severe weather is on the board for today, and after a week of checking the sky every time the wind picked up, that is itself the story. But treat it as an intermission, not a conclusion. The same instability that has been loading up over the interior each afternoon hasn't gone anywhere; forecasters are already watching a fresh system that looks set to push thunderstorms back into severe territory over the weekend, likely tracking across the same Free State, North West and Gauteng corridor that has absorbed most of this month's damage.

While the east has been getting drenched in bursts, the west has been quietly cooking. Extremely high fire danger has been sitting over the western Free State, the western North West, the entirety of the Northern Cape and parts of the Western Cape for over a week now, with very hot to extremely hot conditions baked into the same stretch. That combination — tinder-dry vegetation and temperatures that don't give the land a chance to recover overnight — hasn't eased just because Gauteng's storms have.

This is what an El Niño-leaning summer looks like on the ground: not a single dramatic event but a pattern of whiplash, violent convective storms in one half of the country colliding with weeks of fire-weather in the other, sometimes in the same forty-eight hours. The provinces catching the thunderstorms are the lucky ones in one sense, but hail and flash flooding carry their own repair bill, from stripped farmland to flooded informal settlements where drainage was never built for this kind of rainfall intensity.

None of it resolves today. Thursday's calm is the kind forecasters describe carefully, because the same moisture and heat that built this week's storms is still parked over the subcontinent, waiting for the next trigger. For now, the advice out of SAWS hasn't really changed in a fortnight: stay alert to lightning if you're outdoors in the east, stay fire-conscious if you're anywhere near the tinder-dry west, and don't assume a quiet afternoon means a quiet weekend.
