Cape Town is getting its first-ever direct flight to Australia

Lifestyle Desk

For the first time in the history of commercial aviation between South Africa and Australia, travellers will soon be able to fly nonstop between the two countries. Qantas has confirmed it will launch a direct Sydney-to-Cape Town service from 19 October 2027, a seasonal route running three times weekly through to March 2028, and tickets went on sale earlier this month. Return economy fares start from AU$2,470, putting the mother city within a single, if lengthy, flight of Australia's largest market for the first time ever.

Cape Town Air Access, the route-development partnership run through Wesgro, the city and province's tourism and investment agency, has been lobbying for exactly this kind of connection for years, describing Australia as one of Cape Town's largest genuinely unserved travel markets. The agency's own projections suggest the new route will add close to 30,000 additional seats a year between the two countries, a meaningful jump in capacity for a corridor that has, until now, forced every Cape Town-bound Australian traveller through at least one, usually two, connecting flights via the Middle East or Asia.

The Qantas announcement is not arriving in isolation. Air France has confirmed it will resume its Cape Town-Paris service for the 2026/27 summer season, increasing frequency to five flights a week from 1 November through to the end of March, with departures timed from Cape Town at 08:30. Taken together, the two announcements point to a city positioning itself more aggressively as a long-haul hub, rather than simply a seasonal add-on to itineraries built around Johannesburg.

Anyone planning around either route should keep South Africa's sharply seasonal international flight pattern in mind. European and, now, Australian long-haul capacity into Cape Town concentrates heavily in the city's own summer months, broadly November through March, when the weather that makes Cape Town worth the long flight is also at its best. Outside that window, the same routes often thin out or disappear entirely until the next international travel season rolls around.

Why this route took so long to exist

The absence of a direct Sydney-Cape Town service was never simply an oversight; ultra-long-haul routes like this one sit at the extreme edge of what commercial aircraft can fly non-stop, and airlines only commit to them once a combination of aircraft range, projected passenger demand and fuel economics all line up simultaneously. A seasonal three-times-weekly service, rather than a year-round daily one, reflects exactly that kind of cautious first step: Qantas testing genuine demand on a route this long and this expensive to operate before committing to the kind of permanent, high-frequency service that would signal full confidence in the corridor's commercial viability. Newer long-range aircraft entering global fleets in recent years have made routes like this more economically feasible than they would have been a decade earlier, which is part of why connections like this one are opening up now rather than much sooner.

For travellers, that seasonal, three-times-weekly cadence also means the route functions less as a flexible year-round option and more as a specific booking window worth planning around deliberately, particularly for anyone hoping to combine a Cape Town leg with the Australian long weekend or school holiday periods that tend to drive leisure travel demand on new long-haul leisure routes.

What a second major route signals about Cape Town's ambitions

Air Access partnerships like Wesgro's exist specifically because new international air routes rarely happen organically; they typically require sustained lobbying, revenue guarantees, and marketing support from the destination city to convince an airline the demand risk is worth taking on a route with no existing track record. That Cape Town has now landed both an expanded European service and a first-ever Australian one within the same travel season suggests the city's route-development strategy is working at a scale beyond any single deal, building toward a broader ambition of positioning Cape Town as a long-haul destination in its own right rather than a secondary stop reached via Johannesburg. Whether that ambition pays off in sustained passenger numbers, rather than a single strong opening season followed by quieter years, is the longer test every new route like this one eventually faces.

For Cape Town's tourism sector, the practical payoff of a route like this takes years to show up in annual visitor numbers, since seasonal nonstop services typically need several seasons to prove commercial viability before airlines commit to expanding frequency or running them year-round. What the Qantas announcement does immediately is remove one of the last major gaps in Cape Town's global connectivity map: there is, as of this month, no major travel market left entirely unserved by a route into the city.
