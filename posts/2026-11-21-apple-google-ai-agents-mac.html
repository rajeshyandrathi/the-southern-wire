Apple is reportedly building an AI that can use your Mac for you. Google’s is already here.

Bloomberg reports Apple is working on an agent that can operate apps, browse and write code, possibly by the end of next year. Google has launched a Gemini agent for Mac in the US, and a Meta testing mishap shows why permissions matter.

Tech Desk·Saturday, 21 November 2026·2 min read

Key points

  • Bloomberg reports Apple is developing an AI agent that can control a computer, still at an early stage
  • Apple could introduce it as soon as the end of next year; Apple did not comment
  • Google has launched a Gemini agent that can operate Mac apps for paying US subscribers
  • A coming macOS update will warn users before giving third-party agents full access

The next big race in personal computing is about software that does the clicking for you. Apple is working on its own AI agent that can take control of a Mac, operating apps, browsing the web and even writing code, Bloomberg reported on Thursday, according to TechCrunch.

Bloomberg spotted an Apple job advert for a senior engineering manager to oversee an AI agent that can use a computer. “Imagine an AI agent that can use a computer the way a capable person does,” the posting reads. Bloomberg says Apple has eyed introducing such a service as soon as the end of next year, but that the work remains in the early stages. Apple did not respond to TechCrunch’s request for comment.

At the same time, Apple is tightening control over rivals’ agents on its computers. Bloomberg reports that macOS 27.3 will show warnings discouraging users from giving third-party agents full access to their machines.

Google moves first

Google is not waiting. According to The Neuron’s daily digest, which links to TechCrunch’s report, Google this week brought Gemini’s computer-use abilities to the Mac, letting the assistant organise local files, fill in forms and operate apps while the user carries on working. It is available at first in English for subscribers to Google’s AI Plus, Pro and Ultra plans in the United States.

OpenAI, Anthropic and others already offer agents that can control a browser or desktop, and the big platform companies see this as the next step beyond chatbots that only answer questions. An agent that can book a flight, fill in a tax form or tidy a folder of photos could save time, but only if it can be trusted with the access it needs.

Why permissions matter

A reported mishap at Meta this week shows the risk. TechCrunch reported that Meta employees testing an AI agent called Muse saw messages and information that did not belong to them, including childcare messages, personal reminders and bank balances. Meta said employees test versions with features not available to the public.

For ordinary users the lesson is simple. Before you connect an AI agent to your email, bank or files, check what it can see and do, and whether you can limit that access. Apple’s planned warnings suggest that the company sees full computer access as a real security concern, not just a convenience.

None of these tools is widely available in South Africa yet, and Apple’s agent may be more than a year away. But the direction is clear: the companies that make our computers and phones want their assistants to stop talking and start doing.
