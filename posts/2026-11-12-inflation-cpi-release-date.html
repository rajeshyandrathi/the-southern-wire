The number due in six days that will decide whether the Reserve Bank cuts again

Business Desk

Statistics South Africa will publish October's consumer price index on 18 November, and the figure has quietly become one of the more consequential economic releases of the year. September's reading already showed inflation climbing to 4.5% year on year, up from 4.4% in August, a drift that has taken headline inflation uncomfortably far from the 3% target the Reserve Bank formally adopted late last year. Whether October confirms that drift or interrupts it will shape the tone of the Bank's next policy decision far more than any single speech from its Governor.

The stakes are higher than the usual month-to-month inflation story because of how recently the target itself changed. A central bank that has just lowered its own inflation ceiling cannot easily explain away a run of prints sitting above target without undermining the credibility of the new framework in its first real test. Economists who had been pencilling in further rate cuts through early 2027 are now watching this release specifically for signs of whether September's uptick was a blip tied to fuel and food base effects or the start of a more durable trend.

Food inflation is the component drawing the closest scrutiny. Previous CPI cycles in South Africa have repeatedly shown headline figures cooling even as food prices kept climbing, a pattern that erodes the real purchasing power of lower-income households far more than the topline number suggests. If October's release repeats that split, between a moderating headline number and still-rising grocery bills, it will complicate the Reserve Bank's messaging regardless of which way the overall print moves.

There is also a currency dimension sitting underneath all of this. The rand has spent much of the past month trading in a fairly narrow band against the dollar, but any inflation surprise big enough to shift rate-cut expectations tends to move the currency quickly, and a weaker rand feeds directly back into import costs, fuel prices and the next inflation print in a loop that policymakers are acutely aware of. A higher-than-expected October number would not just delay the next rate cut; it risks reinforcing the very currency pressure that makes future inflation prints harder to bring down.

For now, households and businesses alike are left waiting on a single data point to clarify a question that has been building for months: has South Africa's disinflation run stalled, or was September just noise. The answer arrives in six days, and whichever way it breaks will set the terms for the Reserve Bank's final policy decisions of the year.
