South African film's biggest night happens Thursday, in an industry that spent this month asking for a bailout

Culture Desk

Johannesburg hosts the fourth annual National Film & TV Awards South Africa on 19 November, an evening built to celebrate the year's best local film and television work in front of an industry audience that, days earlier, was publicly pleading with the Presidency to save it. The Polygamist leads this year's nominations, the kind of broad, crowd-pleasing local hit that awards ceremonies love to crown precisely because it proves the industry can still produce work audiences actually want to watch — a reassuring message to deliver in a year when so much of the industry's own public commentary has been about survival rather than ambition.

The timing is not flattering by accident; it is simply unavoidable. The Save SA Film and TV Jobs Coalition confirmed this month that its negotiations with the Department of Trade, Industry and Competition over a stalled production incentive have collapsed for a third time, and the coalition has now escalated its appeal directly to the Presidency, bypassing the department it blames for the impasse. An awards gala celebrating the industry's creative output arrives four days after that announcement, which means Thursday's ceremony will inevitably double as a room full of people whose livelihoods depend on exactly the incentive scheme currently stuck in bureaucratic limbo.

That tension is not new to South African film, which has spent years oscillating between genuine creative wins — local productions cracking international markets, award nominations, box office surprises — and a policy environment that struggles to turn those wins into a stable, investable industry. The production incentive at the centre of this month's breakdown exists precisely to give international and local producers enough financial certainty to commit money to South African shoots rather than taking the production, and its jobs, somewhere with a cleaner approval process.

MultiChoice's changed ownership structure adds another layer of uncertainty that the NFTA ceremony cannot resolve on its own. With Canal+'s takeover of the continent's biggest commissioner of local content now complete, South African producers who once could count on a predictable local buyer for their work are adjusting to a French parent company's global portfolio priorities — a shift that makes the kind of government-backed incentive certainty the coalition is fighting for even more consequential than it would have been a few years ago.

None of that will stop Thursday's ceremony from doing what awards nights are built to do: celebrate the work, photograph the winners, and let an anxious industry feel, for one evening, proud of what it has actually managed to make under difficult conditions. The Polygamist's nominations are a genuine credit to South African storytelling. Whether the industry that produced it still exists in anything like its current form a year from now is a question no trophy on Thursday night will answer.
