Transport costs are already driving inflation higher, and October's fuel spike hasn't even landed in the data yet

Business Desk

South Africa's headline inflation rate climbed to 4.5 percent in September, up from 4.4 percent in August, a reading that came in below what economists had pencilled in but that masks a more worrying story underneath the surface. Stats SA's breakdown shows transport costs accelerating sharply, with annual transport inflation jumping to 10.8 percent from 8.8 percent the month before, making it by far the largest single contributor to the overall increase and the category economists are now watching most closely heading into the final quarter of the year.

The timing matters because September's figure does not yet reflect the worst of what has happened at the pumps since. The regulated petrol price rose by roughly five percent in September before surging a further twelve percent in October, a sequence that pushed inland fuel prices to levels not seen in years and that will only begin showing up fully in the inflation data when the October reading is released on 18 November. Economists are bracing for a sharper acceleration in that release, one that could test the upper end of the Reserve Bank's 3 to 6 percent target band depending on how much of the fuel increase passes through into transport and freight-dependent goods.

That the Treasury moved to soften the blow with a temporary cut to the general fuel levy, running from early November through to the start of December, shows how aware policymakers are of the political and economic risk a sustained fuel-driven inflation spike would carry, arriving as it did in the same stretch as a closely fought municipal election in which the cost of living was a constant refrain on the campaign trail. But a levy cut addresses the symptom at the pump, not the underlying transport-cost pressure that shows up in everything from bus and taxi fares to the price of goods trucked between provinces.

For the Reserve Bank, the September print on its own was unambiguously good news, coming in under consensus and giving the monetary policy committee some room to manoeuvre at its next rate-setting meeting. But a single month's surprise undershoot means little if the October reading, loaded with the full force of that fuel increase, comes back hot enough to erase the goodwill. Governor Lesetja Kganyago's committee has spent much of the past two years trying to anchor inflation expectations near the midpoint of the target band, and a transport-driven spike just as households are still absorbing municipal rates increases and school fee hikes would complicate that project considerably.

Economists polled ahead of the 18 November release broadly expect the October figure to show a meaningfully sharper rise, with the only real debate being over degree rather than direction. For South African households already stretched by a year of rand volatility, mining-driven market gains that have done little for wage earners, and now a fuel price shock still working its way through the economy, the question is not whether transport costs will keep squeezing budgets into the festive season, but by how much.
