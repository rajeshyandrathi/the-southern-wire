Jacaranda season fades from Pretoria's streets, but the city's purple afterglow lingers

Lifestyle Desk

For a few weeks each year, Pretoria becomes, by informal but widely accepted consensus, one of the more photogenic cities in the country, as tens of thousands of jacaranda trees lining its older suburbs burst into the dense, violet bloom that has given the city its enduring nickname. That window is now closing. The jacarandas that typically start flowering in late September and peak through October are, by the first week of November, dropping their blossoms onto pavements and car bonnets across Hatfield, Sunnyside, Groenkloof and Waterkloof, the final stretch of a bloom that is as brief as it is intense.

The trees themselves are not indigenous, a fact that tends to surprise visitors who assume a bloom this deeply woven into the city's identity must be a native phenomenon. Jacaranda mimosifolia was introduced to South Africa from South America in the late nineteenth century and took to Pretoria's climate with such enthusiasm that the city now has tens of thousands of specimens, enough that the annual bloom has become a genuine, if unofficial, tourist draw in its own right, along with a reliable subject for amateur and professional photographers alike each spring.

This year's bloom drew the now-familiar rituals that have grown up around it: a dedicated photo-sharing campaign run through a local radio station, an annual marathon route deliberately planned to run beneath the densest canopy stretches, and a guided photo walk through some of the suburb's best-known jacaranda-lined streets, all scheduled through October to catch the bloom at its fullest. By early November, those organised events have wound down, leaving only the trees themselves, now moving from flower to seedpod, and the purple carpet that collects, briefly and messily, on the pavements beneath them.

For residents, the fading bloom carries its own small seasonal melancholy, a sense that one of the city's more reliable annual pleasures has, once again, passed more quickly than anyone would like. Local gardening groups and urban-greening advocates have used the tail end of the season, as they do most years, to renew calls for the city to replace jacarandas lost to age, storm damage or infrastructure projects with new plantings, pointing out that a tree planted today will take decades to produce the kind of canopy density that makes the bloom so striking in the first place.

For visitors who missed this year's peak, the consolation is that the cycle is entirely reliable: barring a particularly unusual season, Pretoria's jacarandas will begin budding again around the same time next September, building toward the same brief, violet-drenched October and early-November window the city has learned, over more than a century, to treasure precisely because it never lasts very long.
