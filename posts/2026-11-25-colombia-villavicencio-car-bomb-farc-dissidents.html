Car bomb rips through Colombian city on the eve of the peace deal’s 10th anniversary

More than 20 people were injured when a car packed with explosives blew up beside the Meta governor’s office in Villavicencio. President Abelardo de la Espriella blamed FARC dissidents and vowed revenge.

World Desk·Wednesday, 25 November 2026·3 min read

Key points

  • The bomb exploded at about 9.30pm on Monday, 23 November, in central Villavicencio
  • Between 22 and at least 25 people were injured, with no deaths reported
  • Officials said the car carried about 80 to 100 kg of explosives
  • Authorities blamed a FARC dissident faction, though no group has claimed the attack
  • The blast came a day before the 10th anniversary of the 2016 peace accord

A powerful car bomb exploded near the governor’s office in Villavicencio, capital of Colombia’s Meta department south-east of Bogotá, at about 9.30pm on Monday, 23 November. More than 20 people were injured, NBC reported, and President Abelardo de la Espriella said no deaths had been recorded.

Counts of the injured differ. Colombia Reports put the figure at 22, Al Jazeera at 23, and police told AFP that at least 25 people were hurt. Five of the injured were private security guards, one of whom needed surgery, according to Colombia Reports.

The blast tore off building facades, lifted roofs, left a crater in the street and knocked out power in parts of the city centre, AFP and NBC reported. Colombia Reports said more than 80 buildings were damaged, some more than 100 metres away. A hat shop owner who was inside at the time told AFP: “We are alive by a miracle.”

Who is being blamed

Meta’s security secretary, Franklin Olivos, said the car carried about 100 kg of explosives and blamed the 39th Front of the EMC, a FARC dissident group that rejected the peace process, Colombia Reports said. Police operations chief Ricardo Alarcón told local media that early tests pointed to 80 to 100 kg of pentolite, a military-grade explosive, Al Jazeera reported. No group has claimed responsibility.

De la Espriella blamed EMC commander Antonio Medina and vowed to “make him pay with blood”, according to Al Jazeera. He said the attack “will not go unpunished” and that his government would not negotiate under terror. His government had confirmed the killing of another EMC commander the day before the bombing.

Elizabeth Dickinson of the International Crisis Group told Al Jazeera that armed groups are trying to raise the government’s costs by causing harm to civilians.

The EMC is led by a commander known as Iván Mordisco, for whom the government offered a bounty of 5 billion pesos, about $1.4 million, earlier this year, Al Jazeera reported. Meta was a FARC stronghold before the group demobilised, and dissident factions have since spread across an area tied to coca, gold and drug trafficking routes into the Amazon basin.

A peace deal under strain

The bombing came on the eve of the 10th anniversary of the 2016 accord under which the FARC guerrillas laid down their arms. AFP noted the deal sharply reduced bloodshed but did not end Colombia’s conflict, and dissident factions still operate in Meta, funded by drugs, extortion and illegal mining.

De la Espriella, a far-right leader who took office in August, has suspended talks started by his predecessor and launched a US-backed military offensive. Colombia Reports argued that the “iron fist” approach has not yet produced results, with armed groups still attacking security forces and infrastructure. The bombing follows our report on Monday about the government’s clash with former president Gustavo Petro, a sign of how tense Colombian politics has become.
