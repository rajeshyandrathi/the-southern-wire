Apple's first foldable sells out on day one in South Africa

Tech Desk·Monday, 2 November 2026

Apple finally joined the foldable phone race this year, and when the iPhone Duo reached South African shelves on 23 October, local demand answered any lingering question about whether the country's appetite for Apple hardware has a price ceiling. Every unit at every iStore nationwide was gone within hours of launch, a result that would be notable for any device and is especially striking for one that starts at R47,999 with no trade-in.

That starting price is the entry point, not the ceiling. Fully specced configurations run as high as roughly R77,000, placing the iPhone Duo comfortably among the most expensive phones ever sold in the South African market. Apple has built in a release valve for sticker shock: trade-in offers can knock the effective price down to as little as R30,199 depending on the device being handed in, a structure clearly designed to pull existing iPhone owners up the ladder rather than price them out entirely.

What makes the sell-out genuinely interesting is what it says about South African consumer behaviour at a moment when household budgets are under real strain from fuel costs, interest rates and a currency that has spent most of the year drifting rather than strengthening. A near-R50,000 phone selling out on day one, in that context, isn't really a contradiction — it's a reminder that premium device demand and broader cost-of-living pressure tend to run in parallel rather than in opposition, driven by a different segment of the market than the one feeling squeezed at the pump.

For the broader foldable category, Apple's late entry carries outsized weight regardless of local sales figures. Samsung and Huawei have spent years trying to convince mainstream buyers that a folding screen is worth the premium and the added fragility risk, with mixed results outside of enthusiast circles. Apple's arrival — and the willingness of South African buyers to queue for it at a price well above what rivals charge for comparable hardware — suggests the category may finally be crossing from niche curiosity into genuine mainstream contention.

Whether that momentum holds once the early-adopter rush fades is the more interesting question for retailers now restocking shelves. Day-one sell-outs are a reliable sign of pent-up demand; they are a much less reliable predictor of sustained sales once the initial hype settles and buyers have had time to live with a fold line running through the middle of their screen.
