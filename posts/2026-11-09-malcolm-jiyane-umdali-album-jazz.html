A trombonist who came up through Johannesburg's jazz underground drops his debut album this week

Culture Desk

Malcolm Jiyane has spent years as one of Johannesburg's most quietly respected sidemen, the trombonist and pianist whose work with the city's jazz collectives has shaped rooms without necessarily putting his own name on the marquee. That changes on 12 November, when his debut album, UMDALI, arrives through Mushroom Hour Half Hour, the independent label that has become something close to a house style for the generation of South African jazz musicians who came of age in the years after the genre's older guard — the Dollar Brands and Hugh Masekelas — had already defined what South African jazz meant to the rest of the world.

The album's lead single, "Umkhumbi kaMa," gave an early sense of what Jiyane has built: a sound rooted in the township brass traditions he grew up around, filtered through the more exploratory, less commercially tidy instincts of Johannesburg's current jazz underground. It is a scene that has operated for most of the past decade largely outside the mainstream industry's attention, sustained by small venues, self-released recordings and a tight network of musicians who play on each other's projects as often as their own — the same ecosystem that has produced acclaimed work from artists like Thandi Ntuli and the Mabuta collective, several of whom are expected to appear on UMDALI in some capacity.

What makes Jiyane's story resonate beyond the usual jazz-press circuit is the path it took to get here. He is a graduate of the Johannesburg music education pipeline that runs through community arts centres and informal mentorship as much as formal conservatories, and his rise to leading his own album-length project reflects a broader pattern in South African jazz right now: musicians who spent years as collaborators, arrangers and backing players finally stepping forward with work that carries their own name, after building the technical and creative credibility to do so on their own terms rather than a label's commercial timetable.

The album's title, UMDALI — "the creator" in isiZulu — gestures at both a personal and a communal claim: an artist asserting his own authorship after years spent serving other people's visions, while still working within a musical language that is explicitly collective by tradition. South African jazz has rarely been a soloist's genre in the way American jazz mythology sometimes frames the form; it has tended to live in ensembles, in call-and-response, in rooms where the audience's presence shapes the performance as much as the sheet music does. Jiyane's record, by most early accounts, leans into that communal grain rather than away from it.

For a scene that has watched several of its brightest names depart for international labels and bigger stages in recent years, a strong, locally released debut from a player as embedded in Johannesburg's jazz infrastructure as Jiyane carries a particular kind of significance. UMDALI arrives not as a bid for crossover attention but as a document of a scene that has kept building, carefully and mostly out of the spotlight, for longer than most casual listeners have been paying attention.
