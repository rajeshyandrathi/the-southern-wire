Three and a half years after AKA's murder, the Durban High Court is finally hearing the evidence

Crime Desk

For a case that has spent most of its life tangled in extradition hearings, postponements and procedural skirmishes, the murder trial of Kiernan "AKA" Forbes and his friend Tebello "Tibz" Motsoane has reached an unfamiliar stage: actual testimony. All seven accused men entered not guilty pleas when the trial opened at the Durban High Court, and in the weeks since, the state has been building its case witness by witness, a slow and deliberate process that stands in contrast to the years of legal wrangling that preceded it.

Among the more closely watched developments has been the court's decision to grant one of the accused, Siyanda Myeza, a separate trial on a distinct set of charges, numbers twelve through seventeen on an indictment that already runs long enough to require its own index. Splitting the proceedings in this way is common in cases with multiple accused facing overlapping but not identical charges, but it also means the main trial can proceed without being slowed by arguments specific to a co-accused's separate counts, a small mercy in a case that has already tested the patience of the Forbes and Motsoane families.

The testimony itself has filled in details the public has waited years to hear. Tour and road manager Siyabonga Mdluli took the witness stand to describe the hours and movements around the shooting outside a Florida Road eatery in February 2023, evidence prosecutors are using to establish the sequence of events that preceded the attack. The state's case rests heavily on reconstructing exactly who was where, and when, in the minutes before the ambush, work that depends on witnesses like Mdluli being able to recall details from an attack that is now more than three and a half years old.

That gap between crime and trial is itself part of the story South Africans have watched play out, with grim familiarity, across several of the country's highest-profile murder cases. Two of the accused, brothers Siyabonga and Malusi Ndimande, only returned to South Africa a year ago after a protracted extradition battle fought from across the border in Eswatini, and it was only once they were back in the country that the case against all seven accused could finally move toward a full trial date. The years lost to that process are not recoverable, whatever the eventual verdict.

What happens next will test whether the momentum built over the past month can be sustained. With the trial calendar already disrupted once by the municipal election period and a separate proceeding carved out for Myeza, the Durban High Court faces the same challenge that has bedevilled other long-running South African murder trials: keeping witnesses, evidence and public attention focused long enough to reach a verdict that actually answers the question that has hung over the entertainment industry since February 2023 — who ordered the killing of two of its own, and why.
