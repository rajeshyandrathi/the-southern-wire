A single freight train out of Komatipoort just tested whether Transnet's rail monopoly can survive opening up

Business Desk

It was a modest cargo by the standards of a freight network that is supposed to move hundreds of millions of tonnes a year: a single train carrying 2,991 metric tonnes, departing Komatipoort on 1 November and returning the following day along the North East Corridor. But the test run, jointly run by Transnet Rail Infrastructure Manager and logistics group Grindrod, marks something considerably bigger than its tonnage suggests. It is the first time a private train operating company has run services on South Africa's national freight rail network, a milestone years in the making for a reform process that has repeatedly stalled against the scale of Transnet's institutional inertia.

The symbolism is deliberate. For years, South African exporters, particularly in mining and agriculture, have complained that Transnet Freight Rail's deteriorating locomotive fleet, cable theft and chronic underinvestment were costing the economy billions in lost export volumes, cargo that ended up either stranded at origin or shifted onto an already overburdened road network. The open-access model being tested on the North East Corridor is meant to address exactly that bottleneck by allowing private operators like Grindrod to run their own trains on Transnet-owned infrastructure, in theory bringing in capital, equipment and operational discipline that the state-owned entity has struggled to muster on its own.

Whether a single successful test run translates into a genuine shift in how freight moves in South Africa is the harder question. Transnet has signalled it is open to more private-sector participation, both on its rail network and at its port terminals, where chief executive Jabu Mdaki has said seven terminals are now classified as being in a strong operational position and that the focus has shifted toward growth and equipment reliability after years in which simply keeping existing infrastructure running consumed most of management's attention. New port equipment is expected to arrive before the end of November, a concrete deliverable against years of announcements that produced little visible change on the ground.

There are reasons for cautious optimism beyond this single test. Transnet reported its first profit in four years for the financial year ended March 2026, a R4.6 billion result helped by a one-off windfall rather than a sustained operational turnaround, alongside rail volumes of 167 million tonnes that still fell short of a 180 million tonne target. Separately, the company has pressed ahead with restoring the critical iron ore corridor linking Sishen and Saldanha, replacing sleepers, repairing track and overhauling signalling on a line that export-dependent mining houses regard as one of the most commercially important in the country.

None of this amounts to proof that Transnet's long crisis is over. But a private train successfully completing a round trip on a network long associated with breakdowns, theft and missed targets is the kind of small, concrete data point that the logistics sector has been waiting years to see, and that mining and agricultural exporters will be watching closely to see whether it becomes the norm rather than a one-off pilot.
