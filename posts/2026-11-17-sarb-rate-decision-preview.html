South Africa's central bank faces its tightest call of the year, a day after the number that decides it

Business Desk

Thursday's Reserve Bank decision was already shaping up as the hardest call of the year before a single new data point landed. The repo rate sits at 7.25% after the Monetary Policy Committee's unanimous 25-basis-point hike in September, a move that pushed borrowing costs higher just as the economy was still digesting the local government elections and a currency that has spent most of November trading near a one-month high against the dollar. Economists surveyed through late October leaned toward expecting another hike rather than a hold, even as some of the inflation pressure that justified September's move showed early signs of easing.

What actually determines which way this goes lands a day before the decision itself. Statistics South Africa's October inflation print, due out on Wednesday, is expected to show a sharp acceleration from September's 4.5% reading, driven almost entirely by one number: a reported 12% surge in the regulated petrol price during October that pushed fuel costs to a record high. Some economists quoted ahead of the release think that single factor could be enough to push headline inflation above 5% for the first time in this cycle, a threshold that would make a December hold far harder for the MPC to justify politically, whatever the committee's internal economic reasoning says.

That creates an unusually stark fork for Governor Lesetja Kganyago's committee. A below-5% October print would hand the MPC room to hold rates steady and let September's hike do its work quietly through the rest of the summer. A print that clears 5%, driven by fuel rather than any broader demand pressure the Bank can actually influence, would put the MPC in the awkward position of raising rates again to fight an inflation spike that interest rates are poorly designed to fix in the first place — since no repo rate adjustment puts a single rand back into the price of imported crude.

Households are the ones actually caught in the middle of this. A further hike would arrive just as the fuel levy relief due in December is supposed to start easing pressure at the pump, creating the odd spectacle of the government cutting fuel taxes with one hand while the central bank raises borrowing costs with the other. Mortgage holders and anyone carrying variable-rate debt would feel a December hike almost immediately, at precisely the point in the calendar when household budgets are already stretched by school fees, matric-related costs and the first wave of festive season spending.

The rand, for its part, has been largely indifferent to all of this domestic hand-wringing. Its recent strength against the dollar owes more to global dollar and gold moves than to anything happening inside South Africa's borders, according to analysts tracking the currency's trajectory through early November — a reminder that even a textbook-perfect MPC decision on Thursday may do less to move the currency than whatever Washington or London does on the same day.

Whatever Kganyago's committee decides, it will be deciding with Wednesday's number fresh in hand and barely 24 hours to have internalised it, the kind of compressed timeline that leaves little room for the Bank's usual deliberative instincts. For a decision already being called the closest of the year, that is not a comfortable position for anyone involved.
