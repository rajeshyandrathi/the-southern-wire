Eskom passes 500 days without load shedding and nearly halves its diesel bill — but it isn't calling the crisis over

Business Desk

Eskom has now gone more than 500 consecutive days without resorting to load shedding, a streak the utility dates back to mid-May 2025 and describes as the clearest evidence yet that its fleet's reliability has genuinely turned a corner. The improvement shows up most starkly in the utility's diesel spending, which fell by R4.79 billion, or just over 80%, in the six months to the start of October, as a more stable generation fleet reduced the need for Eskom's expensive open-cycle gas turbines to plug gaps in supply.

The numbers behind the streak point to steady, incremental gains rather than any single fix. Eskom credits reduced unplanned outages, down by 763 megawatts year on year, alongside a climbing energy availability factor and a corresponding decline in reliance on emergency generation resources. For businesses and households that spent the early 2020s budgeting around rolling blackouts as a fact of life, the shift has been significant enough that load shedding has largely dropped out of daily conversation, replaced by cautious optimism that the worst of the crisis has passed.

Eskom itself is notably less willing to declare victory. Its own medium-term system adequacy outlook, covering 2026 through 2030, flags a renewed and meaningful risk of load shedding returning in 2029 and 2030, as ageing generation units reach the end of their operating lives faster than new capacity is likely to come online to replace them. The current run of stability, in other words, is being read internally as a window rather than a permanent fix, one that depends on maintenance discipline continuing at a pace the utility has not always managed to sustain over a full business cycle.

A second, less visible risk sits closer to the present: rising municipal debt owed to Eskom, which the utility has separately warned carries severe implications for its own finances and, by extension, for its ability to keep funding the maintenance programme underpinning the current reliability gains. Municipalities that fall behind on payments to Eskom create a funding gap that ultimately has to be absorbed somewhere, either through tariff increases on paying customers or through deferred spending on exactly the kind of plant maintenance that has driven the recent improvement.

For now, the headline number, more than 500 days without a single rotating blackout, is the story most South Africans will take from this update. But Eskom's own outlook makes clear that the utility views this as a hard-won stabilisation rather than a solved problem, and that the next real test of the turnaround will not be this summer or even next, but whether the current trajectory can survive the ageing of its fleet and the financial strain of municipalities that still are not paying their bills.
