Film & Screen

MultiChoice now answers to Paris. South African screens are finding out what that means.

Culture Desk

For thirty years, MultiChoice was the thing that decided what African television looked like, a continental broadcaster built in Johannesburg, commissioning in local languages, and answerable, for better and worse, to African shareholders and an African audience. Canal+'s takeover ends that specific arrangement. The French media group's acquisition is now complete, and the commissioning decisions that used to be made with an eye on Soweto or Lagos or Nairobi now run through a corporate structure headquartered in Paris, with its own commercial logic and its own sense of which markets deserve investment.

Industry commentary since the deal closed has settled into a consistent worry: that African storytelling, having spent a generation building its own platform, is now dependent again on decisions made elsewhere. It is a familiar anxiety dressed in new clothes. South African content creators have spent decades negotiating around international distributors who treated local production as a line item rather than a priority, and MultiChoice's rise was supposed to be the exception, a platform with enough scale and local ownership to commission African stories on African terms. A French parent company changes that calculus, not necessarily by intent, but by structure: decisions about budgets and slates now compete for attention inside a global portfolio rather than a continental one.

The practical stakes show up in the numbers productions have come to rely on. DStv and the broader MultiChoice ecosystem remain the single largest commissioner of South African scripted and factual content, the platform that turned shows into genuine cultural moments and gave local actors, writers and crews a viable career path without having to chase an American or British commission. Whether Canal+ maintains that commissioning appetite at the same scale, or quietly redirects capital toward markets it already understands better, is the question every production house with a MultiChoice deal in its pipeline is now asking in private.

None of this is happening against a backdrop of industry confidence. The same production sector worried about Canal+'s intentions is simultaneously fighting the government over a stalled incentive scheme and warning that crews are already leaving the industry for lack of paid work. A foreign ownership shift at the top of the distribution chain, landing in the middle of a domestic funding crisis, compounds a sector's vulnerability rather than simply adding to a list of separate problems; a struggling local incentive and a less certain local commissioner reinforce each other in exactly the way an industry trying to survive can least afford.

What happens from here will be decided quietly, in slate meetings and renewal negotiations that rarely make headlines until a beloved show gets cancelled or an unexpected one gets greenlit. South African audiences will find out which way Canal+ leans the same way they always find out how a media company's priorities have shifted: by noticing, a season or two later, what's missing from the schedule that used to be there.
