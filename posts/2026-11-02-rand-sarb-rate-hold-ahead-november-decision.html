Rand treads water as markets brace for SARB's next rate call on 19 November

Business Desk·Monday, 2 November 2026

The rand has spent the past fortnight doing very little that would alarm anyone, drifting in a tight band against the dollar while traders wait for the one number that will actually move it: the South African Reserve Bank's next interest rate decision, due on 19 November. After September's quarter-point hike took the benchmark repo rate to 7.25% — the second increase this year, following an earlier move in May — the Monetary Policy Committee's unanimous vote signalled a central bank more worried about imported inflation than about choking off a fragile recovery.

That worry hasn't disappeared. The currency's recent weakness has had far more to do with a broadly stronger dollar than with anything specific to South Africa's fiscal position, which is exactly the kind of imported pressure the Reserve Bank has limited tools to fight. Raise rates too aggressively in response to global dollar strength, and the Bank risks squeezing a domestic economy that is still finding its feet; hold steady, and it risks watching the rand drift further just as the festive season — traditionally a period of heavier import demand — gets underway.

Economists surveyed on the rand's medium-term path remain split on just how far the currency could move from here. Some forecasts see it firming modestly by year-end as global risk appetite improves; others expect it to hover closer to current levels, with the Reserve Bank's November decision serving as the next real catalyst either way. The spread between those views is itself telling — a sign that even seasoned currency-watchers see this as a genuinely close call rather than a formality.

For ordinary South Africans, the abstraction of a repo rate decision translates quickly into something concrete: the cost of a home loan, a vehicle instalment, or a small business's working capital. A further hike in November would mark the third increase in a year, piling pressure onto households already absorbing a fuel price adjustment this week and bracing for whatever the festive season brings. A hold, conversely, would be read as the clearest signal yet that the Bank believes the inflation fight is closer to won than lost.

Either way, the next fortnight promises to be one of the more closely watched stretches on the South African economic calendar this year — a reminder that even in a country consumed by a municipal election this week, the numbers coming out of Pretoria's central bank still set the tempo for what people actually feel in their wallets.
