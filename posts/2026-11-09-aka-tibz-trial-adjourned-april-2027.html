The AKA murder trial goes dark until April — but a taxi boss's company just became impossible to ignore

Crime Desk

The Durban High Court closed the book on 2026 in the AKA and Tibz murder trial last Thursday, adjourning the matter until 12 April 2027, when it is scheduled to resume and run through to mid-May. All seven accused remain in custody, exactly where they have sat since their arrests, and the adjournment means the single most-watched criminal case in the country now goes quiet for five months at precisely the point it had started producing its most damaging evidence.

That evidence centres on money, not footage or cellphone records. Prosecutors told the court that R803,455 moved from the account of a company called Bright Circle into the account of Mziwethemba Gwabeni, the man the state alleges orchestrated the killing, in February 2023 — and that Gwabeni then redistributed portions of that sum to four of his co-accused within days. The figure itself is not new to the record. What has sharpened in the weeks since is the attention now falling on Bright Circle's ownership, which prosecutors have linked to Durban taxi industry figure Mfundo Gcaba. Gcaba has not been charged with anything, and nothing in the state's case so far alleges he knew what the payment from his company would be used for. But a named, identifiable businessman now sits adjacent to the money trail in a case that has spent three and a half years struggling to answer the question everyone outside the courtroom keeps asking: who actually paid for Kiernan "AKA" Forbes to be killed, and why.

The financial evidence arrived on the back of weeks of forensic and technical testimony that had already reshaped how the trial is being read. A Cartrack fleet administrator walked the court through the movements of two rental vehicles the state says were used to track Forbes and his friend Tebello "Tibz" Motsoane in the days before the February 2023 shooting outside a Florida Road restaurant. A crime-scene investigator detailed the ballistic, biological and fingerprint evidence lifted from a white Mercedes-Benz allegedly connected to the killers. Each piece on its own is procedural; together, they have built a timeline the defence has spent as much energy attacking as the prosecution has spent constructing, challenging the chain of custody on the tracking data and the reliability of witnesses with their own complicated histories.

All of that momentum now pauses. The five-month gap is not unusual for a case of this size and complexity, but it is a long time for a trial that has already outlasted most observers' patience, and it leaves the financial thread involving Gcaba's company hanging in a kind of legal limbo until the court reconvenes in the autumn. Whether prosecutors use the break to deepen that line of inquiry, or whether it simply resurfaces in April exactly as it stands today, is impossible to say from outside the case.

What is clear is that nearly four years after Forbes was gunned down, the trial of the seven men accused of killing him has become less a single narrative than a slow accumulation of fragments — a tracking device here, a bank transfer there, a name that edges closer to the center of the story without yet being formally implicated in it. For Forbes's family, and for a fanbase that has waited since February 2023 for anything resembling resolution, April 2027 is now the next, and possibly not the last, date circled on the calendar.
