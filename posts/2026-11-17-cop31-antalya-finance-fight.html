Antalya has days left to answer the question every climate summit avoids: who pays?

World Desk

COP31 entered its final stretch in Antalya this week with the same unresolved argument that has derailed nearly every climate summit before it: not whether the planet is warming dangerously, which nobody at the Expo Center seriously disputes, but who is supposed to pay for the damage already locked in. Negotiators from 196 countries and the European Union have spent the fortnight since the summit opened on 9 November circling a finance gap that Carbon Brief has described as a "staggering shortfall," with wealthy and developing economies as far apart on the adaptation-funding question as they were walking in.

UNFCCC chief Simon Stiell opened proceedings by promising this COP would do things differently, pairing formal negotiations with a parallel "Action Agenda" meant to translate pledges into deployable projects rather than letting them evaporate into another non-binding communiqué. Whether that structural change produces a different outcome than previous summits remains to be seen; the leaders' segment on 11 and 12 November drew the usual roster of heads of state and ministers, but the substantive fight over money has, as usual, been left to the technical negotiating teams working through the summit's final days.

Australia's Chris Bowen, serving as incoming COP31 president of negotiations, inherits a brief that requires squaring two incompatible asks: developing nations demanding finance commitments and delivery mechanisms that are actually binding rather than aspirational, and wealthy economies resisting exactly that kind of enforceable obligation. China's Vice-President Han Zheng attending the leaders' summit adds another variable, given Beijing's own ambiguous positioning as simultaneously the world's largest annual emitter and a self-described leader of the developing-world bloc pushing hardest for climate finance.

A draft decision on operationalising the summit's "just transition" mechanism is expected before COP31 closes on 20 November, alongside the broader cover decision that will stand as the summit's headline outcome regardless of how much of the finance dispute it actually resolves. Some governments are reportedly pushing to have an explicit fossil fuel transition reference written into that cover text, a fight that has derailed consensus at previous COPs and shows no sign of being easier to settle in Antalya.

The backdrop against which all of this is being negotiated makes the stakes harder to dismiss as procedural. Climate monitors now consider it virtually certain that 2026 will rank as the warmest or second-warmest year on record, with heatwaves across Europe and wildfire smoke blanketing parts of North America cited by negotiators as the lived evidence behind the numbers on their briefing papers. For the countries bearing the costs of that reality without the resources to adapt to it, Antalya's remaining days are less about diplomatic choreography than about whether the money promised at past summits finally starts resembling money that actually arrives.
