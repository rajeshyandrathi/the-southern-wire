Hermanus's whale season is winding down, after the earliest start anyone can remember

Lifestyle Desk

Southern right whales started showing up along the Overberg coast as early as May this year, weeks ahead of the June arrival that locals in Hermanus have come to treat as the unofficial start of the season. That early arrival turned what is usually a slow build into a long, sustained run, with whales visible from the town's famous cliff path for the better part of six months rather than the more typical four. As November closes in, that extended season is finally entering its last stretch.

Hermanus has built an entire civic identity around these animals, and for good reason: the cliff path running along Walker Bay offers one of the best land-based whale-watching vantage points anywhere in the world, close enough that visitors regularly spot southern rights breaching without needing a boat at all. The peak crowds typically arrive in September and October, when the largest concentration of whales is usually present, and this year's numbers reportedly held up well through that stretch even with the unusually early start shifting the overall rhythm of the season.

The town's tourism infrastructure has adapted accordingly. City Sightseeing has launched dedicated whale-watching tours running directly from Cape Town to Hermanus, a roughly two-hour drive that puts the experience within reach of visitors who might otherwise only associate whale watching with a long-haul boat trip. That kind of accessible day-trip option matters for a seasonal attraction that depends on getting as many visitors as possible into a window that, however extended this year, is still fundamentally temporary.

Southern right whales migrate to the warmer, calmer waters off South Africa's southern coast specifically to calve and nurse their young before heading back toward Antarctic feeding grounds, which is part of why Walker Bay's sheltered waters have become such a reliable nursery site year after year. An early arrival in a given year does not necessarily predict anything about the following season, but it does suggest a population that remains healthy enough to show up ahead of schedule, which conservationists watching the species' slow recovery from historical whaling losses will take as a quietly encouraging sign.

For visitors weighing a trip before the season properly ends, the window is narrowing but not yet closed. The whales that arrived early will not necessarily leave early, and sightings from the cliff path typically continue into November even as the numbers thin out compared to the September peak. Hermanus will spend the next few weeks easing out of its busiest season, before gearing up to do it all again, likely sooner than expected, next year.
