Fresh off beating Australia, the Proteas face a Bangladesh side nobody wants to underestimate

Sport Desk

Temba Bavuma's Proteas arrive at the Wanderers for Sunday's first Test against Bangladesh still basking in one of the more improbable series wins of their recent history. Having lost the opening Test in Durban, South Africa responded by winning the decider at Newlands by 176 runs, with off-spinner Simon Harmer producing career-best figures of 10 wickets for 120 runs in a performance that single-handedly turned the match. Bavuma has been open about what that comeback did for a group that had looked vulnerable after Durban: a series win salvaged from a losing position tends to settle more arguments inside a dressing room than almost anything else in cricket.

Bangladesh arrive without the subcontinental reputation for weakness in South African conditions that past touring sides have carried. Their 16-member squad for the two-match series includes uncapped batter Mahfijul Islam, a sign that the Bangladesh Cricket Board is willing to blood new talent against a daunting opponent rather than lean purely on experience, and previews of the series have flagged Bangladesh's bowling attack in particular as a unit capable of exploiting a Proteas batting line-up that, despite the series win over Australia, is still finding its settled form.

The first Test runs at the Wanderers from 15 to 19 November, a ground whose pace and bounce have historically rewarded fast bowling attacks over spin, before the series shifts to SuperSport Park in Centurion for the second Test from 23 to 27 November. The scheduling gives both sides a short turnaround between matches, which tends to favour whichever team settles into rhythm first rather than whichever has the deeper reserve strength to rotate through.

For South Africa, the series arrives at a useful moment in the broader context of the World Test Championship cycle, where consistent home results against all opposition — not just the traditional heavyweights — have become the currency that determines finals qualification. Beating Australia from behind was the kind of result that makes headlines; quietly dispatching a Bangladesh side being talked up as competitive is the less glamorous work that actually builds a WTC campaign.

Bavuma's challenge now is less about motivation than about continuity: keeping a batting unit that found its nerve under pressure against Australia from relaxing it against an opponent that history says South Africa should beat comfortably, but that this particular touring party has been specifically built, and talked up, to make difficult.
