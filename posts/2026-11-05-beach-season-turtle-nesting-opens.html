Summer opens along the coast, and the first loggerheads come ashore to nest at St Lucia

Lifestyle Desk

There is a particular, recognisable shift that happens along South Africa's coastline every year around this time, the point at which a handful of warm days stop being a pleasant exception and start being the new normal. From Cape Town's Atlantic beaches to the warmer waters of the KwaZulu-Natal coast, November marks the informal start of a beach season that will run through to March, and this year's opening weekends have drawn the usual early crowds testing water still a few degrees shy of its midsummer peak.

The Western Cape's beaches, Camps Bay, Muizenberg, Clifton, are settling into the dry, sun-heavy pattern that defines a Cape summer, even as the water itself stays bracingly cool for visitors used to the Indian Ocean's warmer temperatures further east. Along the KwaZulu-Natal coast and the Wild Coast, where the warm Agulhas Current keeps the sea considerably more swimmable earlier in the season, the shift to summer brings crowds back to beaches that have spent winter largely to local surfers and dog-walkers.

Further up that same KwaZulu-Natal coastline, summer's arrival means something else entirely: the start of nesting season for the loggerhead and leatherback turtles that return every year to the beaches around St Lucia and the iSimangaliso Wetland Park to lay their eggs, a ritual that runs from November through to March and draws conservation volunteers and researchers back onto the sand for nightly patrols. The park's turtle monitoring programme, one of the longest-running of its kind on the continent, tracks nesting females along a stretch of coastline that remains one of the most important loggerhead nesting sites in the western Indian Ocean.

For the handful of lodges and tour operators licensed to run supervised night-time turtle tours in the area, the opening weeks of the season are a particularly delicate balancing act, managing visitor enthusiasm for a genuinely rare wildlife encounter against strict protocols designed to avoid disturbing nesting females, whose behaviour can be disrupted by light, noise or careless proximity. Operators describe a steady but carefully capped level of demand each season, driven in large part by word of mouth from visitors who describe the sight of a nesting loggerhead as one of the more memorable wildlife encounters the country's coastline has to offer.

Taken together, the warming beaches and the returning turtles mark the same seasonal turn from two very different angles, one a straightforward pleasure that draws crowds back to the sand, the other a quieter, more closely guarded ritual that asks visitors to tread carefully around it. Both, in their own way, are among the clearest signs that a South African summer has properly begun.
