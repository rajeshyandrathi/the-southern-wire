Starlink's South African launch is stuck again, this time on a law nobody has actually changed yet

Tech Desk

More than a year after Communications Minister Solly Malatsi first tried to clear a path for Starlink to operate legally in South Africa, the satellite internet provider remains exactly where it started: unlicensed, unable to sell its service domestically, and waiting on a regulatory fix that keeps running into the same wall. Malatsi's December 2025 ministerial directive instructed the Independent Communications Authority of South Africa to accept equity-equivalent investment programmes as an alternative route to the 30% historically disadvantaged ownership requirement that currently bars Starlink's majority-foreign structure from holding a licence. ICASA's answer, delivered formally in May this year, was that it simply does not have the legal power to grant that alternative without Parliament first amending the Electronic Communications Act.

That finding has produced a genuinely unusual standoff: a minister who wants the policy changed, a regulator that agrees the current rules are the obstacle but insists it cannot act unilaterally, and a piece of primary legislation that nobody with the authority to amend it has yet moved to amend. ICASA has since tried to narrow the practical gap, using a round of spectrum hearings in August to spell out in concrete terms what a satellite operator such as Starlink would actually need to demonstrate to qualify under existing rules. SpaceX representatives used the same hearings to press for clarity on licensing, ownership and fees, a sign the company has not abandoned the South African market even as the legal path to entering it keeps lengthening.

For ordinary South Africans in areas where fibre and mobile broadband remain patchy or unreliable, the deadlock has a direct cost. Starlink's satellite service is already used informally by some South Africans through workarounds — registering to a neighbouring country's address, routing through resellers — that exist in a legal grey zone and that regulators have periodically threatened, without much practical follow-through, to crack down on. A legitimate, locally licensed service would resolve that grey zone outright, and would add a new competitive option in a connectivity market still shaped by the legacy of state-linked fixed-line infrastructure.

The empowerment requirement at the centre of the dispute is not a minor technicality to either side. For the government, the 30% ownership threshold embodies a broader economic transformation policy it has defended against years of pressure to soften for the sake of attracting foreign telecoms investment. For Starlink's parent company, agreeing to cede nearly a third of a South African subsidiary's ownership runs against a global operating model built on tight corporate control. The equity-equivalent compromise was designed precisely to split that difference, letting foreign operators meet transformation goals through verified investment in skills and infrastructure rather than direct shareholding — but only legislation, not a ministerial directive or a regulatory interpretation, can actually make that compromise binding.

Estimates of when that legislative fix might actually arrive range from cautiously optimistic to openly skeptical. One recent legal analysis puts a realistic launch date for a fully licensed Starlink service in South Africa at late this year "at the absolute earliest," with 2027 the more plausible window; another independent assessment goes further, describing the equity-equivalence talks as effectively dead in their current form. Until Parliament moves, if it moves at all, the country's satellite internet question remains a policy argument rather than a service South Africans can simply sign up for.
