Shot, stabbed and mutilated: the Ventersdorp farm attack that has four men in the dock and a province on edge

Crime Desk

Donovan Marx is recovering at Mediclinic Potchefstroom, his family posting photographs of him smiling from a hospital bed, after an attack on his farm in Syferfontein near Ventersdorp on 28 October that even hardened North West police officers have struggled to describe without reaching for the word "brutal." Marx was shot and stabbed, and had both ears cut off during an ordeal that investigators now believe was planned well in advance rather than a robbery that escalated. A farmworker, Christopher Jinga, was restrained with cable ties during the attack, and three firearms were removed from a safe on the property.

What followed was one of the faster multi-suspect arrests North West police have made in a farm attack case in recent memory. Two suspects were picked up within days, with the provincial police commissioner briefing media directly on the operation and calling the attack exactly what its details suggest: brutal, and clearly premeditated. Three more arrests followed in quick succession, with investigators saying some of the men had initially fled the province before being traced and brought back. By the time four of them appeared in the Ventersdorp Magistrates' Court on 3 November, prosecutors were already characterising the case as a planned operation rather than an opportunistic crime, aged between 26 and 56.

The gap between five reported arrests and four court appearances has not been explained in any of the official briefings so far, a loose thread that is likely to surface again as the matter proceeds through the Ventersdorp and Potchefstroom courts. What has been confirmed is that Marx's recovery has not been straightforward. After an initial wave of relatively encouraging updates from his family, a subsequent report described a medical setback, underscoring how serious his injuries were, even as the photographs his family has released show a man determined to project resilience rather than victimhood.

Cases like this one land with particular force in North West's farming communities, where attacks on farmers have become a recurring and politically charged feature of provincial life, invoked by lobby groups and politicians on competing sides of an argument about crime statistics and their meaning that rarely stays confined to the facts of any one case. Marx's attack arrived in the same week that a separate incident elsewhere in North West left a woman dead and another farmer critically injured, a grim coincidence that has sharpened local anxiety regardless of whether the cases are connected.

For now, the suspects remain in the system, the investigation into the gap in the arrest numbers remains open, and Marx remains in hospital. The premeditation prosecutors have alleged — the planning, the restraints, the mutilation — is what will likely drive how harshly the courts treat whoever is ultimately convicted, assuming the case holds together as it moves from magistrate's court toward trial.
