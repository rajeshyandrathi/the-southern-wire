'Rivals' returns to Rutshire, and the split-season gamble keeps paying off

Culture Desk

Rutshire's gloriously overdressed power struggles are back. The second half of Rivals' sophomore season premiered with a three-episode drop on Disney+ internationally and Hulu in the United States, continuing a structure the show committed to at the start of the year: twelve episodes split cleanly into two six-part halves, with the first batch having aired back in May and the second now rolling out through November. It is an unusual release pattern for a series this starry and this expensive, and the fact that the show can sustain a six-month gap between halves without losing its audience says as much about the format as it does about the writing.

The gamble the split season represents is worth sitting with. Streaming platforms spent the better part of a decade training viewers toward full-season dumps, betting that unlimited access would keep subscribers inside the app rather than drifting to a competitor. Rivals has bet the opposite way: that a soapy, character-driven drama built on cliffhangers and scandal is better served by forcing its audience to wait, talk, and come back, the way appointment television always worked before streaming flattened the release calendar. Six months between halves is an aggressive version of that bet, and the fact that the show returned to a waiting audience rather than a diminished one suggests the format is working rather than merely surviving.

Part of why it works is the material itself. Adapted from Jilly Cooper's 1988 novel, Rivals leans fully into the camp, ambition and sexual intrigue of its 1980s setting rather than smoothing it into something more restrained for modern sensibilities, and that commitment to tone has turned out to be the show's real asset. In a streaming landscape crowded with prestige dramas straining for solemnity, a series willing to be unapologetically soapy has carved out a lane almost nobody else is occupying with the same confidence.

The remaining episodes of the back half have not been given a confirmed weekly schedule, and no finale date has been set publicly, which keeps the season's final stretch arriving on the platform's own terms rather than a pre-announced calendar. For a show whose entire release strategy depends on an audience willing to keep showing up without the comfort of knowing exactly when the next episode lands, that uncertainty is arguably part of the design rather than an oversight.

What Rivals has demonstrated, two seasons and one unusual release calendar in, is that audience loyalty can still be built the old way — through good writing, strong performances and a willingness to make people wait — even inside a streaming economy built to discourage exactly that patience. Whether competitors follow the model or simply let Rivals have this lane to itself is the more interesting question the second half of this season leaves open.
