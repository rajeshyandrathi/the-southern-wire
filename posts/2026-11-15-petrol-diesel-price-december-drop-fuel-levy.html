Business & Economy

Petrol could fall by more than R1.50 a litre next month. Whether drivers see it is another question.

Business Desk

South African motorists have spent two weeks living inside a strange arithmetic problem: a temporary fuel levy cut that was supposed to feel like relief, and barely did. When the levy reduction took effect on 4 November, cutting the petrol levy by R1.50 a litre and the diesel levy by R3, the regulated petrol price moved by all of one cent. Diesel fared better, falling by up to R1.20, but petrol's relief got swallowed almost entirely by a nearly 49-cent increase in the slate levy, the mechanism that recovers under-recoveries from previous months, pushing that levy to 135.84 cents a litre and cancelling out most of what drivers were told to expect.

December is where the numbers get genuinely interesting, and genuinely contested. The temporary levy relief steps down on 1 December to R1 a litre for petrol and R1.50 for diesel, continuing at that reduced rate until 5 January 2027. Eyewitness News reports that global and local pricing data point to a petrol drop of more than R1.50 a litre next month, driven by the usual combination of international product prices and the rand's recent performance against the dollar. That would be the kind of number that actually registers at the pump, rather than the one-cent non-event November delivered.

Not every outlet is reading the same data the same way. TopAuto's own modelling projects the opposite outcome for petrol specifically, forecasting an increase of between 53 and 84 cents a litre in December despite the lower levy, on the logic that the slate levy and international benchmark movements could offset the tax relief just as thoroughly as they did in November. Two reputable forecasts pointing in opposite directions, within weeks of the actual announcement, is itself a useful reminder of how little certainty exists in fuel pricing until the Department of Mineral and Petroleum Resources actually publishes the number.

That official figure is expected at the end of November, effective from 2 December, and it will land at a politically sensitive moment. Treasury has been loudly signalling an ambition to scrap fuel levies altogether, a goal that collides directly with the General Fuel Levy's roughly R80-billion annual contribution to the budget, and every month that the temporary relief measure produces a smaller saving than promised adds pressure on Finance Minister Enoch Godongwana to explain how the bigger ambition squares with the smaller reality drivers are actually experiencing at the pump.

For now, the only certainty is the shape of the next six weeks: a scheduled step-down in relief, a disputed forecast on where prices land, and a fuel retailers' association that has already said the R10-billion relief package offers little real ease. Motorists will know which forecast was right by early December. Whether either outcome changes the underlying argument about fuel taxation is a longer fight, and one that outlasts any single month's price announcement.
