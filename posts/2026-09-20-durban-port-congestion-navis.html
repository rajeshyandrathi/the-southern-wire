Durban's port congestion deepens after a rocky switch to new terminal software

Business Desk·Sunday, 20 September 2026

For a country that depends heavily on its ports to move goods in and out of the economy, few stories matter more quietly than what's currently unfolding at Durban's Gateway Terminal. A transition to the new NAVIS N4 terminal operating system in mid-August has triggered exactly the kind of bottleneck logistics planners dread: container ships queuing offshore, cargo delayed, and a ripple effect moving outward through supply chains that depend on predictable turnaround times.

The technical cause is almost mundane compared to its consequences. Terminal operating system migrations are routine events in port management globally, but a rocky rollout at a facility as central to South African trade as Durban doesn't stay a technical footnote for long — it becomes an economy-wide friction point the moment importers and exporters start rearranging schedules around it.

Weeks into the disruption, the backlog hasn't fully cleared, and the costs are accumulating in ways that don't always show up immediately in headline economic data: demurrage charges, delayed inventory, exporters missing shipping windows they can't easily reschedule. For businesses already navigating a challenging trading environment, a congested port adds friction at exactly the point in the supply chain where they can least afford it.

Port authorities will eventually work through the technical issues behind any software transition. The harder-to-resolve question is the one South African logistics operators keep asking after every incident like this one: how a piece of infrastructure this critical to the national economy keeps finding new ways to become a chokepoint, and what it would actually take to build in enough redundancy that a single software migration can't hold a port hostage for weeks at a time.
