Crime & Courts

Matlala says Cele set up a meeting with South Africa's top cop. Mkhwanazi admits money changed hands there too.

Crime Desk

For weeks the Madlanga Commission's most radioactive testimony has orbited one name: Bheki Cele, the former police minister accused of taking a "retirement home" payment from Vusimuzi "Cat" Matlala. This week the orbit widened to swallow someone far harder to sideline. Matlala has told the commission that Cele personally arranged a meeting between him and National Police Commissioner Nhlanhla Mkhwanazi — and Mkhwanazi himself, under his own oath, has conceded that money moved between the two of them at some point in their dealings. That is no longer a story about a retired minister's pension top-up. It is a story about the man currently running the South African Police Service.

The admission did not arrive as a confession so much as a clarification nobody at the commission seemed eager to press further. Mkhwanazi's own account describes an exchange of monies with Matlala without yet spelling out the amount, the purpose, or how many times it happened. In a commission that has spent months picking apart Matlala's shifting figures — the R500,000 "retirement home" claim against Cele already contradicts an earlier R1-million figure attributed to the same alleged transaction — a vague admission from the national police commissioner is the kind of detail that should dominate headlines rather than slide past them.

It does not stand alone. Matlala has also testified that Cele arranged the Mkhwanazi meeting in the first place, which, if true, places the former minister at the centre of introducing a controversial businessman to the country's top police officer rather than merely accepting money from him. That detail matters because it reframes Cele's alleged role from a single bad transaction into something closer to a broker's function, connecting Matlala to people whose cooperation he evidently valued enough to pay for.

Layered onto all of this is Matlala's parallel claim that investigators from the Investigating Directorate Against Corruption wanted Cele arrested and pressured him to fabricate evidence to make that happen — an accusation that, if believed, would mean the very body built to root out this kind of corruption was manufacturing part of the case against the former minister. It is also, conveniently for Matlala, an accusation that undercuts the reliability of the R1-million figure he now disputes, since he attributes that number to the same IDAC investigators he is accusing of dishonesty.

None of this resolves which version of events the commission will ultimately believe, and a commission already strained by one witness's contradictions is now being asked to referee a dispute between that witness and the national police commissioner over money whose terms neither has fully explained. Mkhwanazi has built a public reputation as the officer willing to say uncomfortable things about his own service's corruption; he is now, by his own testimony, part of the uncomfortable thing.

Chairperson Mbuyiseli Madlanga's team is reportedly working toward a final report before the month is out, which leaves little room to chase down a new, barely-detailed admission from the country's top police officer before conclusions have to be drawn. Whatever the commission ultimately writes about Cele, it will now have to write something about Mkhwanazi too — and South Africans will be reading both passages far more closely than the first.
