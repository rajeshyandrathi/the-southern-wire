South Africa can't catch a break this November: storms, hail and extreme fire danger, back to back

Weather Desk

The South African Weather Service has spent most of November issuing one severe warning after another, and the pattern has been unusually relentless even by the standards of a country used to volatile spring weather. Level 4 storm warnings covering hail and lightning hit Gauteng, Mpumalanga, Limpopo and KwaZulu-Natal in the first week of the month alone, and by the second week the warnings had escalated to a level most provinces rarely see more than once or twice a season.

That escalation arrived on 14 November, when SAWS issued a rare level 5 warning for severe storms across KwaZulu-Natal's interior, centred on Howick, Ladysmith and Newcastle, in force from one o'clock in the afternoon until midnight. The warning was explicit about the stakes: potential disruption to water, electricity and communication networks, with residents advised to stay indoors and off the roads wherever possible. A level 5 warning is the Weather Service's highest category, reserved for conditions expected to cause significant, possibly life-threatening, disruption.

The very next day brought another one. A level 5 thunderstorm warning covered the far eastern Free State, western KwaZulu-Natal and the Mpumalanga Highveld on 15 November, arriving alongside a separate, almost contradictory hazard: extreme fire-danger conditions declared across large parts of the country at the same time. That combination, flash-flood-capable storms in some regions and tinder-dry fire risk in others, within the same 48-hour window, is the kind of whiplash that has become increasingly characteristic of South Africa's shoulder seasons as weather patterns grow more volatile year on year.

Heat added its own chapter to the month before the storms took over. Around 9 November, SAWS forecast very hot to extremely hot and uncomfortable conditions for parts of the Northern Cape and the Western Cape interior, with maximum temperatures reaching 36 to 40°C. Coming only days before the level 5 storm warnings further east, the sequence illustrates how differently November has played out across South Africa's regions: baking heat in the west, violent storm systems building and discharging in the east.

What makes a level 5 warning different

South Africa's severe weather warning system runs on a five-level scale, and the jump from the level 4 warnings that opened the month to the level 5 alerts issued on 14 and 15 November is not a small escalation. A level 4 warning typically flags conditions capable of causing localised damage and disruption, serious enough to warrant caution but still within the range forecasters see several times most seasons. A level 5 warning sits at the top of that scale specifically because it signals conditions expected to cause significant, potentially life-threatening disruption across a wide area, the kind of storm system capable of overwhelming drainage infrastructure, downing power lines at scale, and producing hail large enough to cause structural damage rather than simply inconvenience. SAWS reserves that top category deliberately, which is part of why two level 5 warnings landing on consecutive days, in a month that had already seen multiple level 4 alerts, stands out as unusual even against South Africa's famously volatile spring weather patterns.

The practical advice that accompanies a level 5 warning, staying indoors and off the roads for the duration, reflects how quickly conditions at that severity can turn dangerous for anyone caught outside when a storm cell actually breaks. Flash flooding in particular can develop faster than drivers expect on roads that handle normal rainfall without issue, which is why the warning's geographic specificity, naming Howick, Ladysmith and Newcastle directly, matters as much as the severity rating itself for residents deciding how seriously to take it.

A season already running hot

This month's volatility is consistent with a broader pattern meteorologists have been tracking across the 2026/27 summer season generally: a tendency toward more extreme swings between hazard types, rather than simply more rain or more heat in isolation. A season capable of producing extreme fire danger and flash-flood-capable storms within the same 48-hour window, in different parts of the same country, reflects the kind of atmospheric volatility that makes seasonal forecasting increasingly about bracing for a wider range of possible outcomes rather than predicting a single dominant pattern months in advance.

The pattern fits uneasily, but not inconsistently, with the seasonal outlook forecasters have been pointing to all spring: a wetter-than-normal summer for Limpopo and Mpumalanga, against a drier outlook for the southwestern Cape. The storms battering the eastern interior this month are arguably that wetter outlook arriving in its most dramatic possible form, while the Cape's drier trajectory continues to show up not in storm warnings but in the steadier, quieter concern of dam levels that keep sliding even as the region enters what should be among its wetter months.
