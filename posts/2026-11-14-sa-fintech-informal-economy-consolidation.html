South Africa's informal economy is going cashless, and its fintech scene is consolidating to get there first

Tech Desk

For years, the gap between South Africa's formal banking sector and its vast informal economy — the street traders, backyard mechanics, taxi operators and spaza shop owners who move much of the country's day-to-day cash — was filled by a scatter of small, competing apps, each chasing the same underserved customers with barely enough funding to outlast a bad quarter. That picture is starting to change, and the clearest sign of it is who is buying whom rather than who is launching next.

Street Wallet's acquisition of Digitip, a platform built specifically for digital tipping among informal workers, is the latest and most direct example. Digitip's product solved a narrow but real problem: giving car guards, delivery runners and other cash-tip-dependent workers a way to receive payment digitally when customers increasingly carry no cash at all. Folding that into Street Wallet's broader informal-economy payments platform, rather than letting it compete as a standalone app chasing the same small customer base, is exactly the kind of consolidation industry watchers have been predicting as the sector matures past its first wave of scattered startups.

Street Wallet itself raised $350,000 to build out its push into cashless payments for informal traders, a modest sum by the standards of global fintech funding rounds but a meaningful one in a segment where most competitors have been operating on shoestring budgets. SOLmate, a separate player targeting the same demand, has reported doubling its active user base to roughly 100,000 over the past year, growth it attributes directly to the surge in eWallet usage among South Africans who have never held a formal bank account.

The scale of capital flowing into this corner of South Africa's tech sector is not trivial. Industry data attributes roughly 70% of the country's entire startup funding in 2024 to fintech specifically, a concentration that reflects both the size of the underbanked population fintech founders are targeting and investors' conviction that payments infrastructure, rather than any single consumer app, is where the durable value sits.

What consolidation like the Street Wallet-Digitip deal signals is that the market has started to agree with that thesis in a very concrete way: rather than many small apps fighting for the same informal traders, the capital and the user base are beginning to concentrate behind fewer, better-funded platforms. For the millions of South Africans still transacting mostly in cash, which of those platforms wins that race may end up mattering more than any single banking-sector announcement this year.
