Kabila throws in with M23, and Kinshasa braces for a wider war

Politics Desk·Monday, 2 November 2026

Joseph Kabila ruled the Democratic Republic of Congo for eighteen years before handing over power, however reluctantly, in 2019. This month he resurfaced in Goma — a city his former army lost to the Rwanda-backed M23 rebellion nearly two years ago — to announce something that has sent a fresh shock through an already exhausted country: a new political and military coalition built around the very rebel movement that occupies the east.

The Front for the Refoundation of the Nation, as the new grouping calls itself, folds M23's command structure directly into its leadership. Corneille Nangaa and Bertrand Bisimwa, both senior M23 figures, have been named vice-presidents; Sultani Makenga, the rebellion's long-time military commander, becomes the coalition's chief of general staff. For a government in Kinshasa that has spent two years framing M23 as a foreign-backed insurgency rather than a legitimate political actor, having a former head of state formally merge his political capital with the rebels is close to a worst-case scenario.

Kinshasa's response has been swift and severe. The government banned thirteen political parties it says are linked to Kabila, and within days accused the former president of plotting a coordinated "general attack" on the country — language that suggests officials believe this is no longer a containable insurgency in the east but a direct challenge to the state itself. Kabila, for his part, denies any coordination with Rwanda and has dismissed the treason case against him as a political prosecution rather than a genuine legal matter.

All of this lands at the worst possible moment for diplomacy. Peace talks scheduled for Qatar this month were already a long shot given how little ground either side has been willing to concede on the battlefield, where control of towns like Numbi has changed hands multiple times in a matter of days. A new coalition that binds a former president's political network to the armed group doing the actual fighting makes any negotiated settlement harder to imagine, not easier — there are now more interests at the table, and less reason for any of them to compromise.

The international response has started to sharpen too. Washington's decision to sanction a senior Rwandan defence official and a Rwandan bank over their alleged support for M23 signals that outside pressure on the conflict's external backers is intensifying, even as the fighting itself grinds on with no resolution in sight. For millions of Congolese already displaced by years of war, the Kabila-M23 alignment is less a political development to be parsed from a distance than one more reason to fear that the worst of this conflict is still ahead rather than behind them.
