Sudan’s war has hardened into two governments, with no functioning peace process between them

World Desk

Three weeks after Rapid Support Forces fighters stormed the army's garrison headquarters in El-Fasher, Sudan's civil war has settled into something more durable and more disturbing than another contested city: a country effectively split into two states. With El-Fasher's fall, the RSF now holds all five state capitals across Darfur, completing a campaign that began with Zamzam camp and ended with the last major urban centre the army held in the region. What follows a conquest that total is rarely orderly, and Sudan has not been an exception.

Reports from inside El-Fasher in the days after its capture described paramilitary fighters moving through the city carrying out killings and detentions, with medical workers among those reportedly abducted by RSF units. Sudan's Doctors Network said five health staff were taken from the city, part of a pattern of violence against medical infrastructure that has defined this war since it began. The toll from a single raid on Misteriha in North Darfur earlier in the campaign — at least 28 dead, 39 injured including ten women — gives some sense of what "capture" has meant in practice across the region.

The political geography that has emerged from this is now closer to partition than contested sovereignty. Sudan's army-aligned administration operates from Port Sudan on the Red Sea coast, while the RSF runs a parallel government from Nyala in Darfur, complete with its own administrative structures for the territory it controls. Neither side recognises the other's legitimacy, and neither has shown any inclination to negotiate a shared state rather than fight for the whole of it.

The fighting has not stayed confined to Darfur, either. RSF units have pushed into North Kordofan, attacking the town of Bara and intensifying operations well beyond the region the paramilitary group has already secured, a sign that the war's centre of gravity is shifting even as its outcome in Darfur is settled. Diplomatic efforts aimed at a truce have made no visible progress against that momentum; a reported ceasefire framework the RSF had signalled support for in September produced no corresponding halt in fighting on the ground.

The casualty figures that accompany this war carry their own warning about how little is actually known. The United Nations puts the death toll above 40,000, a figure most agencies tracking the conflict consider a significant undercount; other estimates put the real number several times higher, with millions more displaced across Sudan and into neighbouring countries. A war that has already produced the world's largest displacement crisis is now, with Darfur's conquest complete and a rival administration installed in Nyala, showing every sign of entering a second, harder phase rather than approaching an end.
