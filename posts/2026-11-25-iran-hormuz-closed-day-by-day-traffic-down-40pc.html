Iran orders Hormuz squeezed “day by day” as more tankers burn and Gulf traffic falls 40%

Iran’s army chief has told the navy to tighten its grip on the Strait of Hormuz until Washington meets Tehran’s conditions. Two more tankers were reported on fire on Tuesday, even as oil slipped to a three-month low.

World Desk·Wednesday, 25 November 2026·3 min read

Key points

  • Army commander Amir Hatami ordered the navy to close Hormuz “further day by day” until US conditions are met
  • Iran’s army said two more tankers caught fire after hitting mines on Tuesday, 24 November
  • Vessels crossing the strait fell from 226 in the first week of November to 125 last week, Al Jazeera reported
  • Chief negotiator Ghalibaf ruled out talks until Iran’s conditions are met, while Qatar says contacts continue
  • Brent crude traded around $88 a barrel, a three-month low

Iran has raised the stakes in its stand-off with the United States over the Strait of Hormuz. Euronews reported that army commander-in-chief Maj. Gen. Amir Hatami ordered the navy on Monday to close the waterway “further day by day” until Washington meets Tehran’s demands, citing orders from Supreme Leader Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei.

Hatami warned that if the US starts another war, Iran’s response would be “more severe, more crushing and without geographical limitation”, according to Euronews. The order is tied to what Iran calls its seven conditions for talks.

On Tuesday morning, 24 November, Iran’s army said two more oil tankers had caught fire and stopped after hitting a mine, Gulf News reported. Al Jazeera said Iran’s navy claimed the ships had entered a restricted zone with their tracking systems switched off.

Our report on Tuesday covered the three tankers Iran said struck mines on Monday and the attack on Bahrain. This update covers what has changed since then: the new order from Iran’s army, Tehran’s hardened conditions and fresh figures on how far shipping has collapsed.

Traffic through the strait has halved

Al Jazeera, citing Marine Traffic data, reported that 226 vessels entered or left the Gulf between 1 and 7 November, but only 125 between 17 and 23 November, a drop of more than 40%. On Sunday just one vessel exited and two entered, the lowest of the month. Before the war, between 125 and 140 ships passed through every day.

The Revolutionary Guards’ naval chief, Ali Ozmaei, warned shipowners that the strait “is not a place to test unauthorised routes”. The Joint Maritime Information Centre, which tracks shipping in the region, has described Iran’s mine claims as unconfirmed, and recorded 114 passages with US assistance between 19 and 21 November, Euronews reported.

Pressure is also building inside Iran. The National reported that inflation reached 88.7% in October and that about 1,200 trucks were stuck in a 42 km queue at the borders with Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan. It said IRGC-linked media have floated sending an escorted tanker convoy to break the US blockade, although the state broadcaster denied one had set sail.

Talks stuck as Qatar tries to mediate

Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, who leads Iran’s negotiators, said there would be no talks until Washington acts, and spoke of “military ways to break the blockade”. Gulf News listed five of Iran’s seven reported conditions: lifting the naval blockade, easing sanctions, releasing frozen funds, ending hostilities on all fronts and formally declaring the war over.

Qatar’s foreign ministry spokesman, Majed Al Ansari, said on Tuesday that diplomatic contacts were continuing to narrow the gap between the two sides, Gulf News reported. Separately, the Wall Street Journal reported that the US is investigating cyberattacks on as many as 50 commercial vessels, with Iran the main suspect, though this has not been confirmed.

For South African motorists the picture is mixed. Brent crude fell to about $88 a barrel on Tuesday and US crude to about $79, both three-month lows, according to Gulf News. But with Iran promising to tighten its hold on a route that once carried a large share of the world’s oil, any further strike on tankers or terminals could quickly push fuel prices back up.
