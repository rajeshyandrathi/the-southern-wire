No applications, no licence, no end in sight: Starlink's year-long standoff with ICASA

Tech Desk

Starlink still cannot legally operate in South Africa, and the clearest sign of how entrenched the standoff has become is this: in a parliamentary reply earlier this year, Communications Minister Solly Malatsi confirmed that ICASA has not received a single application from the satellite provider for any of the licences it would need — not for spectrum, not for an electronic communications network, not for an electronic communications service. More than a year into a very public fight over access, the company has not filed the paperwork that would actually start the regulatory clock.

The obstacle both sides agree on, even as they disagree about the fix, is ICASA's ownership requirement. The regulator reads the Electronic Communications Act as demanding that individual licence holders be at least 30% owned by historically disadvantaged groups — a rule Starlink's structure, wholly owned by SpaceX, cannot meet without some alternative mechanism. ICASA reaffirmed that reading again in May, closing off any suggestion that the requirement might simply be waived administratively.

Malatsi's attempted workaround was a policy direction, gazetted in December last year, that would let satellite operators meet the ownership test through equity-equivalent investment programmes — essentially committing to local investment, skills development and procurement spending rather than handing over a slice of equity outright. It is the same mechanism global technology firms have used elsewhere to satisfy local-ownership rules without diluting control. But ICASA's own response in May was that implementing it properly would require amending the Electronic Communications Act itself, not just issuing a ministerial direction — pushing the fix out of the regulator's hands and into Parliament's, where the ministry has said it intends to pursue legislative changes.

That legislative path has no fixed timeline, and in the meantime, the people actually affected are not Starlink's shareholders but its South African customers. Reports surfaced in late October of disconnection notices going out to Starlink subscribers as the roaming and licensing situation hardened, with South African users facing restrictions tied directly to the absence of a local licence. SpaceX used ICASA's August spectrum hearings to press publicly for clarity on licensing, ownership and fees, a sign the company is engaging with the regulator even without having filed the formal applications the minister says are missing.

What makes the deadlock particularly stark is how little movement either side needs to make to end it in principle: a legislative amendment on one side, a licence application on the other. Neither has happened, and until one does, the service remains in a legal grey zone that leaves rural and under-served South African users — the population Starlink markets itself most directly to — caught in a fight they have no part in and no leverage over.
