Lifestyle

The whales are still in Walker Bay. They won't be much longer.

Lifestyle Desk

Hermanus is entering the final stretch of a whale season that has, by most local accounts, delivered exactly the spectacle visitors come for: southern right whales gathering close to shore in Walker Bay to mate, calve and nurse their young in waters sheltered enough that sightings barely require binoculars. The season generally runs from around July through to November, which means the coming weeks represent the last reliable window before the whales begin their long migration back toward Antarctic feeding grounds, leaving the bay quiet again until next winter.

What makes Hermanus unusual among the world's whale-watching destinations is how little effort the experience demands. In most other locations, seeing a southern right whale up close requires a boat charter and a fair amount of luck. Hermanus's cliffside paths let visitors watch mothers nursing calves from dry land, close enough to see the barnacle-like callosities that distinguish individual whales, a combination of accessibility and intimacy that has made the town one of the most reliably rewarding wildlife destinations on the South African coast.

The town's annual Whale Festival earlier in the season drew the crowds that typically accompany peak sightings, swelling Hermanus's population many times over and testing the limits of local accommodation and transport. Shuttle services and guesthouses that filled up weeks in advance during the festival period have more availability now, which locals quietly consider one of the better-kept secrets of the late season: fewer crowds, still-reliable sightings, and a town that has settled back into something closer to its everyday rhythm.

City Sightseeing's new guided tours from Cape Town, launched for this winter season, have made the roughly two-hour drive down the coast easier for visitors without their own transport, bundling the journey with narrated stops along one of the country's more scenic coastal routes. For international visitors squeezing a Cape Town trip into a tight itinerary, a day tour that combines whale watching with wine country scenery along the way has become one of the more efficient ways to experience two of the Western Cape's signature draws in a single outing.

Marine biologists monitoring the population generally describe the mothers and calves lingering longest in the bay, often staying closest to shore in the sheltered coves where newborns are least vulnerable to rough water and predators. For visitors weighing whether to make the trip before the season closes, that lingering pattern is good news: the next few weeks are likely to be some of the most reliable sighting conditions left before the bay empties out for the summer.
