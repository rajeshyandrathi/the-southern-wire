Fourteen days, three metros, one ANC decision: Gauteng’s coalition scramble begins in earnest

The Southern Wire Desk·Johannesburg

South Africa's local government elections were supposed to settle something. Ten days after the 4 November vote, they have instead opened the most fragmented round of coalition bargaining the country's metros have seen since 2021. Nationally, the ANC's support collapsed to 30.8%, down from 46% in the last local poll, while the DA climbed from 21.7% to a share strong enough to lead in five of South Africa's eight metropolitan municipalities. Only in Cape Town did any single party cross the 50% line outright. Everywhere else, the maths now runs through rooms full of smaller parties that spent election night as afterthoughts and have spent every day since as kingmakers.

Johannesburg is the sharpest version of that problem. The DA opened a historic gap on the ANC in the city, its best result there in a generation, and still fell short of the 40% that would have made the mayoralty a formality. Fifteen smaller parties now hold council seats, which means a working majority has to be assembled rather than simply claimed, and every one of those parties knows exactly how much that makes its handful of seats worth. Tshwane and Ekurhuleni tell a similar story: the DA emerged as the largest party in all three Gauteng metros without winning any of them outright.

The clock is doing as much work as the negotiators. South African law gives a newly elected council fourteen days from its declaration to hold its first sitting and elect a mayor, a deadline that has already forced ActionSA's Herman Mashaba into the centre of the conversation, whether or not his party's seat count would normally justify it. Daily Maverick's framing of the stakes — Mashaba holding the key, the ANC holding the lock — captures a dynamic that is now playing out in real time, with the ANC's national executive committee convening specifically to settle its Gauteng coalition strategy before the DA's scheduled meeting with ActionSA.

What that meeting is actually about is a trade South Africans have seen attempted before and rarely seen land cleanly: whether the DA cedes Tshwane to secure Johannesburg, using ActionSA as the hinge that makes both deals possible at once. The ANC's calculation is messier. Propping up a DA-led administration in exchange for influence risks looking like surrender to its own branches; refusing to deal risks ceding South Africa's commercial capital to an opposition coalition for the first time in the city's history. Neither option plays well with a party already explaining a 15-point national decline to restive provincial structures.

Johannesburg has had nine administrations and eight mayors in the past decade, a statistic residents now recite the way other cities recite their weather. Whatever combination of parties emerges from this round of talks inherits that reputation for instability before it inherits anything else — a budget to pass, water infrastructure to fix, a billing system still not trusted by half the ratepayers forced to use it. The fourteen-day clock does not care whether the deal that beats it is durable. It only cares whether a mayor is sworn in on time, which is exactly the kind of pressure that has produced South Africa's shakiest coalitions in the past.
