SAWS's summer outlook: a hotter, drier season for parts of South Africa

Weather Desk

Beyond the week-to-week churn of thunderstorm warnings and heat advisories, the South African Weather Service's seasonal outlook is pointing to a more structural concern heading into summer: a developing El Nino pattern that typically tilts the odds toward below-normal rainfall and above-normal temperatures across large parts of the country. That is a very different kind of forecast from the daily bulletins residents are used to checking before deciding whether to carry an umbrella; it is a statement about the character of the entire season ahead.

El Nino's fingerprints on southern African weather are well documented across past cycles, typically suppressing rainfall over the eastern and northeastern interior while leaving the western half of the country comparatively less affected. For farmers in the maize belt stretching across the Free State and Mpumalanga, a below-normal rainfall season arriving on top of already variable soil moisture levels from recent years raises early concerns about planting decisions that typically need to be locked in well before the full scale of any shortfall becomes apparent.

The outlook does not mean an absence of rain altogether, and SAWS has been careful to note that showers and thundershowers will still occur even within a broadly drier pattern. The distinction matters for how the season actually unfolds on the ground: a below-normal season can still include intense, localised downpours capable of causing flash flooding, even as cumulative rainfall across the season ends up well short of historical averages. Residents should expect a summer defined less by steady, predictable rain and more by sharp swings between dry spells and sudden, intense weather events.

Above-normal temperatures carry their own set of knock-on effects beyond simple discomfort. Hotter, drier conditions elevate wildfire risk across the grasslands and fynbos regions that have already seen extreme fire danger warnings issued multiple times this spring, and they place additional strain on a water supply system in several provinces that has not fully recovered reserve capacity from previous dry cycles. Municipalities already managing ageing water infrastructure will be watching this outlook closely as they plan restrictions and allocation strategies for the months ahead.

Seasonal outlooks are probabilistic rather than deterministic by nature, and El Nino's influence on any individual week's weather remains loose enough that short-range forecasts will keep mattering just as much as this broader picture. But for anyone planning further ahead, whether a commercial farmer deciding what to plant or a municipal water manager setting restriction schedules, SAWS's seasonal signal is now clear enough to act on: this looks set to be a hotter, and in many places drier, summer than South Africans have grown used to.
