Pretoria pushes back on Washington's AGOA decision and vows to find new export markets

Politics Desk·Tuesday, 3 November 2026

A day after Washington confirmed it is suspending South Africa's duty-free access under the African Growth and Opportunity Act, Pretoria has hit back, arguing the decision rests on concerns that were never actually raised during the negotiations that have run since 2025. The Department of Trade, Industry and Competition said South Africa had tabled additional concessions during those talks, only for the United States to shift its objections at the eleventh hour, leaving officials little room to respond before the suspension was announced.

Trade, Industry and Competition Minister Parks Tau framed the outcome as a setback rather than a final word, saying a resolution still depends on both governments finding common ground, and that Pretoria remains open to further engagement inside the 60-day window Washington has allowed before the suspension takes effect in January 2027. The tone from government was notably more combative than the department's initial, more muted response a day earlier, which had stressed that AGOA covered only a modest slice of the trade relationship.

That framing isn't wrong, even if it understates the pain for specific industries. Government figures show AGOA accounted for roughly 10% of South Africa's exports to the US in 2025, worth around $1.7 billion, down sharply from 28% and about $4 billion the year before as preferences were already being wound back. The remaining 89% of exports to the US move under ordinary World Trade Organization most-favoured-nation terms, unaffected by Wednesday's decision. But that national-level math offers little comfort to the citrus growers and other agricultural exporters who relied on AGOA's duty-free terms to stay price-competitive in the American market and now face a steep new tariff cliff.

Pretoria's response leans heavily on diversification. The dtic says it will accelerate efforts to redirect export volumes toward the rest of the African continent, India, the European Union and China, framing the pivot as a way to protect jobs that would otherwise be exposed to a single, increasingly unpredictable trading partner. Officials have also pledged company-by-company support for exporters caught in the transition, and say they will keep in place the measures introduced earlier this year in response to Washington's separate reciprocal tariff regime.

Washington's own account of the decision points elsewhere. The US Trade Representative's office says the suspension followed a routine annual eligibility review that found South African practices inconsistent with AGOA's criteria, while the US ambassador called the outcome regrettable and laid the blame squarely on Pretoria's policy choices. Adding an extra edge to the diplomatic moment, the European Union's ambassador to South Africa pointedly noted that EU-SA trade relations remain business as usual — a quiet suggestion that Pretoria has options beyond Washington, even as its own negotiators scramble to use what time is left.
