Gallo Music Africa turns 100, and this year's SAMA nominations lean toward fresh faces

Culture Desk

A hundred years is a long time for any company to survive, and an especially long time for a record label built in a country whose political and economic upheavals have swallowed entire industries more than once. Gallo Music Africa marked its centenary in Johannesburg this week with an event that doubled as both a celebration of the label's archive, one of the deepest and most historically significant in African recorded music, and a reminder of how much of South Africa's musical identity across genres and generations has passed through its catalogue at some point.

Founded in the 1920s and surviving waves of technological disruption, from the shift to vinyl, to cassette, to CD, to streaming, that have each in turn threatened to make traditional record labels obsolete, Gallo's continued relevance owes less to nostalgia than to a catalogue deep enough to keep generating licensing revenue across decades, and to a willingness to keep signing new artists even as the label's commercial identity has increasingly become bound up with its historical one.

That generational question was very much alive elsewhere in the industry this week, with the South African Music Awards releasing a nominations list that leans more heavily toward newer and breakthrough acts than in recent years. Among the names drawing attention is Will Linley, nominated in two categories for his debut album, a result that award-watchers have read as a sign the SAMAs are working harder to recognise artists earlier in their careers rather than waiting for an established track record.

The timing of both stories, an institution marking a century against a backdrop of fresh nominees pushing into an industry it helped shape, has not gone unremarked in local music press, several of whom have framed the week as a kind of informal changing of the guard: the infrastructure and archives built by labels like Gallo over a hundred years now underpinning a new generation of artists working in genres and business models the label's founders could not have imagined.

Funding bodies have played their own quiet role in that generational shift. SAMRO's announcement of 120 recipients for its latest Music Creation Support Fund, timed to land in the same week as the centenary celebrations, extends direct financial backing to emerging artists at a stage in their careers when that kind of support is hardest to find elsewhere. For an industry still working out how streaming economics should fairly compensate South African musicians, it is a modest but concrete contribution to keeping the pipeline Gallo's hundred years helped build from running dry.
