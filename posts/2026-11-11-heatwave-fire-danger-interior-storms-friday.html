After Gauteng's storm week, the interior turns hot, dry and dangerous for fires

Weather Desk

The thunderstorms and hail that put Ekurhuleni's rescue teams on alert last week have cleared out of Gauteng, and what has replaced them is a different kind of hazard entirely. The South African Weather Service's outlook points to fine, warm-to-hot conditions holding across much of the interior through Thursday, a dry, settled spell that sounds benign on paper but is exactly the pattern that turns South Africa's spring grass loads into fire risk.

That risk has already been running hot for weeks. Extremely high fire danger warnings have recurred through the Northern Cape, Western Cape, Eastern Cape, Free State, KwaZulu-Natal, Mpumalanga and Limpopo at various points this spring, a spread that covers the bulk of the country's interior and reflects a wider pattern than any single province's bad luck. A wet winter in parts of the country left behind unusually heavy grass cover, and dry, warm spells like the one settling in this week are precisely what converts that cover into tinder.

Relief, such as it is, is not far off — but it comes from the east first. Isolated to scattered thundershowers are expected to return over the eastern provinces from Friday, reintroducing the same volatile mix of heat and moisture that produced last week's hail and flash flooding in Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal. For farmers and fire services, that is a trade rather than a reprieve: less fire danger in exchange for more flood and lightning risk, with little middle ground in between.

The whiplash between hazard types is becoming the defining feature of this transitional season rather than an anomaly within it. A warning level that eases from severe thunderstorm risk to high fire danger within the space of a week says less about any one forecast being wrong than about how finely balanced South Africa's spring weather has become between drought-adjacent dryness and sudden, destructive wet-season volatility.

For now, the advice from fire services in the provinces under the longest-running warnings has not changed in weeks: no open burning, extra caution with any outdoor flame, and an awareness that a landscape this dry can carry a spark much further and much faster than people expect — right up until Friday's thundershowers arrive to change the risk calculation all over again.
