Two European fintech giants are circling South African banking at the same time

Tech Desk

South Africa's banking market is about to get a lot more crowded, and not by local upstarts this time. Wise has secured conditional regulatory approval to launch in the country, marking the London-listed money transfer giant's first direct move onto the African continent, while Revolut has separately filed its own application for a South African banking licence, a step that would give it its own first African foothold. Two of Europe's most recognisable fintech brands are now pursuing entry into the same market within months of each other, a coincidence that says as much about South Africa's regulatory environment as it does about either company's individual strategy.

For South African consumers, the appeal is obvious enough: both companies have built reputations abroad on transparent, low-fee international transfers and multi-currency accounts, services that remain frustratingly expensive and opaque through most incumbent South African banks. A local market where cross-border remittances, offshore investment flows and international e-commerce payments all carry meaningful friction is exactly the kind of environment these companies have targeted successfully elsewhere.

They will not be arriving to an empty field. Local players have spent the past two years building out exactly the kind of challenger infrastructure Wise and Revolut would otherwise need to construct from scratch. Lesaka's pending acquisition of Bank Zero, Mukuru's new Mastercard-enabled account launched with Bank Zero as sponsor, and payments infrastructure players like Littlefish and TurnStay have all been racing to capture the same underserved segments: fee-conscious retail customers, informal-sector workers, and cross-border traders who have historically been poorly served by the big four banks.

Regulatory approval in South Africa has never been a fast or purely procedural process, and conditional approval is not the same as a finished licence. Wise's next steps will likely mirror what domestic challengers have already experienced: satisfying the Reserve Bank's Prudential Authority on capital adequacy, governance and operational resilience before any public launch becomes possible. Revolut's application sits even earlier in that pipeline, with no confirmed timeline for when, or whether, it clears the same hurdles.

What makes this moment notable is less any single company's progress than the signal it sends about how South Africa is now viewed from outside. A market long characterised by high banking fees and limited competition is suddenly attractive enough to pull in two of the sector's most prominent global disruptors at once, arriving just as local challengers like Bank Zero and TymeBank have spent years softening the ground. Whichever European entrant actually launches first, South African banking customers stand to be the clearest beneficiaries of a fight that domestic players started and outsiders are now racing to join.
