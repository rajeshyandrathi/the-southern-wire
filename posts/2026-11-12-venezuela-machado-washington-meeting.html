Machado heads to Washington, and the question of who actually leads Venezuela gets louder

World Desk

Ten months after American forces captured Nicolas Maduro in an overnight raid, Venezuela's political future is being negotiated less inside the country than in meetings scheduled around Washington calendars. Trump has said he plans to meet opposition figure Maria Corina Machado there next week, a sit-down that arrives just as his administration continues insisting Venezuela "has to be fixed" before any election can be scheduled, with acting leadership under Delcy Rodriguez, not Machado, treated by Washington as the government actually cooperating on the ground.

That arrangement puts Machado in an unusual position for an opposition leader who has spent years building her movement specifically on the promise of eventually taking power. Trump has previously said she lacks sufficient domestic support to govern on her own, a judgment that, whatever its merits, has effectively sidelined the figure most associated internationally with Venezuela's democratic opposition in favour of administrative continuity under officials who served the government she spent her career opposing. Next week's meeting may be as much about managing that tension as about any concrete transition planning.

Washington's own messaging has repeatedly tied the country's economic future to its political one, with Trump advisers framing Venezuela's access to global commerce as conditional on continued cooperation with the United States. That framing gives Washington enormous leverage over day-to-day decisions inside Venezuela, from oil exports to basic trade authorisations, but it also means the country's sovereignty over its own economic choices has become, in practice, something negotiated in Washington rather than decided in Caracas.

Oil remains the thread connecting almost every part of this story. Trump has previously touted a Venezuelan commitment to supply barrels of crude to the United States as part of the broader arrangement that followed Maduro's capture, a deal that gives Washington both an economic incentive to keep the current arrangement stable and a lever to apply if cooperation falters. For a country whose economy has been built around oil exports for generations, control over those flows functions as the single most powerful instrument either side has at its disposal.

Ten months of American administration have produced plenty of meetings, oil arrangements and cooperative gestures, but no actual election date, a gap that is becoming harder to explain away as temporary. Whatever comes out of Trump's meeting with Machado next week, the deeper question, whether Venezuela is actually moving toward a genuine democratic transition or simply toward a different, externally managed arrangement, remains as open as it was the week Maduro was taken into custody.
