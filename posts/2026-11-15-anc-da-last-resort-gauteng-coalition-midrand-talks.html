The ANC calls the DA a 'last resort' in Gauteng. Five other parties are about to test what that's worth.

The Southern Wire Desk·Johannesburg

The ANC's national executive committee did not duck the Gauteng question when it gathered at the Birchwood Hotel and OR Tambo Conference Centre in Boksburg on Thursday. It answered it, just not in the way that makes the maths any easier. The NEC resolved that any coalition with the DA in Johannesburg, Tshwane or Ekurhuleni would be treated as a last resort, not a first option, and that there would be no blanket approach to the three metros until the party's leadership says otherwise. President Cyril Ramaphosa had already signalled where he wanted this to land, urging the party to avoid a DA deal in Johannesburg specifically. The ANC Youth League went further, ruling the DA out altogether. The SACP, watching from its own alliance seat, welcomed the resistance outright.

What the resolution does not do is make a coalition appear. The DA topped all three Gauteng metros without a majority in any of them, and in Johannesburg its 34.79% share dwarfed the ANC's 20.25% and ActionSA's 16.27%, numbers that make the DA the obvious governing partner on seat count alone and the least welcome one on internal ANC politics. So this week the ANC did what a party avoiding its most logical partner has to do: it went looking for enough smaller ones to replace it. Delegations met in Midrand with the DA, BOSA, ActionSA, the IFP and the MK Party in a single run of closed-door sessions aimed at finalising coalitions across Gauteng's hung councils before the fourteen-day constitutional clock runs out on any of them.

ActionSA is understood to be the ANC's first preference, which puts Herman Mashaba in the position every kingmaker wants and every analyst warned about: holding a seat count that would normally be a footnote, deciding outcomes that normally belong to the largest party. One version of the ANC's thinking pairs ActionSA with the smaller parties in a coalition that excludes the DA entirely in at least one metro. The DA, for its part, insists it has not concluded talks with anyone yet, which leaves open whether Thursday's resolution is a genuine redline or an opening position before the ANC's own fourteen-day deadline forces a less comfortable compromise.

The political logic cuts in two directions at once. Governing with the DA, even at arm's length, would hand the ANC's internal critics a line about surrender to the party just weeks after national support fell to 30.8%. Refusing to deal with the DA risks leaving the ANC on the outside of South Africa's commercial capital for the first time in its history, watching an opposition-led administration from the sidelines. Every option on the table this week carries a cost the party would rather not pay, which is precisely why the NEC chose the vaguest version of a decision: a preference, not a rule, and a promise of more detail by Tuesday.

None of that detail changes what residents are waiting for. Johannesburg has cycled through nine administrations and eight mayors in a decade, and the fourteen-day clock that forced this week's scramble does not pause for a party working out its own comfort level with the alternative. Whichever combination of parties survives the Midrand talks inherits a city with a billing system half its ratepayers still don't trust and an infrastructure backlog that has outlasted every coalition built to fix it. The ANC may have bought itself room to avoid naming its partner publicly this week. It has not bought the city more time.
