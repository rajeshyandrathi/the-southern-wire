Matlala's Thursday: a High Court trial in session and a commission going dark

Crime Desk

Vusumuzi "Cat" Matlala is fighting on two fronts today, and neither one is going quietly. In the Johannesburg High Court, his attempted murder trial has resumed with the trial-within-a-trial over the circumstances of his arrest still unresolved, as the defence continues to press claims that Musa Kekana was assaulted and suffocated by police on the day he was taken in. A few kilometres away, in a very different proceeding, the Madlanga Commission has pivoted into closed session, hearing in-camera testimony today and again tomorrow, a scheduling shift that effectively shuts the public and the media out of exactly the material Matlala's own lawyers spent last week trying to keep out of the record altogether.

The irony will not be lost on anyone who has followed the commission's back-and-forth with Matlala's legal team. Advocate Annelene van den Heever had pushed for an urgent review of chairperson Mbuyiseli Madlanga's ruling that questioning on the collapsed plea deal could proceed, and separately sought to have the plea-statement evidence heard in camera rather than in open session. The commission has now moved to closed hearings anyway, for its own reasons tied to sensitive testimony from other witnesses, which leaves Matlala's camp in the odd position of having partly gotten what it asked for, just not necessarily on the terms or the timeline it wanted.

Meanwhile the criminal trial grinds on with its own momentum, independent of whatever the commission decides about secrecy. Medical evidence from forensic pathologist Professor Gert Saayman has anchored much of the recent testimony, and the defence's strategy of challenging the admissibility of evidence gathered at the point of arrest has already added weeks to a case that was originally expected to move faster. Court officials revised the trial's schedule earlier this year to accommodate more than thirty sitting days, an acknowledgement that neither side is in a hurry to let this case resolve quickly.

What ties both proceedings together is the same underlying question: how deep did Matlala's alleged relationships with police, prosecutors and politically connected figures actually run, and what happens to those relationships once the testimony is finally heard. The commission was established to probe exactly that kind of criminal infiltration of the justice system, and Matlala has become one of its most closely watched, and most combative, witnesses. His collapsed plea deal, in which he rejected a proposed twelve-year sentence earlier this year, remains the single thread prosecutors and commissioners keep pulling on.

For a public that has watched this saga stretch across months of postponements, recesses and procedural skirmishes, today offers no resolution, only confirmation that both tracks are still very much live. The criminal trial will eventually produce a verdict. The commission's in-camera sessions, by design, may never produce a public transcript at all. Whichever surfaces more first, Matlala's own account of what he knows, and who he dealt with, remains the piece neither forum has fully extracted from him yet.
