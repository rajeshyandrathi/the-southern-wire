Sudan’s war keeps emptying Darfur, one town at a time

World Desk

The numbers out of Darfur this month read like a region being hollowed out in real time. The International Organization for Migration recorded roughly 1,585 people displaced from Mershing, Nyala and Bali al-Sarif in South Darfur alone as insecurity flared in early November, part of a wider count that put the three-day total across North and South Darfur above 5,200. These are not refugees from a single dramatic battle. They are families leaving in waves, town by town, as the front line of Sudan's civil war keeps finding new places to arrive.

The pattern driving that movement is now well established. Rapid Support Forces units and allied militias have repeatedly swept through Darfur towns with the kind of multi-day violence that produces mass casualties and empties neighborhoods within days. Humanitarian officials no longer describe these incidents as isolated shocks; they describe them as the operating rhythm of a war now well into its fourth year.

Infrastructure has become as much a target as territory. Drone strikes this month hit a substation at Merowe and two power stations in Kassala state, cutting electricity across wide areas in a country where hospitals, water pumps and communication networks all depend on a grid that keeps getting put back together only to be struck again.

Diplomacy has not kept pace with any of it. Sudan's ambassador to South Africa said plainly that Khartoum considers it too early to accept the peace plan currently on the table — a blunt signal from a government that has, through four years of war, shown little appetite for a settlement that would require it to share power with the paramilitary force it is fighting. Nature has offered no relief either: scarce rainfall through 2026 has damaged crops across North and West Darfur, compounding a hunger crisis that was already severe.

What results is a crisis with no obvious floor. Aid officials now routinely describe more than half of Sudan's population as needing humanitarian assistance. For the families counted in this month's Darfur displacement figures, though, the war remains intensely local: a specific militia, a specific road out of town, a specific place they are not yet sure it is safe to return to.
