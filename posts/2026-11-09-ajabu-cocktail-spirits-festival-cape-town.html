Cape Town's bars throw open their doors for Africa's biggest cocktail festival later this month

Lifestyle Desk

AJABU Cocktail & Spirits Festival returns to Cape Town from 14 to 22 November, an eight-day citywide event that has grown, in a handful of editions, into what organisers and local hospitality figures now describe without much argument as the continent's most dynamic bar festival. The format is deliberately simple: a single wristband buys entry into a rotating circuit of participating bars across the city, each running its own festival-specific menu, guest shifts and events for the duration, turning what would otherwise be an ordinary night out into something closer to a city-wide crawl with a shared theme.

What makes AJABU worth watching beyond its own programming is what it says about Cape Town's bar scene more broadly. The city has spent the past several years building a genuine international reputation in cocktail culture, anchored by a handful of bars that now regularly appear on global best-of lists, and a festival of this scale and duration functions as both a celebration of that reputation and a practical showcase for international distillers and brand ambassadors who increasingly treat Cape Town as a stop worth making on the global spirits calendar, not simply a regional afterthought.

For the bars themselves, an eight-day festival is as much a logistical and commercial exercise as a creative one. Participating venues typically need to plan guest bartender rotations, secure limited-release spirits and ingredients well in advance, and manage the kind of sustained nightly footfall that a one-off event would never generate — footfall that, for an industry still working to fully recover margins squeezed by several difficult pandemic-era years and subsequent cost-of-living pressures on discretionary spending, represents a meaningful and predictable revenue window rather than a one-night novelty.

The festival's citywide wristband model also does something subtler: it nudges drinkers toward bars and neighbourhoods they might not otherwise visit, spreading the economic benefit of the event beyond the handful of already-famous venues that would capture most of the attention in a less distributed format. For smaller, newer bars trying to build a reputation in a crowded and increasingly competitive Cape Town scene, inclusion in AJABU's lineup functions as a kind of curated endorsement that can meaningfully shift a venue's trajectory over the following year.

With the festival running right up against the start of Cape Town's broader festive-season calendar, AJABU effectively opens the city's busiest stretch of the year, ahead of the Red Bull King of the Air kiteboarding spectacle at Bloubergstrand later in the month and the swell of visitors that typically arrives through December. For a city whose tourism economy leans heavily on exactly this kind of lifestyle programming, an eight-day cocktail festival is not incidental entertainment. It is part of the infrastructure.
