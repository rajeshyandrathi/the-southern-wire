Court throws out the Phala Phala report, pulling the ground from under Ramaphosa’s impeachment

The Western Cape High Court found the Section 89 panel misread its own mandate and made material errors of law. Parliament’s impeachment committee has paused its work, and the ATM is weighing an appeal.

Politics Desk·Wednesday, 25 November 2026·5 min read

Key points

  • A full bench of the Western Cape High Court on Wednesday set aside the 2022 Section 89 panel report on Phala Phala
  • The unanimous court found the panel misconstrued its mandate, went beyond the charges and made material errors of law
  • Steps the National Assembly took on the strength of the report were also set aside
  • The impeachment committee has paused its work pending legal advice; the ATM is considering an appeal

President Cyril Ramaphosa has won the most important round yet in the long fight over the Phala Phala affair. On Wednesday, 25 November, a full bench of the Western Cape High Court in Cape Town reviewed and set aside the 2022 report of the independent Section 89 panel, the document that found he may have a case to answer over the theft of foreign currency from his Limpopo game farm.

In a unanimous judgment, the court found that the panel led by former Chief Justice Sandile Ngcobo had “fundamentally misconstrued” its task, went beyond its lawful remit and made several material errors of law, the Mail & Guardian reported. The court also set aside the steps the National Assembly had taken on the strength of the report. The judgment was written by Judge President Nolwazi Mabindla-Boqwana, sitting with Judges Mark Sher and Ncumisa Mayosi.

The practical effect is that the report can no longer serve as the basis for Parliament’s impeachment committee, which was revived only this year. African Insider reported that the committee has put its work on hold while Parliament’s legal advisers study the ruling, and that a committee meeting planned for Thursday is in doubt.

Why the court threw out the report

The panel’s job was narrow: to decide whether there was enough prima facie evidence that the President had committed a serious violation of the Constitution or the law, or serious misconduct, to justify a full impeachment inquiry. The court found that the panel blurred that test with the higher standard of whether there was sufficient evidence, two tests the judges said serve different purposes. “Not all information is evidence,” The Witness quoted the judgment as saying.

The judges also found the panel strayed beyond the charges it was asked to consider. According to the Mail & Guardian, it took into account material put forward by the EFF and the ATM about the source of the foreign currency and Ramaphosa’s alleged activities in Cape Town and Namibia. Much of the sworn statement by former spy boss Arthur Fraser, whose criminal complaint in June 2022 started the scandal, was treated as hearsay because Fraser had no first-hand knowledge of the February 2020 burglary.

On the panel’s finding about Ramaphosa’s business interests, the court found an error of law and said he had not breached the anti-corruption law in a way that was intentional, malicious or in bad faith, The Witness reported. The court also found that his delay in bringing the review was not unreasonable, and that the Constitutional Court’s ruling in May had dealt with the National Assembly’s 2022 vote, not with whether the panel’s report itself was valid.

How the case got here

The affair dates to 9 February 2020, when thieves broke into Ramaphosa’s Phala Phala farm and took about $580,000 in cash hidden at the property. Ramaphosa has said the money was legitimate payment from a sale of animals at the farm; reports differ on whether it was game or cattle. Investigations by the Public Protector, the Reserve Bank and SARS cleared him in 2023, and the National Prosecuting Authority declined to prosecute in October 2024.

In December 2022 the National Assembly voted 214 to 149 not to refer the panel’s report to an impeachment committee. On 8 May 2026 the Constitutional Court set that vote aside and struck down the parliamentary rule behind it, which meant the report had to go to a committee after all, Daily Maverick reported. Ramaphosa had already gone to the Western Cape High Court to have the report itself reviewed; the case was argued over three days in September and judgment was reserved. On 24 July he obtained an interdict, and the committee has held no public hearings since.

What happens next

The Presidency said Ramaphosa would study the judgment. ATM leader Vuyo Zungula, who first tabled the impeachment motion in July 2022, called the ruling a “dangerous” precedent and said Parliament still had a duty to establish what happened at Phala Phala; his party is considering an appeal, African Insider reported. The impeachment committee’s chair, Makashule Gana, said members were waiting for legal advice. As we reported on Monday, the committee had been due to meet virtually on Thursday evening to settle its terms of reference and choose an evidence leader.

An appeal, if one is brought, would keep the matter in the courts for months. For now, though, the foundation of the only formal impeachment process against Ramaphosa has been removed, and any new attempt would need a fresh, lawful basis. We will report on Parliament’s legal advice and any appeal as they come.
