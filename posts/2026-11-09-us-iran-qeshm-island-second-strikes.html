A second round of US strikes hits Qeshm Island in a week, and Iran says it shot two drones out of the sky

World Desk

The United States has carried out a second round of limited strikes on Iran's Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz, hitting what officials described as an Iranian air-defence system in the Suza area. Iranian state media reported hearing two explosions on the island, and President Trump, asked about the operation by reporters, characterised it as "minor" — a choice of word that captures the strange register this phase of the standoff has settled into, where both sides seem intent on signalling resolve without triggering the kind of exchange that would force either into open war.

Iran's own account of the incident runs in a different direction. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps said it shot down a US MQ-1 drone over Qeshm, with state media separately reporting the downing of an MQ-9, and Iranian outlet Tasnim reported that two missiles struck the island, though no official assessment of damage has been released by either government. The discrepancy between Washington's framing of a contained, defensive strike and Tehran's account of drones downed and missiles landing is itself a feature of this conflict's current phase: both sides have strong incentives to describe the same events in ways that protect their own domestic narrative of strength without escalating the underlying confrontation.

This is not the first such exchange in recent weeks. US forces struck Qeshm on 2 November in what officials at the time called a defensive action, the first attack on the island since June, and Monday's follow-up strike suggests whatever deterrence calculation Washington is running has not yet produced the result it wants. Qeshm sits at a genuinely strategic chokepoint, overlooking shipping lanes through the Strait of Hormuz that carry a significant share of the world's seaborne oil trade, which is precisely why both the island's air-defence systems and any US action against them carry outsized weight relative to the island's modest size and population.

Diplomacy, remarkably, continues to run in parallel with the strikes rather than being abandoned because of them. Qatar has maintained its role as an intermediary and says talks remain active, and US Vice President JD Vance told reporters he remained hopeful a diplomatic resolution was still achievable. Iranian Parliament Speaker Mohammad Baqer Ghalibaf struck a more combustible note, warning that any further American "miscalculation" would draw an Iranian response he described as unpredictable — language calibrated to deter further strikes without explicitly committing Tehran to any specific retaliation.

What emerges from this pattern is a confrontation that has found an uneasy, repeatable rhythm: a strike, a competing narrative about what was actually hit and by whom, a round of diplomatic reassurance, and then, within days or weeks, another strike. Whether that rhythm holds indefinitely or eventually breaks into something less contained remains the open question hanging over every fresh explosion reported from Qeshm's shores.
