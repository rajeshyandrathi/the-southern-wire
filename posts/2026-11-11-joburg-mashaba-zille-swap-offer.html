Mashaba's extraordinary offer: pull Zille from the Joburg mayoral race, and ActionSA says it will deal

The Southern Wire Desk·Johannesburg

For a week, the fight over who runs Johannesburg produced plenty of noise and no movement. The DA finished November's municipal election well clear of the field, taking 34.79% of the vote and 91 council seats to the ANC's 20.25% and 63 seats, with ActionSA a distant third at roughly 16% — enough to make it the kingmaker in a council nobody can govern alone, and not enough to spare the city a fortnight of brinkmanship over who gets to try. On Monday, Herman Mashaba was still calling the DA "racist" and ruling out any coalition with it. By Tuesday, he had sent Geordin Hill-Lewis a letter that changes the shape of the entire negotiation.

The offer is blunt and, by South African coalition standards, unusually self-sacrificing: both Mashaba and Helen Zille step aside from the mayoral race, the DA nominates someone other than Zille to lead the city, and ActionSA takes the deputy mayor's chair in exchange. Everything else — committee seats, policy commitments, the mechanics of an actual governing agreement — would be negotiated once that single condition is settled. ActionSA's Michael Beaumont has confirmed the letter is genuine, which means this is now the official opening position of the province's third-largest party, not a leak or a trial balloon.

The reasoning behind singling out Zille specifically is not subtle. ActionSA has said for weeks that it will not serve under her as mayor, even as it insists it remains open to working with the DA more broadly — a distinction the party has tried hard to draw between the DA as an institution and Zille as an individual candidate. Mashaba's letter effectively tests whether the DA values Zille's candidacy more than it values a governing majority in South Africa's largest city.

That test now has a date attached to it. The DA and ActionSA are expected to sit down for coalition talks on Thursday, the first structured engagement between the two parties since the result came in. Whatever Hill-Lewis brings to that meeting will answer the question the letter poses: whether the DA is willing to trade its own preferred candidate for a majority, or whether it would rather govern without one for longer while it looks for other partners.

The stakes go well beyond who gets the chains of office. Johannesburg's hung council sits inside a province where several other Gauteng metros are facing the same arithmetic problem at once, and a city administration without a confirmed executive cannot easily make the calls — on infrastructure spending, on service delivery contracts, on basic budget approval — that residents are waiting on. Every day the mayoral question stays open is a day those decisions stay open too.

For now, the clock that has defined this story since election night is still running, but for the first time it is running toward something concrete: a meeting, a named condition, and a party publicly willing to walk away from its own candidate to get a deal done. Whether the DA is willing to do the same is the question Thursday is supposed to answer.
