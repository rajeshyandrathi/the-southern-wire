Hermanus rides out the tail end of a bumper whale season

Lifestyle Desk·Wednesday, 4 November 2026

The cliffs above Walker Bay are still delivering the spectacle that made Hermanus famous: southern right whales breaching, lobtailing and occasionally bringing calves close enough to shore to be seen without binoculars. Even this far into November, visitors arriving for what they assume is a last-chance glimpse are finding the bay still generously populated, a sign that this year's season has run later and fuller than the town's tourism operators had dared to promise.

Local guides describe the whales' presence this deep into spring as unusually persistent rather than unprecedented, noting that southern rights have historically lingered into late November in good years before finally heading back out to the open ocean's feeding grounds. What makes this particular stretch valuable to visitors is less rarity than comfort: warmer days, thinner crowds than the September and October peak, and whales that guides describe as noticeably calmer and more predictable in their movements than earlier in the season.

For the boat-based operators licensed to take visitors closer to the animals than the famous cliff path allows, the tail end of the season has become its own quietly profitable niche. Daily departures continue to run through November, and operators report steady bookings from visitors who deliberately timed their trip for exactly this lower-key window, trading the guaranteed abundance of peak season for a more relaxed experience on the water.

The free cliff-path viewing remains, as it always has, Hermanus's real democratising feature: no boat ticket required to watch a whale breach from a public footpath that runs for kilometres along the town's coastline. That accessibility, paired with Hermanus's reputation as the whale-watching capital of South Africa, continues to draw a mix of international tourists and domestic visitors making a long weekend of the Western Cape's coastal towns.

Tourism operators expect the whales to begin thinning out through the back half of November, with most having departed for deeper waters by early December in a typical year. For now, anyone within reach of the Overberg coast still has a window, narrowing but very much open, to catch one of the country's most reliable natural spectacles before it packs up for another year.
