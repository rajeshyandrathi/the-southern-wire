"Totally in charge": Trump's Venezuela, ten months after Maduro's capture, still has no election date

World Desk

Ten months after US forces captured Nicolás Maduro and installed what Donald Trump now openly calls American control over Venezuela, the president's own account of the country's future remains strikingly short on specifics. The United States is "totally in charge" of Venezuela, Trump said this week, adding that elections will happen "at some point" — a phrase that, after ten months of American administration, has started to sound less like a promise than a placeholder.

Maduro himself remains in American legal custody, having pleaded not guilty last month to US charges of conspiracy to commit torture, part of a broader federal case that also names conspiracy to import cocaine and weapons offences against him and his wife, Cilia Flores. His legal team has separately asserted that he retains some form of immunity and urged the court to dismiss the charges outright — an argument that, if it gained any traction, would complicate Washington's ability to present his removal as a clean legal resolution rather than simply a change of power enforced from outside.

Venezuela's domestic picture, as the US administration presents it, point to an easing in some respects: the country says 131 political prisoners have been freed amid what are described as post-Maduro talks, and the United States has removed Venezuela from its official drug-trafficking list, with Trump citing progress against narcotics as part of the justification. Whether that progress reflects a genuinely transformed internal security apparatus or simply a shift in how Washington wants the country's recent history read is a distinction the administration has not clearly addressed.

The arrangement has not escaped scrutiny at home. House Democrats have pushed to obtain records of Trump's own deliberations before the operation that removed Maduro, including, by some accounts, conversations he had with an AI chatbot about the decision — an unusual and still-developing line of inquiry that, whatever it ultimately reveals, underscores how unconventional the decision-making trail behind the operation is alleged to have been.

What remains absent from every account, administration-friendly or critical, is a timeline. "At some point" has governed Venezuela's political future since January, and the longer American administration continues without a stated election date, the harder it becomes to distinguish a transitional arrangement from simply a new, indefinite status quo — one in which the country that was promised a vote is still waiting to be told when it might actually get one.
