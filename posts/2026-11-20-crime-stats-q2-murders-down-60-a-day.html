Crime & Courts

Murders are down 3.5%. South Africa still loses about 60 people a day.

The latest quarterly crime statistics show fewer murders, carjackings and truck hijackings between July and September, but cash-in-transit robberies and kidnappings went up, and police leaders say violent crime remains far too high.

Crime Desk·Friday, 20 November 2026·3 min read

Key points

  • 5,590 murders between July and September, down 3.5%
  • About 60 people killed every day
  • Carjackings down 17%, truck hijackings down 28%
  • Cash-in-transit robberies up from 24 to 36
  • Firearms used in more than 2,500 murders

South Africa’s latest crime figures, released on Friday, carry a modest piece of good news and a much larger warning. Between July and September, police recorded 5,590 murders. That is 204 fewer than in the same three months last year, a drop of 3.5%. It still works out to roughly 60 people killed every day.

Acting police minister Firoz Cachalia did not try to dress the numbers up. Violent crime, he said, remains far too high despite the downward trend. Firearms were the single biggest factor, used in more than 2,500 of the murders, and Cachalia summed up the problem in one line: a firearm turns a dispute into a death. Arguments and fights were the most common trigger, and nearly 3,000 murders happened in public places.

Several categories fell clearly. Carjackings dropped by almost 17%, truck hijackings by about 28% and robberies at homes by 7%. Overall contact crime was down 2.3%. But other numbers moved the wrong way. Kidnappings rose 3.4% to 4,934 cases, attempted murders went up 3%, and cash-in-transit robberies jumped from 24 to 36 in the quarter.

Gauteng recorded the most murders in total, at 1,418, while the Eastern Cape, Western Cape and KwaZulu-Natal carry the highest risk relative to population. The Western Cape accounted for most of the country’s gang-related murders, 208 of 261, and most taxi-related killings too.

Alcohol remains part of the story. Police linked more than 8,000 contact crimes to alcohol, including 271 murders, and said 235 murders happened in or near liquor outlets. More than 6,400 illegal liquor outlets were shut down during the period, and Cachalia said operations against them will be stepped up.

There were 9,908 rapes recorded in the quarter, and of the cases where victim details were verified, more than 4,300 involved children. Fifteen police officers were murdered, down from 23 a year earlier. National commissioner Fannie Masemola said every illegal firearm taken off the streets is a potential life saved, and police reported seizing almost 3,800 of them in the three months.

Where and how people are dying

The detail behind the headline number is grim. Of the murder victims whose age and sex were verified, about 4,800 were men, 581 were women and 149 were children. One single attack accounted for 18 deaths, when gunmen opened fire at a tavern in Wedela on Gauteng’s West Rand.

The Western Cape stands out for firearm killings, with 732 murders by gun, ahead of Gauteng on 675 and KwaZulu-Natal on 560. The two police stations with the most contact crime cases in the quarter were Delft in Cape Town and Inanda in Durban.

What police say is working

Police leaders pointed to large-scale operations as the reason for the falls. Operation Shanela, the national crackdown that runs across all provinces, led to more than 222,000 arrests in the quarter. Police also say they arrested more than 29,000 wanted suspects for serious and violent crimes, including about 1,670 for murder.

Courts sentenced more than 3,200 people to prison in the period, 186 of them to life. In gender-based violence cases, more than 10,000 people were arrested and 668 were sentenced, 88 of them to life imprisonment.

The harder question

A 3.5% fall is welcome, but it leaves South Africa with one of the highest murder rates in the world. The growth in kidnappings and cash-in-transit attacks also shows how organised crime adapts. As Cachalia put it, violent crime remains far too high even while the trend is heading down.

The next set of figures, covering October to December, will include the busy festive season, when police traditionally see a spike in alcohol-related violence and road deaths.
