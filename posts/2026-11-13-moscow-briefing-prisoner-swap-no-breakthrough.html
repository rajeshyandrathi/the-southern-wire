A Moscow handshake, an Irish briefing, and a prisoner swap that still isn't peace

World Desk

Four hours in Abu Dhabi produced the first trilateral sit-down between Russian, Ukrainian and American negotiators since February, and by the time the delegations stood up from the table, they had agreed on exactly one thing that mattered in the near term: an exchange, in principle, of 314 prisoners of war. Everything that actually ends a war — territory, a ceasefire line, the shape of a security guarantee for Kyiv — stayed precisely where it had been sitting for months.

Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner did not wait around to let that stalemate harden. Within a day they were back in the Kremlin, sitting across from Vladimir Putin for roughly two hours, working through what their own account described as broad agreement on principles and continuing disagreement on how to actually get there. From Moscow they flew directly to Ireland to brief Volodymyr Zelensky in person, a travel schedule that said more about the pressure both Washington and Kyiv feel to keep this process alive than any single readout from either meeting.

Kyiv did not make the mood easier. Ukrainian officials used the window around the briefing to say that their satellite communications capability, which has become indispensable to battlefield coordination, had been restricted again — a quiet, deniable form of leverage that has shadowed these talks before and tends to surface exactly when Kyiv needs to signal it is negotiating from under pressure, not from strength.

Then came the disagreement that may matter more than anything said in the Kremlin. Kushner posted that both delegations had agreed to widen the next round to include Europe's representatives. Moscow's response was immediate and flat: its chief foreign policy aide said no such contact with Europeans had been planned in Abu Dhabi and none had taken place, a rebuttal that reads less like a misunderstanding than a marker Russia wants on the record before talks resume.

None of this has slowed the war itself. Strikes continued through the week the envoys were shuttling between capitals, and the front line, by every account, remains essentially frozen. A prisoner exchange is the kind of outcome both sides can point to as proof the process is producing something; it is also, by now, a familiar enough pattern that nobody serious is calling it progress toward a ceasefire. What comes next is another round, expected within the week, with the question of European participation now a live fight rather than a settled fact.
