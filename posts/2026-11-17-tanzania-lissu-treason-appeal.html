Tanzania cleared its loudest opposition voice of treason. It jailed him for speaking anyway.

Politics Desk

A three-judge panel in Dar es Salaam delivered a verdict on Tundu Lissu earlier this month that managed to be merciful and punitive in the same breath. The judges, led by Justice Dunstan Ndunguru, found that prosecutors had failed to prove Tanzania's best-known opposition figure intended to intimidate the government — the core element of a treason charge that could have carried a death sentence. Lissu walked away from that charge entirely. He did not walk away from prison. The same panel convicted him of incitement over remarks calling for the disruption of the country's 2025 general election, and sentenced him to four years.

The distinction between the two charges matters less for what it says about the specific evidence than for what it says about the state's priorities. Tanzania's courts declined to kill Lissu's case with the bluntest instrument available to them, and then reached for a narrower one that accomplishes much the same political result: removing the country's most prominent government critic from public life for years rather than executing him for it. Amnesty International called the incitement conviction a travesty of justice and demanded his release outright, rejecting the idea that a softer charge makes for a softer injustice.

Lissu's party, Chadema, has already signalled it will appeal, and its chief legal counsel has said he will separately apply for bail while that appeal proceeds. Both moves buy time rather than certainty. If the four-year sentence survives appeal, one consequence looms larger than the prison term itself: a conviction of this kind could bar Lissu from standing in Tanzania's next election in 2030, closing off the very route back to influence that the treason acquittal seemed to leave open.

That is the quieter, more durable form of political engineering at work here. A treason trial that ends in execution draws international condemnation measured in days. A conviction that simply disqualifies a candidate from a future ballot draws statements, then fades from attention well before the election it was timed to affect. The US ambassador to Tanzania said he was closely following the trial's outcome; close following is not the same as consequence, and governments across the continent have learned exactly how little daylight separates the two.

Lissu's case sits inside a wider pattern that rights groups have been documenting with increasing alarm: prominent government critics across several African states facing treason or treason-adjacent charges that function less as prosecutions than as removals. The charges vary in severity and the courts vary in how far they are willing to go, but the effect across cases is strikingly consistent — a loud critic becomes a legally disqualified one, and the underlying grievance that made them loud in the first place goes unaddressed.

For a continent already watching several governments tighten the space available to opposition politics, Lissu's conviction is a test of how much international attention a single case can sustain once the most dramatic possible outcome — a death sentence — has been taken off the table. The early signs are not encouraging: the verdict has already slipped from leading a news cycle to filling a paragraph in it, exactly the trajectory a four-year sentence, as opposed to a gallows, is built to produce.
