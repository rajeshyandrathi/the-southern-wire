Poland moves to close the sky on its Ukrainian border after Russian missiles landed

Warsaw is drafting rules for a restricted flight zone along its eastern border that would let pilots fire on targets they cannot see, and it wants NATO to consider downing Russian missiles over Ukraine.

World Desk·Wednesday, 25 November 2026·4 min read

Key points

  • Deputy Defence Minister Cezary Tomczyk announced the plan on Wednesday, 25 November
  • The zone would run from about a dozen to several dozen kilometres inland from the border
  • Pilots could engage targets using radar and allied data without seeing them
  • Aim is to have it in force by late December or early January
  • Poland has also asked NATO to consider intercepting Russian missiles over Ukraine; no decision yet

Poland is preparing to close the skies along its border with Ukraine. Deputy Defence Minister Cezary Tomczyk said on Wednesday that the government is drafting an order for a restricted flight zone running from about a dozen to several dozen kilometres inland, UNITED24 Media reported, citing Reuters.

Tomczyk described it as “a limited zone” to support military operations. The aim is to have the rules in force by late December or early January, according to the Kyiv Independent’s daily war briefing, carried by Yahoo News.

The key change is what Polish pilots would be allowed to do inside it. Under a principle known as positive identification, they could engage an aerial target on the strength of radar data, allied information, altitude and speed, without first having to see it, UNITED24 reported. Warsaw also wants other NATO countries on the alliance’s eastern flank to adopt similar rules.

Why Warsaw is acting now

The plan follows the Russian Kh-101 cruise missiles that came down in south-eastern Poland on Friday 20 November during heavy attacks on Ukraine, as this site reported on Tuesday. They spent about three minutes in Polish airspace, and two Swedish Gripen fighters on NATO air policing duty could not identify them in time because of poor weather. Clash Report said a Russian reconnaissance drone also briefly crossed the border on 24 November.

That weather problem is exactly what positive identification is meant to solve. If pilots must see a target before firing, a missile can cross the border and land before anyone is allowed to act.

Existing rules already limit civilian flying near the eastern border, restricting flights below about 3 km during the day and generally banning them at night, UNITED24 reported. The new zone would go further by giving the military clearer rules of engagement.

A harder question for NATO

Poland has also asked its allies to consider shooting down Russian missiles over Ukraine itself, before they reach NATO territory. It raised the idea at a meeting of alliance ambassadors in Brussels on 23 November, the Kyiv Independent reported. Some allies were sympathetic, but no decision was taken.

Foreign Minister Radosław Sikorski has argued that intercepting Russian missiles over Ukraine at Kyiv’s request could count as self-defence. Tomczyk, however, acknowledged that NATO does not support the proposal because of the complications it would bring, since it could be seen as drawing the alliance directly into the war.

For now NATO has sent an airborne early-warning aircraft to Poland and is assessing whether more forces are needed. The draft order on the border zone is the next thing to watch, along with whether neighbours such as the Baltic states follow Poland’s lead.
