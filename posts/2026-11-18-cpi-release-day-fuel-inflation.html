Today's the day South Africa finds out how bad the fuel shock really was

Business Desk

Statistics South Africa releases its October consumer price index today, and for once the number genuinely is in doubt right up to publication. September's reading came in at 4.5%, itself a rise from August's 4.4% and already ahead of what most economists had forecast, driven largely by transport costs that climbed 10.8% year-on-year. October had one job: show what happens to that trend once South Africa's biggest fuel price increase of the year works its way fully into the basket.

That increase was not subtle. The regulated price of 95 octane petrol rose by R3.33 a litre from the start of October, pushing it above R30.25 and into record territory, while diesel climbed by as much as R3.24 a litre to beyond R35. Both increases landed well above the more modest hikes economists had pencilled in only weeks earlier, which is why October's CPI print carries more weight than a routine monthly release: it is the first full reading of a fuel shock large enough to move the entire inflation basket on its own, without any help from demand-side pressure the Reserve Bank's tools are actually designed to influence.

Economists surveyed ahead of today's release have been divided mainly on magnitude, not direction. Several expect headline inflation to clear 5% for the first time this cycle, a threshold that matters less for its round-number symbolism than for what it implies about the composition of the increase: a jump driven almost entirely by a regulated fuel price the South African Reserve Bank cannot touch, arriving one day before the Bank has to decide whether to raise interest rates to fight it anyway.

That is Thursday's problem, but it is being set today. The Monetary Policy Committee's rate decision, already described as the tightest call of the year before this release, now rests heavily on a number households will feel in their own fuel tanks before the Bank even opens its meeting. A print that clears 5% narrows Governor Lesetja Kganyago's committee's room to hold rates steady; a print that stays contained gives the Bank space to let September's hike continue doing its work through the rest of the summer without another increase stacked on top.

Households, in the meantime, are caught in a policy mismatch that no single data release can fix. Fuel levy relief promised for December is meant to ease pressure at the pump just as today's number confirms how much pressure there has been to ease. If the Reserve Bank responds to a high October print by raising rates again, families already absorbing record petrol and diesel prices would face higher borrowing costs at the same moment government tax relief is supposed to be softening the blow — two arms of economic policy working in opposite directions during exactly the weeks South African households can least afford it.
