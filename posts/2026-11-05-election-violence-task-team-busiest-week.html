The task team built to stop political killings faces its busiest week yet

Crime Desk

For the detectives attached to South Africa's political-killings task teams, election week did not end when the polls closed on Wednesday night. If anything, it marked the start of the harder part of the job: turning a run of politically tinged shootings, intimidation cases and at least one assassination of a party official into prosecutable dockets, at the same time as the ordinary business of counting and declaring a result unfolds around them.

The unit, first constituted in KwaZulu-Natal in 2018 after a wave of killings of local councillors and party officials, has built a track record that police leadership points to whenever political violence flares again: a detection rate north of 65 percent and, among cases that reach court, a conviction rate reported at above 80 percent, figures that stand out against South Africa's broader struggles with case backlogs and low prosecution rates for violent crime. That history is now being tested against a fresh and immediate caseload generated by this week's vote.

Investigators are working multiple threads at once: the murder of the MK Party organiser shot dead in the hours before polls opened, a string of intimidation complaints logged in contested wards, and the ballot-tampering and storage-site incidents the IEC has already referred for criminal investigation. Detectives say the pattern is a familiar one from past elections, violence concentrated in the handful of wards where the margin between parties is thinnest and the local stakes, control of patronage networks, tender access, and employment at municipal level, are highest.

What has changed since the task team's founding is the political map it now has to navigate. Where earlier waves of killings played out largely within and around the ANC, the current caseload spans a far more fragmented landscape, implicating MK Party, EFF and ANC structures in various provinces, and occasionally crossing party lines in ways that complicate the question of motive the task team exists to untangle. Officers say that complexity, not a shortage of leads, is the main obstacle to quick arrests in the organiser's killing.

Civil society groups monitoring the election have renewed calls this week for the task team's mandate and funding to be made permanent and extended beyond KwaZulu-Natal, rather than treated as an emergency response that activates and fades with each electoral cycle. Police leadership has not committed to that, but with a contested result now working its way through coalition talks in several metros, few expect this week's caseload to be the last the unit sees before the new councils are even sworn in.
