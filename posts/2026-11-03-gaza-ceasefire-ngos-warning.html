More than 100 aid groups say Gaza's ceasefire exists on paper only

World Desk·Tuesday, 3 November 2026

More than a year after the Gaza ceasefire formally took hold in October 2025, over 100 humanitarian organisations issued a blunt joint assessment this past week: the truce, they said, exists in name only. The warning followed a weekend in which Israeli strikes killed at least eight Palestinians, including several police officers, in bombardment that Israel says was a response to Hamas rearming and launching attacks, while Hamas denies the accusation and says Israel is the party violating the agreement.

Gaza's health ministry now puts the death toll since the ceasefire took effect at upward of 1,580 Palestinians killed and more than 5,700 wounded — a toll that, measured against the stated purpose of a truce meant to end large-scale killing, has led aid groups to argue that the agreement has functioned less as a genuine cessation of hostilities than as a rebranding of a conflict that never actually stopped.

The humanitarian picture inside Gaza compounds the political failure. The United Nations estimates that 1.4 million people face crisis-level hunger or worse through the end of the year, while more than 90% of the territory's population is living in tents, under plastic tarpaulins, or inside partially destroyed homes more than a year after the ceasefire was meant to open the door to reconstruction and recovery.

For mediators who spent months brokering the original agreement, the one-year mark was supposed to be an occasion to point to incremental progress — reconstruction beginning, aid flows stabilising, violence tapering toward zero. Instead, the joint NGO statement amounts to an indictment of how little of that has actually materialised, with strikes, casualties and acute deprivation all persisting well past the point the truce was meant to have resolved them.

Whether renewed diplomatic pressure can revive the ceasefire's original intent, or whether this is simply the shape the agreement will continue to take indefinitely, remains the central unanswered question as the conflict's second year under a nominal truce begins.
