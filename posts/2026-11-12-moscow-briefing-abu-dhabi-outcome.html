Two envoys fly to Moscow carrying an Abu Dhabi deal that settled almost nothing

World Desk

Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner are expected in Moscow today to brief Vladimir Putin on the outcome of two days of trilateral talks in Abu Dhabi, a trip that will test whether a narrow prisoner-exchange agreement can be stretched into something resembling genuine diplomatic momentum. The talks produced a deal both sides can point to as evidence of progress, but they stopped well short of the ceasefire framework Washington had reportedly hoped to extract, leaving the actual substance of the war, Russia's demand that Ukraine abandon its NATO ambitions and accept hard limits on its military, exactly where it was before the delegations sat down.

The gap between what Abu Dhabi delivered and what a genuine breakthrough would require is the story Witkoff and Kushner now have to manage in front of Putin. A prisoner swap is meaningful to the families involved and useful as a confidence-building gesture, but it does not address a single one of the structural disagreements that have kept this war grinding on for years. Briefing Putin on a deal this limited, while still framing the overall engagement as productive, requires a diplomatic balancing act that both envoys have had considerable practice performing over recent months.

Russia's continued strikes on Ukraine's energy infrastructure throughout the negotiation window underline how little the talks have actually constrained battlefield behaviour. A sustained winter campaign against Ukrainian power generation has continued even as negotiators sat in Abu Dhabi discussing a pathway toward peace, a pattern that has repeated across nearly every previous round of talks: diplomacy proceeds on one track while military pressure continues uninterrupted on another, each side calculating that neither needs to pause the fighting simply because envoys are in the room.

For Ukraine, the NATO and military-limits demands remain the two conditions it has consistently signalled it cannot accept without abandoning the security guarantees it has spent the entire war trying to secure. That fundamental incompatibility is precisely why previous rounds of talks, in Istanbul, in Geneva, and now in Abu Dhabi, have kept producing partial agreements on peripheral issues like prisoner exchanges while leaving the core territorial and security questions untouched. Each partial deal buys diplomatic goodwill without forcing either side to confront the trade-offs a real settlement would require.

What happens after today's Moscow briefing will say more about where this process is actually headed than anything that happened in Abu Dhabi itself. If Putin treats the prisoner deal as a foundation to build on, a further round of talks becomes more likely in the coming weeks. If he treats it as confirmation that Russia can extract concessions without conceding anything structural in return, the pattern of talks without resolution that has defined this war's diplomacy for years will simply continue into its next iteration.
