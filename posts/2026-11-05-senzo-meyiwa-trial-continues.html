Twelve years on, the Senzo Meyiwa murder trial returns to a familiar Pretoria courtroom

Crime Desk

There is a weary ritual to the Senzo Meyiwa trial by now. Court officials, lawyers and the handful of family members who still attend most sessions filed back into the North Gauteng High Court this week for another sitting in a case that has outlasted several changes of lead counsel, a presiding judge's own scheduling constraints, and the public's patience in roughly equal measure. Twelve years after the Bafana Bafana and Orlando Pirates goalkeeper was shot dead at a house in Vosloorus, five men remain on trial for his murder, and the proceedings show no sign of reaching a verdict soon.

The case has become a case study in what can go wrong, procedurally, in a high-profile South African prosecution. Postponements have piled up over missing witnesses, defence applications, and disputes over the admissibility of cellphone and forensic evidence, each of which has added weeks or months to a trial that was never going to be simple, given the number of accused, the competing versions of events on the night of the murder, and the public pressure attached to a case involving one of the country's best-known sportsmen.

This week's session continues the pattern, with counsel working through evidentiary matters that, to an outside observer, can feel disconnected from the central question the trial exists to answer: who pulled the trigger, and why. For the Meyiwa family, who have sat through the bulk of the proceedings since they began, the grinding pace has become its own source of grief, a reminder renewed roughly every few weeks that resolution remains out of reach.

Legal observers who have tracked the case note that its length is no longer unusual by the standards of South Africa's most complex multi-accused trials, but that does not make it any less corrosive to public confidence in the justice system's ability to deliver timely outcomes in cases that capture national attention. Each adjournment draws fresh commentary online and in the press about whether the state's case can hold together after so many years, a question the prosecution has consistently insisted it can answer when the evidence is finally weighed in full.

The court has set aside further dates for the weeks ahead, continuing a pattern of sitting in short bursts rather than the sustained daily sessions that might bring the matter to a faster close. For now, the only certainty in the Meyiwa case is that another hearing, dealing with another procedural dispute, is already on the calendar.
