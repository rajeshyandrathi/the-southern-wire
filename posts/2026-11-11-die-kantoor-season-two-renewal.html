Die Kantoor proved a South African Office could work. Now it has to prove it wasn't a fluke

Culture Desk

MultiChoice has confirmed what the show's growing following had been hoping for since its first season wrapped: Die Kantoor, the South African adaptation of The Office, is coming back for a second run. For a format that has already been localised more times across more countries than almost any other television comedy, the confirmation matters less as a surprise than as a vote of confidence — proof that a South African workplace, rendered in the same mockumentary style that made the British original a cult classic and the American version a global phenomenon, found an audience willing to come back for more.

What made the first season a genuine local talking point, rather than simply another franchise import, was how specifically South African its discomfort felt. The Office format lives or dies on the universality of workplace awkwardness — the oblivious manager, the office politics nobody admits to playing, the long stretches of nothing happening punctuated by moments of real cringe — and translating that into a recognisably South African office culture, with its own particular hierarchies and politeness rituals, was always the hardest part of the adaptation to get right.

A second season carries a different kind of pressure than a first. Launching an adaptation of a beloved format invites comparison on novelty alone — audiences arrive curious to see how the South African version handles familiar beats. Season two has no such shelter: it has to generate its own comic momentum, deepen characters audiences have already met, and avoid simply repeating the gags that worked the first time around, all while the comparison to its overseas predecessors never fully goes away.

The show's success also lands at a moment when MultiChoice has leaned hard into locally produced comedy and drama as a way of differentiating its platforms from the flood of international streaming content now available to South African viewers. A homegrown format that can hold its own next to imported hits is exactly the kind of proof point the broadcaster needs as it argues for continued investment in local productions rather than simply licensing shows made elsewhere.

For viewers, the renewal is simpler than any of that corporate calculus: it means more time in a fictional South African office whose particular brand of dysfunction felt, on first viewing, uncomfortably familiar. Whether season two can sharpen that familiarity into something funnier, rather than just more of the same, is the only question that will actually matter once the new episodes land.
