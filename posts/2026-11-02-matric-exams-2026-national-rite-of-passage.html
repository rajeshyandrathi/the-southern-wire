A nation holds its breath as the matric class of 2026 writes its way to the finish line

Lifestyle Desk·Monday, 2 November 2026

Since 13 October, close to a million Grade 12 learners across every corner of South Africa have been living inside the same shared ritual: the National Senior Certificate examinations, the six-week gauntlet that every matriculant before them has had to survive and every matriculant after them will too. The timetable runs all the way through to 26 November, which means the class of 2026 is currently sitting somewhere in the middle of its own personal marathon, with the finish line visible but still a genuine distance away.

There is something almost ceremonial about how consistently this exam season repeats itself every year, and yet how much weight each individual household attaches to it in the moment. Education officials reported a smooth start to this year's sitting, with candidates in all nine provinces working through Computer Applications Technology practicals and language papers on day one without major disruption — the kind of unremarkable, well-organised opening that barely makes headlines but matters enormously to the families whose children are depending on it going right.

What makes matric distinct from almost any other national ritual is its sheer reach across class, geography and circumstance. A learner writing in a well-resourced private school in Sandton and one writing in a rural Eastern Cape school under far more constrained conditions are nonetheless bound by the identical timetable, the identical papers on the identical days, and the identical pressure of a result that will shape university admission, bursary eligibility and job prospects for years afterward. Few other events in South African public life touch so many households so personally and so simultaneously.

For parents and guardians, this stretch of the year brings its own familiar anxieties — the quiet reorganisation of household routines around study schedules, the shared tension of waking a teenager early enough to be sharp for a 9am paper, the low hum of worry that doesn't really lift until the last exam is written. It is a form of collective national stress that rarely gets discussed in economic or political terms, even though its outcomes ripple into both.

With exactly three and a half weeks still to run, the class of 2026 is past the midpoint but nowhere near the end. Results won't land until the new year, but for now the country's focus stays fixed on simply getting every candidate through to the final paper — one more exam, one more day, one more step toward a finish line the whole country is watching for, even if most of it is watching from a comfortable distance.
