Transnet's turnaround only works on paper so far, and the rail numbers show why

Business Desk

Transnet's annual results for the financial year ended March 2026 carried a headline that, taken at face value, looked like genuine good news: a profit of R4.6 billion, the state logistics group's first positive bottom line in years. Look past the headline and the picture is considerably less reassuring. That profit exists only because of a one-off asset sale; strip it out, and the underlying business actually posted a loss of roughly R4.8 billion. The core operations that are supposed to move the country's freight — the ports, the railway lines, the terminals — are still losing money even as the parent company gets to report black ink.

The volume numbers tell the same story from a different angle. Rail freight volumes rose 4.9% to 167 million tonnes over the year, a genuine improvement after a long stretch of decline, but still well short of the 180 million tonne target Transnet had set for itself. Getting closer to a target is not the same as meeting it, and the gap matters because so much of South Africa's export economy — coal, iron ore, agricultural produce, manganese — depends on rail capacity that has been constrained for years by locomotive shortages, cable theft, and maintenance backlogs that the company has struggled to clear.

The reform Transnet is betting on to close that gap involves giving up some of its own monopoly. Rail Access Agreements have now been concluded with eleven private train operators, opening sections of the state-owned network to competing freight services for the first time in the company's modern history. The first of those private services are expected to actually start running in the 2026/27 financial year, which means the reform's real test — whether private operators can move freight more reliably than Transnet has managed on its own, and whether that competition drags Transnet's own performance up rather than simply redistributing a shrinking pie — is still ahead rather than behind.

A parallel move is underway at the ports. Transnet Port Terminals disclosed late last month that it is weighing additional private-sector partnerships specifically aimed at lifting volume growth, an acknowledgment that the state-run terminal operator has not been able to generate that growth alone. Taken together with the rail access agreements, the direction of travel is unmistakable: Transnet is opening up pieces of a logistics network it has run as a near-total monopoly for decades, because running it alone has not worked.

None of this resolves the underlying tension in the latest results. A R4.6 billion profit built on an asset sale buys goodwill and headlines, but it does not fix a rail and ports business still losing money on its core operations, missing its own volume targets, and now dependent on private operators it does not yet have a track record of working alongside. The reforms are real. Whether they arrive fast enough to matter for the exporters who have spent years routing around Transnet's bottlenecks is the question the next set of results will have to answer.
