Hail, flash floods and a warning that kept changing: Gauteng's storm week, explained

Weather Desk

The South African Weather Service's warning map over the past week has read almost like a moving target, and for residents of Gauteng trying to plan around it, that has been the whole problem. A yellow level 4 warning covering Gauteng, Limpopo and Mpumalanga ran through to 23:59 on Thursday, 5 November, flagging severe thunderstorms capable of localised damage. By the following day, the warning for Gauteng specifically had been escalated again, running through midnight on 6 November and explicitly flagging the risk of large hail over populated areas in both Johannesburg and Tshwane.

Ekurhuleni's Disaster and Emergency Management Services treated the escalation seriously enough to place its technical rescue teams on high alert, anticipating that severe thunderstorms could trigger flash flooding and disrupt both electricity supply and communications infrastructure across the metro. That is not a precaution municipalities take lightly; placing specialised rescue units on standby signals genuine concern about water rescues and storm damage, not just inconvenient rain.

Then the picture shifted again. By 7 November, the warning level for Gauteng had been downgraded to level 2, even as Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal remained under level 4 warnings of their own, alongside new level 2 coverage extending into Limpopo, North West and the Free State. The storm system that had threatened Johannesburg and Tshwane with large hail had effectively moved east, leaving Gauteng to clean up whatever damage the preceding days had caused while the more severe risk shifted toward provinces further along the storm's track.

That kind of rapid escalation-then-downgrade pattern is becoming a familiar feature of South African summer storm seasons, where systems can intensify sharply over a 24-to-48-hour window before weakening or shifting track just as quickly. For residents, it means treating any level 4 warning as genuinely serious in the moment it is issued, even knowing the picture may look completely different by the following day.

The broader context is a summer outlook that forecasters have repeatedly flagged as unusually volatile, swinging between severe storm risk in some provinces and heat stress in others within the same week. Gauteng's storm scare has passed for now, but the warning level dropping to 2 is not the same as the threat disappearing entirely, and residents in the province would do well to keep watching the Weather Service's updates rather than assuming the worst has already happened.
