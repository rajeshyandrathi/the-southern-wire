Nasiphi Moya's record just made ActionSA Tshwane's kingmaker — now she has to prove coalition government can actually work

Politics Desk

Outgoing Tshwane mayor Nasiphi Moya cast her own ballot last week and told reporters she hoped the capital's long run of coalition instability was finally coming to an end. Days later, as counting confirmed ActionSA's strongest electoral showing yet in the city, her argument looks less like hope and more like vindication. Moya has credited her own performance in office, rather than any national campaign message, for the result, and the numbers support her: a party that entered this term as a junior coalition partner has emerged from it as one of Tshwane's two indispensable players.

That status cuts both ways. No party looks likely to secure an outright majority in the capital, which means whichever coalition eventually forms will need ActionSA's seats to function. But Tshwane's outgoing administration was itself already fraying before voters went to the polls, strained by disputes including the suspension of the city manager, and the same fragmentation that makes ActionSA powerful also means any new arrangement will be built from the same brittle material as the last one.

The contrast with Johannesburg is instructive. There, ActionSA is using its leverage to block a deal rather than negotiate one, refusing to work with the DA's preferred mayoral candidate. In Tshwane, the party finds itself in the opposite position: not the disruptor forcing concessions, but the incumbent trying to persuade coalition partners that continuity under its leadership is the safer bet after years of the capital cycling through governments that collapsed almost as soon as they formed.

Whether that argument lands will depend less on Moya's record than on whether her prospective partners can agree on anything beyond who gets credit for forming a government. Tshwane's recent history offers little reassurance: coalitions here have broken apart over much smaller disputes than the ones already simmering, and a council this evenly split leaves almost no margin for a single disaffected partner to walk away without bringing the whole arrangement down.

For a capital that has spent years being cited nationally as the cautionary tale of coalition politics gone wrong, the next fortnight carries unusually direct stakes. If Moya's performance really was enough to earn ActionSA the standing to stabilise Tshwane's government, residents may finally get the five years of continuity the last two terms never delivered. If it wasn't, the capital risks becoming, once again, the example everyone else points to when arguing that coalition government simply cannot work.
