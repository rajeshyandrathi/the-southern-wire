Mncube's cellphone records become the new battleground in the Senzo Meyiwa trial

Crime Desk·Tuesday, 3 November 2026

Twelve years after Bafana Bafana and Orlando Pirates goalkeeper Senzo Meyiwa was shot dead during a home invasion in Vosloorus, the murder trial's latest chapter is unfolding in a familiar setting: the Pretoria High Court, where accused number three, Mthobisi Mncube, is back on the stand for a second week of testimony. Monday's resumption picked up where Friday left off, with Mncube firmly denying under cross-examination that he has ever known or associated with co-accused number two, Bongani Ntanzi.

State prosecutor Ronnie Sibanda has built his cross-examination around cellphone records, pressing Mncube on call data and shared contacts that, the state argues, place the two accused in each other's orbit well before the night of the murder. Mncube's defence has centred on casting doubt over whether phone records alone can establish genuine association, a line of argument that has featured, in various forms, throughout a trial defined as much by forensic minutiae as by dramatic testimony.

The case against the seven accused has always rested on a web of circumstantial and technical evidence — cellphone tower triangulation, firearm ballistics, and the shifting and sometimes contradictory accounts of who was present in the house that night. Mncube's insistence that he is not the shooter, despite being named as such by earlier witnesses, has added yet another layer of contested narrative to a prosecution that has already stretched across multiple years and several changes in legal strategy.

For Kelly Khumalo, Meyiwa's partner at the time of his death and a recurring, scrutinised figure throughout the proceedings, each new stretch of testimony renews public attention on allegations — made and contested in open court — about cash payments and her household's role on the night of the killing. None of that has yet produced a definitive answer to the central question the trial was convened to resolve.

As cross-examination continues this week, the court is no closer to a verdict than it has been for long stretches of this marathon case, but the volume of technical evidence being tested suggests the state believes the cellphone data, more than any single witness, could ultimately prove decisive.
