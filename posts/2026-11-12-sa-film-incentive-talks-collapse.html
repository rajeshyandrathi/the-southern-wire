Talks collapse for a third time, and South Africa's film industry runs out of patience

Culture Desk

Negotiations between the Department of Trade, Industry and Competition and representatives of South Africa's film industry have broken down for a third time, according to the Save SA Film and TV Jobs Coalition, which is now asking the Presidency to intervene directly over the future of the Film and Television Production Incentive. For an industry that has spent much of the year describing itself as being brought to its knees, a third collapsed round of talks is less a setback than confirmation that the usual channels have stopped producing results.

The incentive at the centre of the dispute has long been the mechanism that made South Africa competitive against other international production destinations, offering rebates that persuaded foreign studios to shoot on local soil, employ local crews and spend local money on everything from catering to equipment rental. When the incentive's future becomes uncertain, so does every production decision downstream of it, and industry groups have warned for months that international productions are simply choosing other countries rather than waiting out South Africa's policy limbo.

The stakes go well beyond any single film shoot. The coalition's framing, that the industry is being brought to its knees, reflects a workforce of camera operators, set builders, caterers, drivers and hundreds of other freelance crew roles who depend on a steady pipeline of productions to earn a living. Unlike salaried employees at a studio, freelance crew in South African film have no cushion when productions dry up, and months of stalled incentive talks translate directly into months of lost income for people with no other safety net.

Layered on top of the incentive dispute is the industry's uncertainty over Canal Plus's acquisition of MultiChoice, a deal that has left creatives waiting to learn what the new ownership intends for local content commissioning. Two separate sources of instability arriving at once, a government incentive in limbo and a major broadcaster's ownership in transition, have combined to make this one of the most uncertain periods South African film production has faced in years, even as FAME Week Africa's recent gathering of filmmakers and musicians tried to project confidence under the banner of an untold, unstoppable continental creative economy.

Whether a Presidency intervention actually breaks the deadlock remains to be seen. Government departments have shown a pattern of returning to the negotiating table only when political pressure becomes too loud to ignore, and the coalition's public appeal is clearly designed to generate exactly that kind of pressure. For now, the incentive that used to bring international productions and their budgets into the country sits in exactly the kind of bureaucratic suspension that drives those productions somewhere else.
