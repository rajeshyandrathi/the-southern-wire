Cat Matlala's own story about the money is starting to change

Crime Desk

Three days on the witness stand were supposed to cement Vusimuzi "Cat" Matlala's place as the Madlanga Commission's most damaging informant. Instead, his testimony this month has done something almost as newsworthy: it has started contradicting itself. Across his latest appearances, Matlala's account of who asked him for money, how much, and through whom has shifted in ways his own evidence leaders have had to work to reconcile, and observers in the hearing room have noted a man who seems far more careful, and far more fearful, when the name on the table is Shadrack Sibiya than when it is almost anyone else.

The clearest example concerns former police minister Bheki Cele. Matlala previously told Parliament, under oath, that Cele had personally asked him for R1 million. This month, on the commission's stand, he changed that account: the proposal for the money, he now says, came not from Cele directly but from an associate of the former minister. It is the kind of shift a defence lawyer would call a material inconsistency and a prosecutor would call a problem, and it lands squarely on the credibility of a witness the commission has built much of its public narrative around.

The pattern repeats with Sibiya, the suspended deputy national police commissioner, though in the opposite direction. Matlala has previously insisted he never paid Sibiya anything directly, instead claiming that a police sergeant, Fannie Nkosi, collected money from people in Gauteng on Sibiya's behalf — a claim that conveniently keeps Matlala's own hands clean of a direct transaction while still implicating Sibiya's orbit. Observers at this month's hearings have described Matlala as visibly more guarded discussing Sibiya than discussing other senior figures, raising the question of what, specifically, makes that name different.

None of this unravels the broader picture the commission has assembled over its many months of hearings: that senior political and police figures moved in and out of financial relationships with an alleged underworld tycoon who was, at the same time, a central figure in criminal justice processes meant to hold him accountable. But a star witness whose own account keeps moving is a gift to every lawyer representing someone he has implicated, and cross-examination by commission chair retired justice Mbuyiseli Madlanga and his co-commissioners has increasingly turned toward testing exactly those inconsistencies rather than simply cataloguing new allegations.

Matlala's own legal position adds another layer of pressure. His testimony continues to intersect with a collapsed plea deal he once believed had buried an earlier, less convenient statement, and his lawyers have spent commission time trying to have elements of the investigative record struck out altogether. A witness fighting to control what evidence exists about his own past, while simultaneously revising his testimony about other people's conduct in real time, is not a comfortable position for any commission relying on him to anchor findings that will eventually have to survive judicial and public scrutiny.

Why a single witness carries so much weight

Commissions of inquiry in South Africa do not themselves hand down criminal convictions; their findings feed into recommendations that the National Prosecuting Authority, police disciplinary processes and, where warranted, separate criminal trials then have to act on independently. That structural reality is exactly why Matlala's inconsistencies matter disproportionately. A commission can note a contradiction and move on, but a prosecutor building a criminal case against Cele, Sibiya or anyone else implicated by Matlala's testimony has to anticipate that every shifting detail will be put to a judge by defence counsel long before any matter reaches a courtroom. Evidence leaders are acutely aware that the commission's credibility, and the credibility of whatever prosecutions eventually follow from it, rests substantially on a witness whose own story keeps moving.

That dynamic explains why commissioners have spent so much hearing time this month on process rather than on new allegations. Testing exactly where and why Matlala's account shifted, rather than simply cataloguing the shift and proceeding, is the kind of forensic groundwork that protects a commission's eventual report from being dismissed wholesale the moment a single inconsistency surfaces in a later legal challenge.

The Sibiya question specifically

What makes the asymmetry between Matlala's Cele testimony and his Sibiya testimony so striking is the direction each shift runs. Where his account involving Cele moved toward implicating a named intermediary rather than the minister directly, softening the allegation, his account involving Sibiya has moved the opposite way: insisting on an intermediary, Nkosi, specifically to keep his own hands clean of a direct transaction while the allegation against Sibiya's orbit stays intact. A witness softening claims against one figure while hardening the insulation around his own exposure to another is not, on its own, proof of anything improper. But it is exactly the kind of pattern defence lawyers are trained to highlight, and exactly the kind of pattern that has visibly unsettled observers tracking the hearings closely enough to notice it.

For a process already stretched across more than a year of hearings and repeated deadline extensions, the stakes of Matlala's credibility problem reach well beyond his own exposure. If his account of the Cele money changed once under oath, the question hanging over every other allegation he has made is simple: what else might change the next time he is asked.
