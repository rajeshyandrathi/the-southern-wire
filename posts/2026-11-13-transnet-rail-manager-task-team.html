Transnet's rail network is finally being cut loose from the company that has run it for a century

Business Desk

For more than a century, the company that owns South Africa's rail tracks has also been the company that runs trains on them, an arrangement that made sense when Transnet had no competition to worry about and makes considerably less sense now that the government has spent three years trying to open the network to private operators. That contradiction is finally being addressed directly. A technical task team is being established before the end of this year to finalise how the Transnet Rail Infrastructure Manager, the division responsible for the physical network, gets separated from the rest of the Transnet group and corporatised into its own entity.

The urgency behind the move comes from the Presidency's own progress tracking rather than from Transnet itself. Operation Vulindlela's latest quarterly report flagged the corporatisation process as running behind the timeline originally set out, language that in government reform programmes usually precedes exactly this kind of intervention: a dedicated task team, drawing in oversight from the Presidency, National Treasury and the Department of Transport, built specifically to unstick a reform that the implementing agency has struggled to complete on its own.

The Rail Infrastructure Manager itself already exists as a distinct entity on paper, established as a separate division from Transnet Freight Rail back in October 2024 specifically to prepare the ground for private operators to run their own trains on state-owned track. That groundwork has produced real, if incremental, progress — Grindrod ran the network's first private test train earlier this month, a milestone that took years of negotiation and regulatory preparation to reach. But a test train is not the same as a fully competitive, third-party-access rail network, and everyone involved in the reform knows the harder work is what the task team now has to finish: untangling pricing, slot allocation, maintenance obligations and governance structures that currently all sit inside one state-owned company answering to one board.

Why this matters beyond the logistics industry is straightforward. South Africa's rail freight volumes have collapsed over the past decade as mining and agricultural exporters shifted cargo onto roads that were never built to carry it, driving up transport costs, accelerating road damage and squeezing the competitiveness of export industries that depend on reliable, affordable freight. Every month the rail network stays under-reformed is a month those costs keep compounding, which is precisely why Treasury has made faster progress on rail reform one of the conditions attached to the financial guarantees keeping Transnet solvent in the first place.

What the task team cannot promise, however it is eventually staffed, is that corporatisation alone fixes a freight network that has lost both capacity and credibility with the shippers it is trying to win back. Separating the Rail Infrastructure Manager from Transnet is a necessary step toward genuine competition on South Africa's tracks, not a guarantee that competition arrives quickly once the paperwork is done. For now, the clearest signal is that the Presidency has stopped waiting for Transnet to find its own way there.
