Rand and JSE hold their nerve as the municipal vote delivers a hung, fractured verdict

Business Desk

South African markets greeted Thursday's partial election results with the same composure they showed through election day itself. The rand traded in a narrow band against the dollar overnight, and the JSE's all share index opened little changed, even as early counting confirmed what pollsters had been warning for weeks: no party was close to an outright majority in any of the metros investors watch most closely, Johannesburg, Tshwane or Ekurhuleni.

That calm reflects a market that has already priced in a coalition outcome rather than one that expects a clean result and has simply not reacted yet. Traders and fund managers interviewed this week described the municipal vote as a secondary risk event compared with the national coalition arithmetic investors have been living with since 2024, important for service delivery and local governance, but unlikely on its own to move the kind of macro variables, debt issuance, the fiscal framework, that drive currency and bond pricing.

The bigger test for market sentiment, several analysts argued, will not be the vote count itself but what it does to the mood inside the national Government of National Unity over the weeks that follow. A particularly poor showing for the ANC or the DA in cities each party has staked real political capital on could sharpen frictions that have already flared over the budget and over how to respond to the loss of AGOA trade benefits, and it is that kind of knock-on tension, rather than any single municipal result, that would be more likely to unsettle the rand.

Fixed-income desks have been watching the local count mainly for its bearing on provincial and municipal creditworthiness, since several of the councils now facing fresh coalition talks carry significant municipal debt and have histories of service-delivery failures that eventually show up in ratings reviews. A messy, slow-to-form coalition in a major metro is, in that narrower sense, a real cost, even if it is not the kind of cost that shows up immediately in rand or equity pricing.

For now, the market's working assumption is that whatever coalitions eventually emerge in Johannesburg, Tshwane and elsewhere will look recognisably similar to the fractious, multi-party arrangements South African cities have lived under since 2016. That assumption has kept volatility low through a count that, politically, is producing genuine uncertainty. Whether it survives contact with the actual negotiations, due to begin in earnest once the IEC's final numbers are declared, is the question traders say they are watching for next.
