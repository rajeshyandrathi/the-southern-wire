South Africa's film industry runs out of patience with government, appeals straight to the Presidency

Culture Desk

The Save SA Film and TV Jobs Coalition has done something South African industry bodies typically treat as a last resort: it has gone over the head of the department it has spent months negotiating with and asked the Presidency to intervene directly. The coalition says its repeated attempts to engage the Department of Trade, Industry and Competition over the stalled Film and Television Production Incentive have proven futile, a blunt assessment of a relationship that was supposed to be collaborative and has instead become, by the coalition's own account, a dead end.

The incentive at the centre of the dispute is not a minor subsidy line item. It is the mechanism that has, for more than a decade, made South Africa competitive as a filming location against Eastern Europe, Canada and other jurisdictions chasing the same international productions, rebating a portion of qualifying production spend to attract foreign crews, equipment hire and local below-the-line employment. When the incentive's processing stalls or its funding pipeline becomes uncertain, the effect ripples outward quickly, because international productions that choose filming locations years in advance will simply route around a country whose incentive regime looks unreliable.

That is precisely the coalition's warning. South African crews, equipment houses and post-production facilities built over years of steady international work are describing a production pipeline that has visibly thinned, with projects that might once have defaulted to Cape Town or Johannesburg now being scouted and budgeted for other markets instead. Every production that goes elsewhere does not just cost the immediate jobs attached to that shoot; it chips away at the country's reputation among location scouts and international studios as a dependable, incentive-backed filming destination, a reputation that took years to build and could erode far faster than it was earned.

The dispute lands at an awkward moment for an industry that has otherwise had genuine wins to point to this year. International productions built around South African stories and talent, local maskandi and gospel releases finding real streaming traction, and award recognition for homegrown films have all given the sector reasons for cautious optimism about its creative output even as its policy environment has frustrated it. The gap between creative momentum and administrative paralysis is exactly what the coalition is hoping a presidential intervention can close.

Whether the Presidency chooses to step into what has, until now, been treated as a trade and industry department matter will say a great deal about how seriously government regards the film sector's economic contribution versus its cultural one. For an industry whose international competitiveness depends entirely on predictable, promptly administered incentives, every month the stalemate continues is a month closer to losing productions that, once lost to a rival filming destination, rarely come back.
