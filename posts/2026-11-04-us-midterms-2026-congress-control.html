Control of the US Congress hangs in the balance as midterm voters deliver their verdict

World Desk·Wednesday, 4 November 2026

American voters cast their final midterm ballots on Tuesday in an election that will determine not just the balance of power in Washington for the next two years, but how much room the current administration has left to govern without resistance from Capitol Hill. Every seat in the House of Representatives and roughly a third of the Senate were in play, and early reads of the result point toward a familiar midterm pattern reasserting itself: the party out of the White House clawing back ground in the chamber that changes hands most easily.

Analysts going into election night widely expected control of the House to flip to the Democrats, a shift that would hand the party subpoena power, committee chairmanships and the ability to stall the administration's legislative agenda for its remaining two years. The Senate presented a tighter and less predictable picture, with control riding on a handful of competitive races where neither party entered the night with a comfortable advantage.

The vote landed against a tense geopolitical backdrop that bled into the campaign's final hours. Reports of American strikes near the Strait of Hormuz, with explosions reported inside Iran even as Tehran signalled it was prepared to reopen the strait within days if Washington met a set of conditions, gave foreign policy an unusually prominent place in an election cycle that had been expected to turn mostly on domestic economic concerns.

For a White House that has leaned heavily on executive action over the past two years, a divided or opposition-controlled Congress would represent the most significant check yet on an agenda that has otherwise moved with relatively little legislative friction. Trade policy, including decisions like the recent move to strip South Africa of its AGOA benefits, falls more within executive authority than most domestic legislation, but a hostile Congress could still complicate funding, oversight and the broader political capital available to sustain an assertive foreign policy posture.

Full results were still being tabulated as polls closed across different time zones, with the closest House and Senate races likely to take days to call definitively. Whatever the final composition of the incoming Congress, the result will shape how much of the administration's remaining agenda survives intact, and how much of its final two years is spent managing a Capitol Hill no longer willing to simply get out of the way.
