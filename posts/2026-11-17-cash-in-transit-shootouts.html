South Africa's cash-in-transit war just had one of its bloodiest Novembers yet

Crime Desk

Count the cash-in-transit incidents this month and the pattern is less about the robberies than about how they end. On 5 November, five armed men took cash and the service weapons of two security guards during a delivery in Lwandle in the Western Cape, and vanished; none have been caught. The following Friday, a robbery in Virginia in the Free State spilled into a shootout in Gauteng, where police say two suspects were killed and two more escaped on foot. By 9 November, a pursuit along Kempston Road in Gqeberha ended with five suspects shot dead by police after what the Hawks described as the gang opening fire during a high-speed chase.

Three incidents, two provinces, one unmistakable shape: when these jobs go wrong for the robbers, they tend to go wrong fatally, and when they go right, the getaway is usually clean. What almost never happens, on this run of cases, is an arrest followed by a trial. The industry's armed response has effectively become its own parallel justice system, dispensing outcomes well before any court gets a chance to weigh in — a dynamic that satisfies nobody's definition of accountability even when the dead are, by all available evidence, genuinely the robbers.

That lethality sits alongside an older, less visible problem the industry has never fully solved: insider involvement. KwaZulu-Natal's provincial police leadership has previously warned that company staff are sometimes complicit in the attacks on their own vehicles, a claim that reframes cash-in-transit crime as partly an inside job dressed up as an external siege. If that pattern holds nationally, the shootouts making headlines this month may be only the externally visible half of a supply chain that starts inside the security firms themselves.

The industry insists it is adapting. Plans floated at the start of 2026 to reduce the frequency of attacks have leaned on better route randomisation, armoured upgrades and tighter vetting, the standard toolkit for an industry that has been fighting this exact war for over two decades. Whether any of it is working is hard to say from the outside, because the number that would prove it — attacks attempted versus attacks succeeding — is not one the industry publishes in a form the public can check against police figures.

What the public does get is the aftermath: armoured vehicles blown open with explosives on highways, viral video of cash scattered across tarmac, and, increasingly, body counts from the police response rather than convictions from a courtroom. Durban's N3 has seen an armoured vehicle burn after an explosive breach; Braamfischerville and Verulam have each had their own version of the same spectacle in recent years. The genre of crime barely changes from incident to incident. Only the location does.

What nobody in this debate has convincingly answered is whether South Africa's increasingly lethal police response to cash-in-transit crime is actually suppressing the robbery rate or simply raising its price in blood. Five dead in Gqeberha and two dead near Virginia inside a single week reads, depending on where you stand, as either a crackdown working exactly as intended or a warning sign that the industry's arms race has no ceiling left to reach.
