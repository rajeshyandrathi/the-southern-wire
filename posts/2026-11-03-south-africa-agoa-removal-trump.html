Washington moves to cut South Africa from AGOA, and citrus growers are the first to flinch

Business Desk·Tuesday, 3 November 2026

The United States has notified South Africa and Rwanda that it intends to terminate their eligibility for the African Growth and Opportunity Act, the duty-free trade arrangement that has underpinned a chunk of South African exports to America for more than 25 years. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer informed Congress and both governments of the move, with the change set to take effect from 1 January 2027 — and the reaction from export-dependent industries was immediate.

The Citrus Growers' Association was among the first to sound the alarm, warning that growers in the Western and Northern Cape stand to lose a market that has absorbed a meaningful share of South Africa's citrus exports for years, and that job losses in those export-dependent regions are now a real risk. South Africa has been the single largest AGOA beneficiary on the continent, accounting for more than half of all AGOA-related exports to the US over the programme's quarter-century run, with last year's AGOA-linked trade estimated at close to $1.7 billion. Beyond citrus, sectors including automotive components and edible ice have been flagged as exposed.

The US side framed the decision around trade imbalances, investment barriers, and what it described as national security and foreign policy concerns, rather than any single flashpoint. Trade Minister Parks Tau said Pretoria has roughly 60 days to engage Washington and address the concerns raised before any suspension actually bites, framing the window as a last chance to negotiate rather than a done deal. US Ambassador Brent Bozell, for his part, called the outcome regrettable and laid the blame on South African policy choices rather than on the US decision itself.

The move lands at an awkward moment for the relationship between the two countries, which has been strained for much of the year over a string of disagreements spanning trade, land policy and diplomatic rhetoric. It also arrives squarely in the middle of South Africa's own election week, giving opposition parties fresh ammunition to question the government's handling of relations with its largest single-country trading partner outside the continent.

Not every voice in the debate is sounding the alarm bell at full volume. Trade economist Eckart Naumann described the removal as regrettable but said it "does not spell doom" for South Africa, noting that AGOA access has already been narrowing in practical importance as exporters diversify into other markets. Whether that diversification has gone far enough to cushion the blow for citrus farmers and component manufacturers on the ground is likely to become clearer only once the 60-day negotiating window starts running out.
