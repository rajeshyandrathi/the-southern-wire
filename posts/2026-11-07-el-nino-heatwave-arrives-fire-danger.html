The El Niño summer SAWS warned about has arrived early, and it's already setting off fire alarms

Weather Desk

Forecasters spent much of October cautioning that a strong El Niño pattern was building for South Africa's summer; the past fortnight has made clear that the pattern did not wait politely for summer's official start. The South African Weather Service has already issued heatwave warnings for northern KwaZulu-Natal and flagged severe fire danger conditions in parts of the Northern Cape, while Johannesburg residents were urged to limit sun exposure over a scorching weekend, a sequence of alerts that reads less like an early warning and more like the thing itself arriving.

The seasonal outlook behind these warnings has not changed, only sharpened. SAWS's forecast for November 2026 through January 2027 points to below-normal rainfall and above-normal temperatures across most of the country as the El Niño event strengthens in the Pacific, a reversal from the wetter La Niña conditions that brought welcome relief to the interior a year earlier. For farmers who had banked on another season of decent rainfall after last year's reprieve, the shift is landing at precisely the point in the planting calendar where it will do the most damage.

Fire risk has emerged as the most immediate practical consequence. Authorities have flagged extremely high fire danger conditions across four provinces, including KwaZulu-Natal and the Northern Cape, a combination of dried-out vegetation, low humidity and the kind of wind patterns that can turn a small grass fire into an uncontrollable blaze within hours. Emergency services in Johannesburg and Mpumalanga have both issued public guidance this spring urging residents to avoid open flames and report smoke sightings early, standard advice that carries more weight in a season forecasters have specifically flagged as unusually dangerous.

The human cost of sustained heat is also drawing attention from emergency medical services, which have repeatedly urged residents in Gauteng to check on elderly neighbours, keep hydrated and avoid unnecessary outdoor exertion during the hottest parts of the day. Heat-related illness tends to be undercounted in South African health statistics relative to more visible disasters like floods or fires, but emergency responders say the strain it places on hospitals and ambulance services during sustained hot spells is real and climbing in step with each new heatwave warning.

What happens over the rest of November will be an early test of how accurately this El Niño event's strength has been forecast. If the pattern holds as SAWS expects, the heatwaves and fire warnings already punctuating the past fortnight will become the season's default setting rather than its exception, a prospect that has water utilities, disaster management officials and farmers all watching the same set of models with considerably more anxiety than South Africans typically bring to the start of summer.
