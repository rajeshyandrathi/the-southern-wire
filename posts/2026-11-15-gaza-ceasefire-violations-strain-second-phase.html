Gaza's ceasefire has held for five weeks and been broken hundreds of times. Both claims are true.

World Desk

The ceasefire that took effect in Gaza on 10 October has now outlasted every prediction that it would collapse within days, and it has also been violated, by Gaza authorities' own count, more than 738 times since it began. Both of those facts are true at once, and reconciling them is the actual state of the truce five weeks in: a framework that has stopped the war from resuming at full scale while doing very little to stop it from continuing in smaller, grinding increments that keep adding to a death toll the ceasefire was supposed to end.

Gaza's Health Ministry has counted at least 377 people killed in Israeli strikes since the truce began, a figure that sits awkwardly next to any description of the arrangement as a success. Mediators have continued pushing for progress on implementation even as the bombing continues, which is less a contradiction than a description of how this specific ceasefire has always worked: a framework agreement that reduced the intensity of the war without actually ending the targeted strikes that both sides have continued to justify as responses to specific threats or violations by the other.

The diplomatic architecture around the ceasefire remains more solid than the ceasefire itself. The United Nations Security Council has formally endorsed the US-backed peace plan, giving the arrangement a legal foundation that previous Gaza truces often lacked, and that endorsement is meant to pave the way for an international stabilisation force whose first deployment was expected to begin in early 2026. Whether that force actually materialises on schedule, and whether its presence would meaningfully reduce the strike pattern rather than simply monitor it, remains the open question hanging over the plan's entire second phase.

Hamas has used the violation count to press for more international pressure on Israel, arguing that a ceasefire enforced this loosely is not really a ceasefire at all. Israel, for its part, has continued to describe individual strikes as responses to specific threats rather than breaches of the broader agreement, a framing that lets both sides technically maintain that the truce is intact while the casualty figures tell a less tidy story. That gap between the diplomatic framing and the reality on the ground is precisely what phase two of the peace plan is supposed to close, assuming it ever gets fully implemented.

Five weeks is long enough to prove the ceasefire is not nothing: the full-scale war it replaced has not resumed, and that alone is a result worth weighing against the alternative. It is also long enough to prove that "ceasefire" is doing a great deal of definitional work in this specific case, describing an arrangement that has reduced violence without eliminating it, and left both sides free to keep counting the ways the other has broken a promise that, on paper, is still holding.
