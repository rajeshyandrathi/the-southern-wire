Nine dead in Blue Nile, fourteen power stations down: Sudan's cabinet declares maximum alert

Politics Desk

Nine civilians were killed and several more wounded when an RSF drone struck Kurmuk county in Sudan's Blue Nile state, officials said, the latest and bloodiest entry in a drone campaign that has now hit fourteen power stations across the country in a little over two weeks. The Kurmuk strike landed on a civilian area rather than a military target, and it has pushed Sudan's cabinet, under Prime Minister Kamil Idris, to declare a maximum state of alert nationwide.

The alert is not just rhetorical. The cabinet has threatened severe punishment for anyone found supplying the Rapid Support Forces with coordinates for its drone strikes on infrastructure — language that points to a government convinced it has a spy problem as much as a drone problem, and that is now treating the leaking of targeting information as an act approaching treason. Earlier strikes this month hit power stations at Abu Hamad and Al Dabbah in Sudan's north, part of a pattern that has left blackouts rippling across towns with no military role in the war at all.

The diplomatic fallout has escalated alongside the military one. Sudan's Foreign Ministry has formally accused Ethiopia of hosting the launch sites for RSF drones, a serious claim against a neighbouring state that Khartoum has not made lightly. Separately, army Chief of Staff Yasir al-Atta has accused the United Arab Emirates and its president, Mohammed bin Zayed, of arming and backing the RSF — an allegation the UAE has repeatedly and categorically denied, but one Sudanese officials keep repeating publicly, suggesting Khartoum sees little diplomatic cost left in saying it outright.

On the ground, the army says it continues to make gains against the RSF in West Kordofan and Darfur, including the capture of Iyal Bakhit and the downing of multiple RSF drones over the past week. The RSF disputes the picture, claiming its own advances including the capture of the Sali military base in Blue Nile, and conflict trackers have reported that RSF-allied militias killed more than 800 people in a multi-day rampage in Ardamata, in western Darfur, in early November — a figure that, if confirmed, would mark one of the deadliest single episodes of the war's recent phase.

What makes this moment different from the war's earlier years is the geography of the violence: drones can reach power stations and civilian towns far behind any front line, which means a war that was once fought over cities and garrisons is now being fought over the national grid itself. For a government already accusing two of its neighbours of enabling that campaign, the maximum alert is as much a statement of how exposed Sudan now feels as it is an operational order.
