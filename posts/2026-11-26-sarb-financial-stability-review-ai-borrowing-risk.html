Reserve Bank says banks are sound, but warns the AI boom is competing with SA for borrowed money

The SARB’s latest Financial Stability Review finds the system resilient. But it flags a possible sharp fall in AI share prices, the cost of government borrowing and stretched household finances as the risks to watch.

Business Desk·Thursday, 26 November 2026·3 min read

Key points

  • The SARB says South Africa’s banks and financial institutions are well capitalised and liquid
  • Disruption to critical digital infrastructure is rated the biggest risk to the system
  • Local unit trusts held nearly 13% of their assets in AI-related companies by the third quarter
  • Governor Lesetja Kganyago warned that AI firms are competing with governments for long-term funding
  • Buy-now, pay-later loans must be reported to credit bureaus from 1 February 2027

South Africa’s financial system is in good health, but the global rush to build artificial intelligence could make life harder for the country, the South African Reserve Bank (SARB) said on Wednesday, 25 November. Its twice-yearly Financial Stability Review found that local banks and other financial institutions are well capitalised and have enough cash, and it expects the system to keep working smoothly over the next year unless there is a major shock, Reuters reported.

Governor Lesetja Kganyago, speaking in Pretoria, focused on a less familiar worry: the huge amounts of debt being raised by the technology companies building AI data centres. “Firms, especially in the AI sector, are increasingly vying with governments for long-term capital,” he said, according to Bloomberg, as reported by the Daily Star. “This competition for funds is intensifying the pressure on long-term borrowing expenses.”

That matters for a government that borrows heavily. The Daily Star report noted that the National Treasury expects debt repayments of close to, or more than, R400bn a year from the end of this decade until 2032. If global investors demand higher returns because AI companies are soaking up capital, South Africa could pay more to refinance that debt.

Big bets on big tech

Local investors are also directly exposed. By the third quarter, South African unit trusts held nearly 13% of their portfolio assets in AI-related companies, and more than two-thirds of that was in just five firms: Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet. Naspers and Prosus, whose value rests largely on Tencent, together make up about 8% of the JSE.

Vafa Anvari, who heads macro-financial vulnerabilities at the SARB, said doubts about whether AI will pay off could trigger a correction in large technology shares and cause losses in local portfolios. A correction in AI share prices is a new risk in this edition of the review, Reuters reported. The top-rated risk remains disruption to critical digital infrastructure, which the bank says has become more of a vulnerability since June.

The review also points to the effects of the Iran war on oil prices, capital flows and household budgets. It expects the economy to grow by 1.2% this year, about the same as last year.

Households and the banks

Household finances are a growing concern. The household savings ratio has been negative since 2022 and fell to its lowest level in more than a decade in the third quarter, the Daily Star reported, and families are leaning on savings and short-term unsecured loans as living costs rise again. The SARB said high levels of bad revolving-credit loans mean a shock to incomes, inflation or jobs could quickly turn into missed payments.

One change is coming: from 1 February 2027, the National Credit Regulator will require buy-now, pay-later products to be reported to credit bureaus, so those debts will show up on people’s credit records. That is relevant this week, with Black Friday on 27 November.

On the banks themselves, Kganyago said stress tests show they would still meet their regulatory capital requirements even in a severe downturn, although their buffers would be thinner afterwards. Full stress test results are due in early 2027. The review comes a week after the SARB kept the repo rate at 7.25% in a split vote.
