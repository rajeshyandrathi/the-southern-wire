Fifteen drug labs in a year: police say they are winning the war on drugs

National commissioner Fannie Masemola says police have dismantled about 15 major drug laboratories in the past 12 months, worth more than R2.4 billion combined, the latest a R100 million operation in Kranskop.

Crime Desk·Friday, 20 November 2026·2 min read

Key points

  • About 15 major drug labs dismantled in 12 months
  • Combined value of more than R2.4 billion
  • More than 60 arrests, including Mexican nationals
  • Latest lab in Kranskop, KZN, worth about R100 million

South Africa’s police chief has a new headline number for the fight against drug syndicates. Speaking at Friday’s crime statistics briefing, national commissioner Fannie Masemola said police have dismantled about 15 major drug laboratories over the past 12 months, with a combined estimated value of more than R2.4 billion.

The most recent bust came in the past few days in Kranskop, KwaZulu-Natal, where police shut down a lab with drugs, chemicals and equipment valued at around R100 million. A 31-year-old suspect was arrested there. Across the 15 labs, Masemola said, more than 60 people have been arrested, including foreign nationals from Mexico.

The labs have turned up in Gauteng, KwaZulu-Natal, North West and Limpopo, often on farms or in rented properties away from town centres. Their scale points to organised, well-funded networks rather than small backyard operations.

Masemola framed the results as proof that intelligence-led policing is working. Critics will note that the steady stream of discoveries also shows how deeply manufacturing has taken root in the country, and that the drug trade feeds the gang violence that drives the Western Cape’s murder figures.

The commissioner also put cash-in-transit gangs on notice. Robberies of that kind rose sharply in the latest quarter, and police say 55 of their most wanted suspects linked to these attacks have been arrested since 1 July, while six were shot dead in confrontations with officers. With the festive season operations now under way, Masemola said, the pressure on both groups will only increase.

Why labs matter

For years, much of South Africa’s drug problem was framed as one of imports, with cocaine and heroin arriving through ports and airports. The repeated discovery of large labs inside the country shows a shift towards local manufacturing, which is harder to stop at the border and can supply both local streets and neighbouring countries.

Labs also bring their own dangers. They use large quantities of toxic and flammable chemicals, and police and specialist teams often need days to make a site safe after a raid.

The bigger picture

The drug announcement came at the same briefing as the latest crime statistics, which showed a 3.5% fall in murders but rising cash-in-transit robberies and kidnappings. Police leaders want to show that they are hitting the money and networks behind violent crime, not only individual offenders.

Whether the busts translate into fewer drugs on the street is harder to measure. Communities in gang-affected areas of the Western Cape, where drug markets drive much of the violence, will judge the campaign by what changes in their neighbourhoods.
