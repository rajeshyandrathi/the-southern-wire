Twelve years after Senzo Meyiwa's murder, his trial grinds through another week in Pretoria

Crime Desk·Wednesday, 4 November 2026

The North Gauteng High Court has settled into a rhythm that would have seemed unthinkable when Senzo Meyiwa was shot dead in a Vosloorus home in October 2014: five accused men, a rotating cast of witnesses, and a case that has now outlived the football career the murdered Bafana Bafana captain never got to finish. This week's sitting in Pretoria continues that long march, with the state working through the forensic and testimonial threads it hopes will finally tie the shooting to the men in the dock.

What has made this trial a national fixture, far beyond its outcome, is how often it has stalled. Postponements over witness health, disputed evidence, legal technicalities and a cast of defence teams determined to test every procedural seam have repeatedly pushed the case back, sometimes by days and sometimes by months. For a public that watched the murder unfold as breaking news in 2014, the trial's slow crawl has become its own kind of story: a test of whether South Africa's justice system can deliver answers on a timeline that still means something to the people waiting for them.

Kelly Khumalo, the singer and Meyiwa's former partner who was present the night he died, remains the gravitational center the case keeps returning to, even when she is not herself testifying on a given day. Her account, and the inconsistencies other witnesses have alleged in it over the years, continue to shape the defence's strategy as much as the state's. The five accused — among them Bongani Ntanzi, Mthobisi Mncube, Muzikayise Sibiya, Fisokuhle Ntuli and Sifisokuhle Shiwa — have each pursued their own legal angles, occasionally turning the courtroom into as much a contest between co-accused as between the accused and the state.

Legal observers following the case closely note that the sheer duration of the trial has started to work against everyone involved. Witnesses' memories fade, evidence ages, and public patience, however morbidly fascinated, is not infinite. Yet neither the prosecution nor the defence teams show any appetite for shortcuts; both sides appear committed to litigating every disputed fact, aware that an appeal built on a rushed process would only extend the case further.

For now, the trial continues its incremental progress, one session at a time, with no firm date yet for when the state expects to close its case. Meyiwa's family, who have spent over a decade watching from the gallery and occasionally outside it, have said they believe justice has not yet been served. Twelve years on, that sentence remains the only thing in this case that nobody disputes.
