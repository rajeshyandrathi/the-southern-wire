The fuel shock was worse than expected. Inflation just hit 5.2%.

Business Desk

Stats SA put a number on the fuel shock this morning, and it is bigger than almost anyone expected. Annual consumer inflation jumped to 5.2% in October, up from 4.5% in September, the highest reading in nearly two and a half years and well clear of the 5.1% most economists had pencilled in. The jump confirms what filling up a car in October already made obvious: the petrol price spike that hit at the start of the month has worked its way through the entire economy faster than the Reserve Bank's own forecasts assumed.

Fuel is doing almost all of the damage. Annual fuel inflation accelerated to 42.9% in October from 27.9% in September, a jump that on its own explains most of why the headline number moved as much as it did. Transport costs as a category contributed 2.1 percentage points to the overall 5.2% reading, by far the single biggest driver, with every category that depends on moving goods or people — from food logistics to taxi fares — absorbing some share of that cost. For a country where fuel is taxed and regulated rather than left to the market, a move of this size usually means the price-setting formula caught an oil or rand shock that nobody budgeted for, and October did exactly that.

What makes the number harder to dismiss as a one-off spike is what it shows once fuel is stripped out. Core inflation — the measure that excludes food, non-alcoholic beverages, fuel and energy, and which the Reserve Bank watches as its best read on underlying price pressure — still came in at 4.2%, comfortably above the midpoint of the 3-6% target band's lower half and a sign that price pressure has not stayed contained to the pump. The trimmed mean, a separate measure that strips out the most extreme movers in the basket, was lower at 3.4%, which gives the Bank at least one reading it can point to as less alarming than the headline figure.

The timing could not be sharper. The release lands a single day before the Reserve Bank's Monetary Policy Committee delivers its last rate decision of 2026, a meeting that was already being described as too close to call before this morning's data arrived. Governor Lesetja Kganyago's committee has held the repo rate at 7.25% since September's hike, and economists had been split three ways heading into this release: a hold to wait out what some hoped was a temporary fuel spike, a cut on the view that September's projections already implied one, or, for a smaller camp, the case for another hike if inflation expectations started drifting. A headline print of 5.2%, with transport doing almost all the work and core inflation still elevated, strengthens the hand of anyone arguing the committee cannot afford to loosen policy tomorrow, whatever relief that would offer borrowers.

Reading the three measures together

Why the Reserve Bank publishes three separate inflation gauges, rather than relying on the headline figure alone, becomes obvious on a day like this one. Headline CPI at 5.2% captures the full basket, fuel spike included, and is the number that dominates headlines and household conversation because it reflects what people actually pay at the till and the pump. Core inflation at 4.2%, stripping out food and energy, is the measure the Bank treats as closer to the underlying trend, since food and fuel prices are volatile and often driven by factors monetary policy cannot influence. The trimmed mean at 3.4%, which goes a step further and removes the single most extreme movers in the basket each month, is the most conservative read, designed specifically to filter out exactly the kind of one-off shock October's fuel price represents. That the three measures diverge this sharply, by nearly two full percentage points between the highest and lowest reading, is itself a signal: it tells the MPC that most of this month's inflation story really is concentrated in one place, even if the headline number alone would suggest a much broader problem.

That divergence does not resolve the committee's dilemma so much as sharpen it. A central bank that leans too heavily on the trimmed mean risks looking out of touch with what households are actually experiencing at the pump and in the shops; one that reacts purely to the headline figure risks over-tightening policy in response to a shock interest rates cannot actually fix. Kganyago's committee will have all three numbers in front of them on Thursday, and how they weigh one against the others will say as much about the Bank's own priorities as any public statement that follows the decision.

What an eight-month run above target actually signals

A single month of elevated inflation is noise; eight consecutive months above the Reserve Bank's 3% target line is closer to a pattern the Bank has to account for in how it talks about its own credibility, regardless of what drove any individual month's number. South Africa's inflation-targeting framework gives the Bank a formal band, not a single fixed number, but persistent prints toward the upper half of that range for the better part of a year put pressure on the institution's broader claim that it keeps price growth anchored near its stated midpoint. That reputational dimension, separate from the economics of any single month's fuel shock, is part of why a print this far above forecast lands as more than just one surprising data release.

Inflation has now sat above the Reserve Bank's 3% target for eight straight months, and October's number means the run is getting worse, not better, just as the year's final rate call arrives. Thursday's decision will say a great deal about how the Bank reads this release: as a temporary pass-through from a single bad month at the pumps, or as evidence that price pressure is broader than the fuel line alone suggests.
