Cat Matlala's two versions of the same bribery story are starting to collide in public

Crime Desk

Vusimuzi "Cat" Matlala has now told his story about police corruption twice in two different rooms, under two different kinds of oath, and the Madlanga Commission has spent its past two sessions methodically holding those two versions up against each other. What the commission found this week were not minor slips of memory. The gaps between what Matlala told Parliament's ad hoc committee investigating the police and what he has told the commission itself are specific enough, and consistent enough in their direction, that they have become the week's central line of questioning rather than a footnote to it.

At the centre of both accounts sits a R360-million tender, the deal through which Matlala has alleged senior police officials received payments in exchange for protection and favourable treatment. The commission's interest is not simply whether the bribery happened, but whether Matlala's account of who was involved and how the money moved has shifted depending on which body he was addressing and what he stood to gain or lose from each version. A witness whose testimony moves the way his has moved invites exactly the kind of cross-examination the commission delivered this week.

The timing matters as much as the content. Matlala returned to the stand on Wednesday after his testimony had already been interrupted and rescheduled multiple times since the commission first sought his evidence in July, each delay feeding speculation about what pressure, legal or otherwise, was shaping what he was prepared to say and when. Commissioners have shown visible impatience with a witness whose cooperation has never looked unconditional.

Running in parallel to Matlala's own testimony is the commission's pursuit of Suliman Carrim, the businessman it has moved to charge directly after he failed to appear to give evidence. That decision, to initiate contempt proceedings itself rather than simply note the absence, signals a commission under Judge Mbuyiseli Madlanga that is no longer treating non-cooperation from implicated figures as an acceptable cost of doing its work.

What hangs over all of it is the credibility of the broader claim the commission was established to test: that organised crime figures bought protection inside the senior ranks of the South African Police Service. Every inconsistency in Matlala's account is being read, fairly or not, as evidence either that he is finally telling the truth under the pressure of cross-referencing, or that his testimony was never built to survive it. Which of those readings the commission settles on will shape how much weight its eventual findings carry with a public that has watched state capture-style inquiries produce damning reports with limited consequences before.
