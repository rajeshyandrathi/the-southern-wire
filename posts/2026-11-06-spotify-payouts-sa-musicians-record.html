South African musicians banked more than half a billion rand from Spotify last year, and the number keeps climbing

Culture Desk

South African artists earned more than R504 million from Spotify in 2025, a 28 percent increase on the previous year's payout, according to figures the streaming platform has shared with the local music industry. For a sector that spent much of the previous decade treating streaming as an existential threat to recorded-music income, the scale and trajectory of that number tells a very different story: streaming has become, by a wide margin, South African music's single largest source of recorded revenue, and it is still growing faster than almost any other part of the industry.

The shift did not happen overnight, and it did not happen by accident. Spotify's local push over the past several years has leaned heavily on amplifying genres with strong domestic followings but historically thin international distribution, amapiano chief among them, through curated playlists and targeted marketing that treated South African listening habits as worth building for directly rather than serving as an afterthought to European or American catalogues. Local labels and independent artists who adapted early to streaming-native release strategies, frequent singles, collaboration-heavy tracklists, consistent playlist pitching, appear to have captured a disproportionate share of the growth.

The broader context makes the number more striking still. South African consumers have historically been price-sensitive subscribers to paid streaming relative to markets in Europe and North America, with data costs and smartphone penetration shaping listening habits as much as musical taste. That a market with those structural headwinds has still produced a 28 percent year-on-year jump in artist payouts suggests either genuinely rapid growth in the local subscriber and advertising-supported listener base, or a meaningful rise in how much South African music is being streamed by listeners outside the country, most likely some combination of both.

For individual musicians, the headline number obscures a familiar and persistent problem: aggregate payouts rising sharply does not mean the money is reaching artists evenly. Streaming economics still reward the handful of acts who can generate consistent, high-volume plays across a catalogue, while mid-tier and emerging artists often see royalty cheques that remain difficult to live on despite genuinely respectable stream counts. Industry bodies have continued to push for greater transparency in how streaming revenue is split between labels, distributors and performers themselves, a conversation that a rising national payout figure does little to resolve on its own.

Still, for an industry that has spent years anxiously watching its recorded-revenue base shrink, a streaming payout climbing by more than a quarter in a single year is the kind of trend line worth sitting with. It suggests South African music's commercial centre of gravity has genuinely moved, from physical sales and radio play toward a platform-driven, algorithmically mediated listening culture, and that the shift, however uneven its benefits, is not slowing down.
