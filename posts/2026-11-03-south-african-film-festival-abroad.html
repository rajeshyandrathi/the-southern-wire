A Maskandi documentary and a filmed opera send South African storytelling abroad this November

Culture Desk·Tuesday, 3 November 2026

The South African Film Festival's North American leg opens this month in Canada and the United States, streaming online from 6 to 20 November with in-person openings in Toronto and Vancouver, and this year's programme leans unusually heavily on music as both subject and texture. Squashbox, a documentary following a revival of traditional Maskandi music, anchors a slate that has already collected five awards on the festival circuit, including at Sydney's SmartFone Flick Fest, well before its North American premiere.

Maskandi, the guitar-and-vocal tradition rooted in Zulu migrant culture, has spent decades occupying a curious position in South African music: deeply beloved within its core audience, yet largely absent from the export narratives that have carried amapiano and gqom to international dance floors. A documentary built around the genre's revival offers festival audiences abroad a very different entry point into South African sound than the one most streaming platforms have trained them to expect.

Elsewhere in the programme, a filmed staging of Aida performed with the Cape Town Philharmonic Orchestra extends the festival's music focus into a different register entirely, pairing South African orchestral performance with one of opera's most enduring works. Pairing an intimate documentary on rural musical tradition with a grand orchestral production in the same festival slate is itself a statement about the range the organisers are trying to claim for South African screen culture.

This marks the festival's sixteenth year running in Canada and its seventh in the United States, a longevity that has given it room to move beyond the festival circuit's default diet of social-issue dramas and crime narratives that have historically dominated how South African film gets marketed overseas. Films built around music, memory and craft are a deliberate broadening of that pitch.

For South African filmmakers and musicians whose work rarely travels much beyond local release, a platform like this — modest in scale compared to the major European festivals, but consistent and specifically curated for North American audiences — remains one of the more dependable routes to international visibility available.
