Petrol fell by a single cent. Households felt none of it.

Business Desk

South Africa's November fuel price adjustment landed with almost no drama: petrol 93 and 95 both dropped by about one cent a litre, a change so small it barely registers at the pump, while diesel absorbed a much heavier cut thanks to a temporary government levy intervention rather than any shift in the underlying cost of crude. Inland motorists are now paying roughly R30.24 a litre for 95 unleaded, with coastal buyers paying slightly less at R29.37, figures that have left households wondering what, exactly, the relief they were promised actually amounts to.

The answer lies in a R10 billion temporary fuel-levy package that cut the general fuel levy on diesel by R3 a litre and on petrol by R1.50 a litre, running from the start of November through to the start of December. On paper, that is a meaningful intervention. In practice, a rising wholesale under-recovery largely cancelled out the petrol levy cut before it ever reached consumers, while diesel buyers, mostly commercial operators and transport fleets rather than ordinary commuters, captured most of the real relief, with wholesale prices falling by close to a rand a litre in some calculations.

Debt counselling organisations have been blunt about what this means for household budgets. A one-cent decrease offers essentially no financial relief to families already stretched by a cost of living that has climbed steadily across food, transport and electricity over the past year. For a commuter filling a modest sedan once a week, the saving amounts to a handful of rand, nowhere near enough to offset a shopping basket that keeps getting more expensive regardless of what happens at the pump.

What makes the short-term relief feel even more fragile is the warning attached to it. Early Central Energy Fund tracking data already points to rising under-recoveries building up behind the scenes, and forecasters are flagging a possible increase of between 53 and 84 cents a litre for petrol in December once the temporary levy cut expires and the slate adjustment catches up. If that materialises, November's one-cent dip will look less like relief and more like a brief pause before a sharper correction.

For a government trying to demonstrate it is responsive to cost-of-living pressure ahead of the festive season, the optics of a R10 billion levy package producing a one-cent petrol saving are awkward at best. The diesel relief will genuinely help logistics-heavy sectors manage input costs, and that matters for an economy still trying to keep freight and food distribution affordable. But for the household filling a tank each week, November's adjustment confirms what Debt Rescue and similar organisations have been saying for months: the relief on offer rarely survives contact with the rest of the pricing chain.
