Government cuts the fuel levy by R1.50 a litre — then a quieter tax hike eats almost all of it

Business Desk

South Africa's November fuel price adjustment landed with a headline that promised relief and fine print that mostly took it back. Mineral Resources and Energy Minister Gwede Mantashe announced that petrol 93 and 95 would each fall by a single cent a litre from 4 November, a figure that on its own would suggest a genuinely quiet month at the pumps. Diesel buyers fared noticeably better, with more meaningful reductions flowing through to the retail price.

The real story sits beneath that headline number. Treasury's R10 billion relief package cut the General Fuel Levy by R1.50 a litre on petrol and R3.00 on diesel, a reduction running until 1 December that was explicitly designed to cushion motorists against an underlying price increase of roughly that same magnitude. But the slate levy, the lesser-known mechanism that reconciles over- and under-recoveries in the fuel pricing formula, rose sharply at the same time, climbing from 87.66 cents to 135.84 cents a litre, adding back close to 48 cents that the headline levy cut was meant to deliver.

Fuel retailers, who had lobbied for the levy relief in the first place, have been blunt in their assessment that the package will offer little real ease for consumers once the slate levy adjustment is accounted for. The net effect is a petrol price that moved by barely a cent even as global oil prices and the exchange rate continued to exert the kind of upward pressure that, without the levy cut, would have pushed pump prices up by closer to R1.50 a litre, exactly the outcome Treasury's intervention was designed to prevent.

For a government keen to be seen responding to cost-of-living pressure ahead of further inflation readings, the optics of a levy cut are more politically useful than the economics turned out to be for ordinary motorists. Transport costs have already been identified as a key driver of inflation running hotter than the headline rate would suggest, and a fuel price that barely moves despite a nominal tax cut does little to change that trajectory heading into the final months of the year.

The arrangement is also only temporary. With the levy relief set to expire on 1 December, attention will now turn to whether Treasury extends the cut, lets it lapse entirely, or finds some other mechanism to manage the slate levy's bite, at a moment when every one of those choices carries consequences for household budgets already stretched by a year of rising transport and living costs.
