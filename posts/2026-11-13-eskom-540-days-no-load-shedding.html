Five hundred and forty days without load shedding, and Eskom is finally arguing about what comes next

Business Desk

There was a time, not so long ago, when a South African business plan had to include a line item for diesel generators and a contingency for lost production hours, because load shedding was simply a cost of doing business in the country. That assumption is now harder to defend than it has been in over a decade. Eskom has gone more than 540 days without implementing load shedding, the utility's longest uninterrupted stretch since the crisis first took hold, and its public messaging has shifted accordingly — from explaining why the lights kept going off to explaining how it intends to keep them on.

The numbers behind that shift are genuinely substantial rather than merely rhetorical. Eskom's energy availability factor, the single measure that best captures how much of its generation fleet is actually working on any given day, has climbed to roughly 68%, a level that would have seemed implausible during the worst years of stage six blackouts. Unplanned outages are down by well over two gigawatts year on year, diesel expenditure on the utility's expensive open-cycle gas turbines continues to decline, and Koeberg's second unit has secured a licence extension through to 2045, locking in a source of baseload power that was never guaranteed to survive this long.

None of this means the underlying fleet has been fixed in any permanent sense. Eskom's own language has stayed carefully hedged even while celebrating the milestone, framing the challenge now as sustaining gains rather than having solved the generation problem outright, and warning that the risk of a relapse into load shedding remains real if maintenance discipline slips or an unplanned breakdown at one of the ageing coal stations coincides with high winter demand. Load reduction — the targeted, localised outages applied to high-risk municipal networks rather than the nationwide schedule load shedding implies — remains in place in parts of the country, with Eskom targeting its elimination only by March 2027.

The distinction between a stable national grid and a fully reliable municipal distribution network is also widening in ways that matter for ordinary households and businesses. A recent outage affecting parts of Johannesburg, for instance, traced back to a City Power substation fire rather than anything happening at Eskom's generation level — a reminder that the country's electricity problems have increasingly migrated downstream, from power stations that are slowly stabilising to municipal networks that are not keeping pace with the same discipline.

For the businesses that spent the better part of a decade budgeting around blackouts, 540 days is long enough to start trusting the grid again, but not yet long enough to tear up the contingency plan entirely. What Eskom is effectively asking the country to do is believe that this time the improvement is structural rather than seasonal — a case that gets stronger with every additional day added to the count, and considerably weaker the moment a single serious breakdown puts the number back to zero.
