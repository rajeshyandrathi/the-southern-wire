South Africa's Reserve Bank faces its tightest call in a year, and petrol relief is working against it

Business Desk

For most of the past two years, South Africa's interest rate debate has run in one direction: how soon, and by how much, the Reserve Bank would start cutting. That conversation has stopped. With the Monetary Policy Committee's next decision due on 19 November and the repo rate already sitting at 7.25%, economists are no longer simply arguing about the pace of relief. Some are now pricing in a hike.

The reason is inflation that has refused to behave. Consumer prices rose 4.5% in the most recent reading, comfortably above the Reserve Bank's 3% target and the upper end of its one-percentage-point tolerance band, and have stayed above that target consistently since March. A central bank mandated to keep inflation anchored near 3% does not have much room to look past eight straight months of overshoot, whatever the growth argument for holding or cutting rates might otherwise suggest.

That has split the forecasting community in a way that has not been this even in years. One camp, pointing to the persistence of the overshoot, expects the committee to deliver a 25 basis point increase at this month's meeting rather than risk inflation expectations becoming unanchored. Economists at Old Mutual take the opposing view, arguing the current spike is largely temporary and that the policy rate will eventually settle somewhere between 5.5% and 6% once the transient pressures clear — a forecast that implies cuts are still coming, just not yet.

Working against the hawks' case, at least partially, is the fuel relief the Treasury has already pushed through. Temporary cuts to the fuel levy that took effect this month are expected to ease pump prices and, with them, some of the transport and logistics costs that feed directly into the inflation basket. Whether that relief shows up in time to change the Reserve Bank's calculation this month, or only shapes the committee's view of where inflation is headed into 2027, is itself part of what makes the November decision harder to call than usual.

For households and businesses that have spent two years waiting for borrowing costs to ease, the practical stakes are straightforward even if the economics are not. A hike would land directly on mortgage repayments and business financing costs already strained by a weak growth environment; a hold buys time without offering relief; and a cut, the outcome most of the public still associates with where this cycle was supposed to be heading, now looks like the least likely of the three.
